# Worked example — the handbook applied to a Phoxta founder

Two of the Phoxta personas from `knowledge-base/02-market-and-customers.md`, run through the
book's frameworks. All figures are illustrative, not book data. They show the *shape* of a good
answer: name the framework, apply the founder's numbers, keep the confidence/test discipline, and
end with the gate verdict and the next tool.

## Example 1 — Maya, audience founder launching a DTC brand

Maya is 29, a marketing manager in London with a large Instagram following. She wants to launch a
small-batch skincare brand and has bought a Phoxta storefront blueprint.

### Stage 0 — Founder fit (chapter 1)
- Trait clusters: ideas and drive high (she already sees the problem: her audience asks her for
  fragrance-free products); people skills high (audience, persuasion); work style untested
  (never been her own boss); financial savvy low (has not read a balance sheet); background low
  (no founders in the family, never worked in a startup).
- Must-haves: plan (not yet), execution (partly), motivation (high).
- Verdict: proceed, with mitigations: appendix A and B before the money stages; an accountant or
  finance-minded co-founder; the advisor uses more hand-holding.

### Stage 1 — Opportunity (chapter 2)
Ten questions, each with confidence and a test (Table 2-1 discipline):

| Question | Maya's answer | Confidence | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem | Fragrance-free skincare that still feels premium | 4 | 30 DM conversations in two weeks |
| Market size | UK premium skincare buyers with sensitive skin | 2 | Desk research + poll of her audience |
| Awareness | Aware need (people complain publicly) | 4 | Count complaint posts |
| Growth | "Sensitive skin" search trend rising | 3 | Trend data |
| Benefit | No reactions, premium feel, her curation | 3 | Sample kits to 50 followers |
| Share | 1% of her followers buy in year one | 1 | Pre-order landing page |
| Competitors | Two established fragrance-free lines, both clinical-looking | 4 | Price and review scan |
| Customers | Women 25-40, urban, already buying premium | 3 | Audience analytics |
| Reach | Instagram, email list, storefront | 5 | Already owned |
| Substitutes | Clinical brands (cheaper, less pleasant) | 4 | Reviews |

Two lowest-confidence rows (share, size) become the first experiments: a pre-order page and a
sample-kit test. Canvas hypotheses go on the board as "untested" until those return.

Economics: price setter within a niche; fixed costs low (no factory, contract manufacturer);
variable costs high → low breakeven, modest profitability after it (the book's low-fixed model).

Breakeven (appendix B): price £45, variable cost £18 (product, packaging, shipping, payment
fees) → unit contribution £27, CM ratio 60%. Fixed costs £2,500 a month (platform, content,
part-time help) → breakeven 93 units a month, £4,180 revenue. Target £2,000 a month pre-tax
profit → 167 units. With 1% of a 40,000 audience buying once, that is 400 units in year one,
about 33 a month: below breakeven. The verdict is "learn more", not "go": the share assumption
must be tested before ordering stock.

Five characteristics: value (likely), profit (unproven), fit (strong on marketing, weak on
finance and operations), durability (moderate, low barriers to entry; her audience is the
barrier), financeable (bootstrap-shaped, not VC-shaped). Compared with doing nothing (UK gilts as
the risk-free line), it clears the line only if the pre-order test works.

### Stage 2 — Model and strategy (chapter 3)
- Five questions: value = curated, fragrance-free, premium feel; profit = 60% contribution on
  direct sales; differentiate = her taste and trust with the audience; defend = community and
  content, not the formula; discovered = she already owns discovery, which is her real advantage.
- Analogy from Table 3-1: subscription (a refill plan) plus community.
- Narrative test passes; numbers test depends on the share experiment.
- Scale-readiness: hold. Hoffman thresholds are far away and the book says do not scale early.

### Stage 3 — Legal form (chapter 4)
The book's rules say early losses and owner-drawn cash → flow-through form; sale or investment
later → corporation. Non-US mapping: start as a UK sole trader for the pre-order test, incorporate
a private limited company (Ltd) once stock is ordered and liability becomes real. Check local law
and registration with an accountant.

### Stage 4 — Plan and pitch (chapter 5)
Maya does not need a 40-page plan yet. The book's format table points to: a 100-word pitch for
her audience and suppliers, a reading deck for a possible small loan or family money, and the
executive summary as the one document that is always current. The team section is honest: one
founder, an accountant on a day rate, a contract manufacturer; "how the team must grow" names
operations first.

### Stage 5 — Startup capital (chapter 6)
Business type: Main Street (the ~70% majority). Opening balance sheet, Table 6-1 style:

| Item | £ |
|---|---|
| First stock run (500 units at £14) | 7,000 |
| Packaging and design | 1,500 |
| Three months' fixed costs | 7,500 |
| Cash cushion | 2,000 |
| **Launch capital** | **18,000** |

Source stack, cheapest first (Table 6-3 discipline): savings £10,000, pre-orders £4,000
(also the market test), a family loan £4,000 with written terms. No angel or VC: the book says
those follow a track record, and this is a lifestyle-scale business.

### Stage 7 onward
Growth money, if any, is a bank line against stock once she has three statements and the lender
ratios (chapter 7). The advisor says so and stops; chapters 8 to 13 are not loaded.

## Example 2 — Tom, acquisition entrepreneur valuing a blueprint

Tom is 38, ex-private equity, with £250,000 and an investor network. He is looking at a Phoxta
blueprint listing with verified unit economics and wants to know what it is worth.

- Appendix C, earnings normaliser first: reported net income £60,000; add back the seller's
  below-market salary gap (£15,000 lower than a replacement manager costs) and a one-off refund
  spike (£5,000 lost) → normalised net income £50,000; EBIT £62,000; EBITDA £66,000.
- Multiples: the book gives no industry tables, only worked P/E examples of 8x and 10x, so Tom
  supplies comparables. At a 4x EBITDA comparable: enterprise value £264,000. No interest-bearing
  debt, so equity value equals enterprise value.
- Cross-checks: adjusted book value (inventory and domain at market) £40,000 as a floor; DCF at
  Tom's own cost of capital as the buyer's view.
- Chapter 13 deal terms: prefer an asset purchase, pay cash, agree the seller's post-deal role.
- Chapter 8 framing if he syndicates: he is the angel here; the founder should expect the three
  investor questions (like, trust, do business) and a term sheet with preferred-stock features.
- Verdict: a range of £220,000 to £300,000, with "the seller's number is not a valuation until
  you know how it was derived" (the chapter 8 box).

## How the advisor should sound
Short, framework first, founder's numbers second, gate verdict third, next tool last. Cite the
chapter. Flag US-only content and 2018 statistics. Never present a single valuation number.
