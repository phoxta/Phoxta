# Ch 11 — Leadership for a Growing Business
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 11, book pages 181–194.

## Purpose
How the founder's role and way of working must change as the company grows: which leadership
mode fits which size, how to tell whether you can and will adapt, where to get objective feedback
(funders, advisory board, executive coach), and when to bring in professional management or step
aside. Load when a user says "I'm the bottleneck", "should I hire a CEO", "how do I delegate",
"advisory board", or when a journey enters scaling after chapter 10.

## Core ideas
- Organizational issues often eclipse sales as the biggest growth challenge; the founding team must
  periodically reinvent the organization to cope with changing circumstances.
- Bhidé: sustainability requires broadening the capabilities of the **firm**, not the entrepreneur —
  more qualified people, more specialised functions, decentralised decisions, systems for a larger
  and more complex organization, and employees oriented to a common long-term purpose.
- Founders must reinvent themselves: from **doing things yourself to doing things through other
  people**. Many find this hard, fail to change, and become liabilities to the companies they founded.
- Growth strains each founding contribution — vision, technical skills, management skills, and
  personal energy and time.
- Roberts' four leadership approaches — **content, behaviors, results, context** — map to enterprise
  size and complexity. There is no best way to manage a startup, but there is a best mode for a
  particular company at a particular point, and the transitions between modes need extra attention.
- Modes can be mixed: hands-on with a newly appointed manager while running the overall operation
  on results.
- Some founders adapt through years of growth (Page & Brin, Gates, Kelleher, Cook, Branson). Others
  cannot change habits that served them in a small setting, or actively resist changes that dilute
  their control.
- If you are willing but struggling to adapt, recruit people who will criticise your leadership
  objectively: the management team, the board, funders, an advisory board, an executive coach.
- If you cannot or will not adapt, change role or step aside — there is no shame in either. Bring in
  professional management, with the founder staying on as chair.
- Professional managers know how to build the systems and processes every growing firm eventually
  needs (Flamholtz & Randle). Many firms recognise the need too late to avoid a crisis, and some
  founders only give "the illusion of turning the organization over".

## Frameworks & tables
**Four founding contributions strained by growth** (p. 182)

| Contribution | Strain |
|---|---|
| Common vision | Must be instilled in newly hired employees |
| Technical skills | Become relatively less important as operational and management skills matter more |
| Management skills | May not be up to the challenge of a larger organization |
| Personal energy and time | Finite, while the need to direct and control keeps growing |

**Roberts' four leadership approaches** (p. 182–185)
- **Managing content** — do it yourself or directly supervise those who do (hiring, product design,
  moving goods to the stockroom). Maximum control, which appeals to founders motivated by control;
  fails when the founder's time and energy can't keep pace and new challenges need skills or
  experience they lack. Not recognising when this mode is no longer appropriate can kill the business.
- **Managing behaviors** — specify how people should act; codify the behaviors that lead to success
  in policies, rules, procedures, job design and behavior-auditing systems (e.g. train lab staff to
  run specific tests, then audit compliance). Best when employees are inexperienced or need clear
  direction. Frees the founder's time while keeping control.
- **Managing results** — define what the outcome must look like and hand over responsibility for
  how (military vehicle: 25 mpg on paved roads, rough terrain, protects driver plus five passengers
  from small-arms fire). The leader supplies resources, training and motivation. Needed where
  problems have no clear guidelines (science, engineering) and talented people must find the answer.
- **Managing context** — also results-focused, but achieved by shaping culture, values and
  structure so highly competent people are attracted, retained and do their best work; select and
  develop people, communicate broadly, spend little or no time saying what or how.

**The sales-team illustration across modes** (p. 183–185)

| Mode | Instruction to salespeople |
|---|---|
| Behaviors | Talk with 20 prospects a day → about one new account a day, five a week; a solid commission base after six months. Manager monitors the 20-contact rule and helps where needed |
| Results | Produce at least EUR 150,000 in commission revenue this year — by whatever method works |
| Context | Decide both how to win accounts and what the goals should be (e.g. win back lapsed accounts, measure account profitability to choose leads) |

**Table 11-1 — Four leadership approaches** (Roberts, p. 186)

| | Content | Behavior | Results | Context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | Young, small, simple enterprise | Somewhat larger, more involved enterprise | Large, complex organization | Very large, very complex, mature organization |
| Driving assumptions | Insufficient knowledge and experience to plan; subordinates not capable of independent action or decisions | Too little time to do everything; subordinates can act independently but per managerial prescription | Too little time; subordinates achieve better outcomes with their own means | Too little time and knowledge; the right people in the right environment with the right mission will succeed |
| Behavior | On the front lines; barking orders; pitching in to help out | Developing process and procedure; observing | Attending meetings and reviews; studying plans, papers, reports; writing memos | Lots of time on key hires and promotions; tone-setting events |
| Key skills, tools | Action; decisions | Policies; procedures; behavior audit | Plans; budgets; organizing structure and systems | Communication; leadership by example |

**Three damaging consequences of a founder who won't adapt** (p. 187)
1. Employee initiative is smothered by the founder controlling all activities and decisions; the
   best employees leave in frustration.
2. Opportunities are missed because the organization can only run at the overworked founder's pace.
3. The enterprise's scope is limited to the founder's knowledge and vision.

**Sources of objective feedback and help** (p. 187–189)

| Source | What it offers | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Management team and board | Tell you if your grip is too tight or too loose, where you need help, and when it's time to go | Must not be afraid to say so |
| Funders (VCs) | Experienced entrepreneurial professionals; Andreessen: CEO skills can be taught (innovator skills are more innate), so his firm trains CEOs | Mulcahy: VCs differ widely in how much they actually coach — verify with portfolio CEOs |
| Advisory board | Sounding board for ideas, plus skills, mentorship and a broader network | Hard to choose members before you know which expertise you lack |
| Executive coach | One-on-one, customised behavior change to improve on-the-job performance | Expensive, but may be worth it |

**Two executive-coaching approaches** (p. 188–189)

| Approach | Character |
|---|---|
| Diagnosis and development | Traditional, rooted in psychology; deeper method; takes longer to deliver |
| Prescriptive | Like the everyday coaching managers give subordinates; faster and more direct |

**Box — How to build a board** (adapted from MacPherson, p. 190–191)
1. **Look outside your existing network.** Not friends and fans: diverse experience, expertise,
   viewpoints and skills; people who have built successful businesses; a constructively critical
   audience; access to customers, suppliers, strategic partners, financiers, publicists and
   professional-service vendors.
2. **Recruit a well-known community member or industry influencer first.** Like film producers
   lining up bankable talent — a star's commitment attracts others and gives credibility from day one.
3. **Invest time in the relationships.** Most members are unpaid; their reward is the satisfaction
   of helping, so show appreciation. If one insists on pay, judge how uniquely valuable they are and
   consider remuneration where a long-term business relationship is possible.
4. **Set goals and expectations up front.** In-person meetings every three to six months usually
   suffice; reserve the right to consult individuals ad hoc; always have an agenda with specific
   goals; assess the board yearly; if affordable, host an off-site at your expense to review progress.
5. **Have a framework for changing members.** Your needs shift by growth phase; most members can't
   serve more than two or three years; some won't be as helpful as hoped — say up front that they
   serve as needed and spell out term limits.
6. **Be clear on the role.** An advisory board is not a board of directors: no defined duties, no
   fiduciary responsibility, no legal or financial responsibility for your decisions. It is a group of
   volunteers with knowledge you lack who assist, challenge, guide and open your eyes.

**Box — Do you need professional management?** (p. 192) — if this scenario sounds familiar,
consider professional management:
- Every decision must be made at the top.
- Policies for handling routine functions are almost nonexistent.
- The firm's human resources are not being developed.
- You make decisions, but no one follows through with action.
- Accounting functions are haphazard and amateurish.
- You're having trouble recruiting competent people.
- People are spending a lot of their time putting out fires.

**The founder's three options** (p. 193): change yourself (often difficult); change your role by
bringing in professional management; or cash in your equity and move on to new challenges.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- Young, small, simple → content mode; larger organizations call for behaviors, then results, then
  context in turn. Match the mode to size and complexity, not to personal preference.
- Inexperienced staff who need clear direction → manage behaviors. Complex problems with no clear
  guidelines (science, engineering, creative work) → manage results or context.
- Behavior mode assumes the prescribed behavior produces the result and is the only way to it; when
  either assumption fails, move to results.
- Rigid, by-the-book rules work for McDonald's and would be disastrous for IDEO — the mode must fit
  the work, not just the size.
- You may run more than one mode at once (hands-on with a new manager; results for the whole).
- Transitions between modes are where things break; give them extra attention.
- Behavior-mode sales heuristic: 20 prospect conversations a day ≈ 1 new account a day ≈ 5 a week;
  six months to a solid commission base.
- "If you want advice for your startup, ask for money. If you want money, ask for advice."
  (Baehr & Loomis) — you will need both.
- Before taking VC money, get the names of the firm's other portfolio CEOs and ask them: is the
  partner accessible, do they add to boardroom discussion, have they given constructive help?
- Advisory board: meet in person every 3–6 months, agenda with specific goals, yearly assessment,
  term limits of 2–3 years, recruit the bankable influencer first, no friends-and-fans.
- An advisory board carries no fiduciary duty; a board of directors does.
- If any of the seven professional-management signals sound familiar → act before the crisis.
- If you cannot or will not adapt → change role (founder as chair, professional CEO) or step aside.
- A handover must be real: hiring managers and then controlling everything is the illusion of
  letting go.

## Process / steps
1. Diagnose the mode you use today (Table 11-1) and whether it fits your current size and growth.
2. Identify the mode the next stage needs and the tools it requires (policies and audits; plans,
   budgets and structure; communication, key hires and tone-setting).
3. Reflect: is the business at a transition point? Can you adapt? Are you willing to?
4. If willing but struggling: recruit objective critics — management team, board, funders, an
   advisory board (built per the box), or an executive coach (choose diagnosis-and-development or
   prescriptive).
5. Run the "Do you need professional management?" checklist.
6. If you cannot or will not adapt, or the firm needs systems you can't build: bring in
   professional management with the founder as chair (or cash out) — and genuinely hand over.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
Leadership-mode self-check (p. 185):
- Which management method am I using today?
- Is it appropriate for my current state of development and growth?
- As volume and scope grow, how much less time do I have for hands-on involvement?

Adaptation check (p. 187):
- Is my business at a transition point where my style of leading and managing must change?
- Can I adapt?
- Am I willing to adapt?
- Who will tell me objectively if my grip is too tight (or too loose), where I need help, and when
  it's time for me to go?

VC-mentoring due diligence (p. 188) — ask the CEOs of the VC's other portfolio companies:
- How effective is the firm's mentoring?
- Is the VC partner accessible?
- How much do they add to boardroom discussion?
- Have you received constructive help in dealing with company problems?

Advisory-board setup (p. 190–191):
- Who outside my inner circle has built a successful business and would be constructively critical?
- Who can open doors to customers, suppliers, partners, financiers, publicists, service vendors?
- Who is the bankable first member?
- Meeting cadence and place, agenda, yearly assessment, term limits — written down?
- Have I stated clearly what the board is and is not?

Professional-management scenario (p. 192) — tick each that applies:
- Every decision must be made at the top.
- Policies for routine functions are almost nonexistent.
- Human resources are not being developed.
- I make decisions, but no one follows through with action.
- Accounting is haphazard and amateurish.
- We have trouble recruiting competent people.
- People spend a lot of their time putting out fires.

Final reflection (p. 193):
- Is a lack of systems and processes holding the firm back?
- Am I personally up to the challenge of building the business, or would the company be better off
  if I stepped aside in favour of experienced managers?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Page & Brin (Google), Gates (Microsoft), Kelleher (Southwest), Cook (Intuit), Branson
  (Virgin)** — each adapted his management mode as the business grew — adaptation is possible.
- **McDonald's vs IDEO** — McDonald's thrives on behavior-mode procedures and job design and would
  never tell crews to "figure it out"; the same rules would wreck a creative design firm — fit the
  mode to the work.
- **Pierre Omidyar / eBay** — kept the chairman's job, handed management to Meg Whitman as CEO;
  had brought in a VC partly to recruit management expertise — change role without shame.
- **Esther / "ChickenFix"** (fictional) — molecular biologist; university took a 25 percent share;
  seed money from university and a VC; content as CEO while lab-based, out of her element once
  testing, a product manager, VCs and MBAs arrived; "everything seems anticlimactic" — heart not in
  the executive role → professional CEO, founder as chair.
- **Flamholtz & Randle's founder** — hired two experienced managers, made a big deal of turning
  over the reins, then kept controlling everything — the illusion of letting go.
- **Andreessen vs Mulcahy** — CEO skills can be taught and some VCs train them, but coaching
  quality varies widely — verify with portfolio CEOs before you sign.

## Pitfalls
- Staying in content mode past the size where it works; the business fails when the founder can't
  see it.
- Assuming prescribed behaviors guarantee results, or are the only route to them.
- Resisting change to protect control — smothering initiative and losing the best people.
- Filling the advisory board with friends and fans; letting it drift without goals, agenda,
  assessment or term limits.
- Confusing an advisory board with a legal board of directors.
- Taking VC money assuming mentoring comes with it, without checking.
- Recognising the need for professional management only after a crisis.
- Pretending to hand over to professional managers while still controlling everything.

## Key terms
`managing content` — leading by doing the work yourself or directly supervising those who do.
`managing behaviors` — leading by prescribing and auditing how people act (policies, rules, procedures, job design).
`managing results` — leading by defining outcomes and giving people responsibility for the means.
`managing context` — leading by shaping culture, values, structure and hiring so the right people succeed on their own.
`advisory board` — unpaid volunteers with knowledge you lack; no fiduciary, legal or financial responsibility (unlike a board of directors).
`executive coach` — one-on-one behavior-change professional; diagnosis-and-development (deep, slow) or prescriptive (fast, direct).
`professional management` — experienced managers who build the systems and processes a growing firm needs.

## Summing up (book)
- Growth challenges the founding team, who may lack the skills, experience or temperament to lead
  a larger, more complex organization.
- Roberts' four modes — real-time management of content, behavior, results, context — must be
  matched to circumstances, and the team must know when to change from one to another.
- A few entrepreneurs adapt with their companies' growth; others must change themselves (often
  difficult), change roles by bringing in professional management, or cash in their equity and
  move on to new challenges.
