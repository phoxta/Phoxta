# Ch 6 — Startup-Stage Financing
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 6, book pages 103–114.

## Purpose
Where does launch money come from, and how much do you actually need? Maps the business
life cycle (startup → growth → maturity), sizes a startup's asset needs into an opening balance
sheet, and surveys the bootstrap and seed sources. Load when a user asks "how do I fund my
startup", "should I crowdfund / join an accelerator", or is building a launch budget.

## Core ideas
- Financing is needed at every size and stage — corner bookstore to Amazon; even a
  billion-dollar mature company keeps financing to stay on the cutting edge.
- US small businesses fall into three types (Mills & McCarthy, HBS/Fundera): **Main Street
  (~70%)**, **supply-chain (~17%)**, **high-growth (~3%)**. Life cycle and financing needs
  differ by type.
- Every business passes through **startup, growth and maturity**; what varies is the scale of
  growth and the length of the growth period. Rapid growers need successive rounds at
  ever-higher levels.
- Few companies make it through all three phases: many fail within a few years, others are
  acquired before reaching full potential.
- Startup money is mostly **personal (bootstrap)**, then **seed** from friends and family, small
  banks and online lenders, supplier trade credit, crowdfunding and accelerators.
- A team with a strong reputation or prior exits can attract angels, PE or even VC before it has a
  marketable product. Financing has decentralised: VC was an exclusive club; angels, accelerators
  and crowdfunding now widen the menu.
- Equity has a price: you sell ownership, control and future profit. Worth it only if you must
  raise quickly.
- Early-stage equity is still rare. Kauffman: **40% of initial startup capital, even for fast
  growers, is bank debt**, with an almost equal amount of owner equity.
- Most businesses never grow large enough for accelerators or equity funding; a $10–20M revenue
  ceiling is a fine business, just not a venture-scale one.

## Frameworks & tables
**Three types of US small business** (Mills & McCarthy, p. 104)

| Type | Share of small-employer firms | Profile | Life cycle and financing need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main Street | ~70% | Dry cleaners, mechanics, clinics; serve the owner's personal/family income; sustain rather than grow once established | Startup → gradual growth → no/slow-growth maturity. Startup money buys or leases equipment, rents premises, stocks inventory and fixtures, funds working capital — or buys an existing business (see *HBR Guide to Buying a Small Business*) |
| Supply-chain | ~17% | Niche firms serving a focused need in an industry, region or supply chain; ~37% of US employment and 80% of 2013 US patents | Keep pursuing growth after startup |
| High-growth | ~3% | Mostly high-tech (Apple, Facebook, Amazon, Airbnb, Uber); disproportionate job creation | Successive financing phases at ever-higher levels |

**The business life cycle**: startup → growth → maturity. This chapter covers startup; chapter 7
covers growth and maturity; chapter 8 covers angels/VC; chapter 9 covers IPOs.

**Startup-phase sources** (p. 105)
- *Bootstrap* (your own resources, no big money): personal savings; a second mortgage on the
  home; credit-card lines of credit.
- *Seed* (early outside money): loans from friends and relatives; bank debt from small banks and
  online lenders (especially important for Main Street firms); 30–60 day trade credit from
  suppliers; crowdfunding; equity from an accelerator.
- *Reputation-driven*: angels, private equity, VC, hedge funds — realistic only when the founders
  have a demonstrated "Midas touch".

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Size the raise from the balance sheet**: cash runway for the months costs exceed revenue
  (McDuff: 3 months) + opening inventory + prepaid insurance/rent + fixed assets.
- **Trade credit first.** 30–60 day supplier terms finance inventory at zero interest if you sell
  through before the bill is due — better than any bank line.
- **Credit cards and home-equity lines are legitimate but expensive** — use them last.
- **Buy used fixed assets** from a former employer or industry contact (McDuff got a truck, two
  lathes, tools and benches for $10,000).
- **Bank debt has been tight since 2008** despite legislative fixes → crowdfunding, angel
  syndication and online lending matter more for early needs.
- **Crowdfund if the idea has broad consumer reach or deep reach into a narrow market.** Rewards
  are early sales, and the campaign validates interest and tests messaging and pricing.
  US crowdfunding exceeded $2B in 2015.
- **Crowdfunding levels the field for women**: under 8% of VC-backed companies have female
  cofounders, but women are 13% more likely than men to succeed on Kickstarter (Mollick).
- **Equity crowdfunding is legal but suspect.** The JOBS Act (SEC rules, March 2015) lets nearly
  anyone invest without accredited status, but Isenberg warns that equity is too complex to
  extrapolate from rewards successes and the due diligence makes it too high-cost to work.
- **Accelerators only for high-growth candidates, and only top programs.** Hathaway: graduates of
  top programs raised VC, gained traction and exited faster; other programs showed no strong
  effect — and every accelerator takes equity.
- **Reputation buys early equity.** Financiers actively pursue founders with past successes; a
  first-timer should plan on debt + own equity instead.
- **Don't leave the job early.** McDuff went full-time only once the $32,000 was in hand.

## Process / steps
The McDuff launch sequence (the chapter's implied startup-financing procedure):
1. Use the final year of employment to design the product line, cost it with real production
   numbers, and learn the distribution channels.
2. Compute current assets: cash runway, inventory by category, prepaid insurance and rent.
3. Compute fixed assets: tools, vehicle, fixtures — sourced used where possible.
4. Add them up to the total launch capital ($32,000).
5. Stack sources cheapest first: personal savings ($25,000) → zero-interest family loan ($5,000,
   repaid $1,000/year) → rewards crowdfunding ($2,000 plus product feedback).
6. Write the opening balance sheet (assets = liabilities + owners' equity).
7. Leave the job and go full-time.
8. Once the bootstrapped company shows signs of success (the *seed stage*), add external
   sources: trade credit, small-bank/online loans, crowdfunding, accelerators.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
Startup capital worksheet (Tables 6-1 and 6-2 as a form):
- Cash to cover months when costs exceed revenue: ___ months × $___ per month = $___
- Opening inventory, by category: ___
- Prepaid expenses: insurance (1 year) ___; rent (first ___ months) ___; other ___
- Fixed assets: equipment ___; vehicle ___; tools ___; fixtures ___ (new or used?)
- Total assets required = $___

Source stack worksheet (Table 6-3 as a form):
- Personal savings available: $___
- Second mortgage / home-equity line: $___ (rate ___)
- Credit-card lines: $___ (rate ___ — expensive)
- Friends and family loans: $___, interest ___, repayment ___ per year
- Rewards crowdfunding target: $___
- Supplier trade credit: ___ days on $___ of inventory
- Small bank / online lender: $___
- Accelerator equity: $___ for ___% ownership
- Check: total sources = total assets required.

Crowdfunding fit check:
- Does the product have broad consumer reach, or deep reach into a narrow market?
- Can you offer rewards that are effectively pre-sales of the product?
- Will you use the campaign to test messaging and pricing and to collect product feedback?
- Rewards or equity? If equity, can you carry the due-diligence cost and the complexity?

Accelerator / support-institution fit check (Table 6-4):
- Are you a high-growth business (the 3%) or a Main Street / supply-chain firm?
- Does this program have a record of VC raises, customer traction and acquisitions?
- Can you relocate on-site for 3–6 months and work in a cohort?
- Are you willing to give away equity for the funding and mentorship?
- How long do you need support (months vs years)? Do you need space, seminars, intense
  mentorship, or just capital?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Amalgamated Hat Rack (Angus McDuff)** — launched on $32,000: $25,000 savings, $5,000 uncle's
  zero-interest loan, $2,000 Indiegogo — mostly self- and family-financed, like most small
  businesses; may top out at $10–20M revenue and never suit an accelerator or VC.
- **Apple** — Jobs and Wozniak's garage company, the archetypal 3% high-growth firm, now the
  world's largest by market cap — same three phases, wildly different scale.
- **Oculus Rift** (Mollick) — VCs shunned VR after the 1990s hype; a 2012 Kickstarter raised
  nearly $2.5M; Facebook bought the company for $2B under two years later — crowdfunding can
  bypass traditional funding entirely.
- **Airbnb, Dropbox** — accelerator graduates — the path works for the right kind of company.
- **Shoe-store owner** — $3,000 of shoes on 60-day terms, sold before payment is due — the
  supplier finances inventory for free.

## Pitfalls
- Assuming early equity investment is normal; debt and owner equity dominate startup capital.
- Paying credit-card interest for launch capital when cheaper sources exist.
- Treating rewards-crowdfunding success as evidence that equity crowdfunding will work.
- Giving equity to a mediocre accelerator for little performance uplift.
- Chasing VC or accelerators when you are a Main Street business.
- Quitting the day job before the launch capital and plan are in place.

## Key terms
`bootstrap financing` — launch capital drawn entirely from the founder's own resources (savings, home equity, credit cards).
`seed stage / seed investment` — the first outside money, sought once the bootstrapped business shows signs of success.
`Main Street firm` — a locally serving small business run mainly to sustain the owner's income.
`supply-chain firm` — a niche business serving a focused need in an industry, region or larger firm's supply chain.
`high-growth firm` — the ~3% of small firms, mostly high-tech, that grow very rapidly and need successive rounds.
`trade credit` — 30–60 day supplier terms that finance inventory without interest.
`rewards crowdfunding` — backers give money for rewards (often early product); also validates demand.
`equity crowdfunding` — backers buy actual ownership; open to non-accredited investors under the JOBS Act.
`accelerator` — fixed-term, cohort-based, competitive program giving funding, education and mentorship for equity, ending in a demo day.
`incubator` — longer-term (1–5 years), noncompetitive, rent-based on-site support.
`demo day` — the accelerator's closing showcase where participants pitch to investors.

## Summing up (book)
- Startup financing is bootstrapped from personal savings, credit cards and other personal
  sources, then friends and family, and sometimes small bank loans.
- Supplier trade credit is another low-cost source.
- Other early sources: online banks, accelerators and crowdfunding.
