# Appendix C — Valuation: What Is Your Business Really Worth?
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, appendix C, book pages 245–256.

## Purpose
What a business is worth and how appraisers reach a number: four asset-based methods, three
earnings-multiple methods and discounted cash flow, each with its bias and its inputs. Load when a
user is buying a business, selling their own, pricing shares for an ESOP or a round, or asks
"what is my company worth / how do multiples work". Every worked example reuses the Amalgamated
Hat Rack statements from appendix A (tables A-1 and A-2).

## Core ideas
- Every entrepreneur eventually values a business — buying one or selling their own. Overpay and
  the return disappoints; undervalue what you sell and you shortchange yourself without knowing it.
- Valuation is neither easy nor exact and is normally expert territory; the founder's job is to know
  each method's strengths and weaknesses well enough to negotiate with the experts.
- Two structural reasons value is never certain: (1) different methods consistently give different
  answers even when meticulously calculated; (2) every method is only as good as its inputs, which
  are often incomplete, unreliable or projections.
- A business is worth different amounts to different parties (the Hemingway first edition that
  completes a collector's set). Acquirers rationally pay above the market value of existing shares
  when the target completes something they already own — e.g. a small high-tech firm whose
  technology leverages the acquirer's other operations.
- Public markets (NASDAQ, NYSE) make value transparent: share price × shares outstanding
  approximates value at a point in time. Valuation problems arise where no public price exists —
  closely held businesses (a few owners) and operating units of public companies.
- Three families: **asset-based** (what it owns), **earnings-based** (what it earned, capitalised by a
  comparable multiple) and **DCF** (what it will earn, discounted to today).
- Equity value ≠ enterprise value. Enterprise value = equity + debt — a house sells for the
  mortgage plus the owner's equity, not the equity alone.
- Because the methods disagree, most appraisers run more than one and triangulate.

## Frameworks & tables

**The valuation map — three approaches, eight methods**

| Family | Method | Formula / approach | Key inputs | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset-based | Equity book value | Total assets − Total liabilities | Balance sheet | Quick floor; owner's "at least what we invested" |
| Asset-based | Adjusted book value | Book value with assets and intangibles restated to appraised market values | Balance sheet + appraisals | Asset-heavy firms, LBOs, stale property/inventory values |
| Asset-based | Liquidation value | Net cash from a quick sale of assets − all liabilities settled | Balance sheet + fire-sale haircuts | Distress, wind-down, lender's floor |
| Asset-based | Replacement value | Cost to reproduce the business's assets | Current cost to rebuild each asset | Build-vs-buy sanity check; usually a ceiling for a buyer |
| Earnings-based | P/E (earnings) multiple | Equity value = Net income × comparable P/E; EV = Equity + interest-bearing debt | Normalised net income; comparables' P/E | Comparable public firms or recent private deals exist |
| Earnings-based | EBIT multiple | Value = EBIT × comparable EBIT multiple | Normalised EBIT | Comparables differ in leverage or tax position |
| Earnings-based | EBITDA multiple | Value = (EBIT + D&A) × comparable EBITDA multiple | Normalised EBITDA | Comparables also differ in depreciation/amortisation |
| Cash-flow-based | Discounted cash flow | PV of forecast cash flows + PV of terminal value at the buyer's cost of capital | Multi-year forecast, terminal value, discount rate | Buyer valuing what it could earn under new ownership |

### Asset-based methods (book pp. 247–249)

**1. Equity book value**
- Formula: `Equity book value = Total assets − Total liabilities` (identical to total owners'
  equity on the balance sheet).
- Worked example (Amalgamated Hat Rack, 2017, table A-1): $3,932,500 − $1,750,000 = **$2,182,500**.
- Strength: quick, easy, a common industry shorthand; an owner argues the firm is worth at least
  book because that is what was invested.
- Weakness: assets sit at historical cost. Receivables may be uncollectible; inventory may be
  spoiled or obsolete (or undervalued); land booked in 1995 in the heart of San Francisco may be
  worth 10–20× its book figure.

**2. Adjusted book value**
- Approach: restate each balance-sheet asset to a realistic market level and appraise listed
  intangibles (goodwill, patents) at what a buyer would actually pay.
- Worked example (leveraged buyout of a major retail chain): equity book value $1.3 billion →
  **$2.2 billion** after inventory and property were restated to appraised values — **+69%**.
- Goodwill caution: usually an accounting fiction created when one company buys another at a
  premium to book value; to the next buyer it may be worth nothing.

**3. Liquidation value**
- Approach: the net cash realised if assets were disposed of in a quick sale and every liability
  paid off or settled.
- Bias: inventory and fixed assets fetch less in a hurried sale than a deliberate one → a floor.

**4. Replacement value**
- Approach: estimate the cost of reproducing the business's assets.
- Bias: a buyer may not want to replicate every asset in the package, so replacement value often
  exceeds what the buyer would pay → a rough ceiling.

**Shared verdict on asset-based methods**
- Strengths: easy, inexpensive to calculate, easy to understand.
- Weaknesses: equity book value and liquidation value fail to reflect actual market values; all four
  ignore the intangible value of a going concern — human knowledge, skill and reputation.

### Earnings-based methods (book pp. 249–253)

Principle: **capitalise earnings** — multiply an income-statement earnings figure (net income,
pre-tax earnings, EBIT or EBITDA) by a multiple observed in comparable companies.

**5. Price-earnings (P/E) multiple**
- Public company: `Equity value = Share price × Shares outstanding`; `Enterprise value = Equity
  value + Debt`.
- Read the multiple off a comparable: `Multiple = Share price ÷ Current earnings per share`.
  XYZ Corp: $50 ÷ $5 = **10** ("trading at ten times earnings").
- Apply it: `Share price = Current earnings × Multiple`; for a whole private firm
  `Equity value = Net income × Comparable multiple`.
- Worked example (Amalgamated): comparable public firm Acme Corp trades at P/E **8** (similar in
  industry and size); a private firm in the same industry was recently acquired at ~**8** → the
  multiple is in the ballpark. Net income (table A-2) $347,500 × 8 = **$2,780,000 equity value**.
- Enterprise value: `EV = Equity value + Interest-bearing debt` (short- plus long-term debt).
  Book: $2,780,000 + $1,175,000 = **$3,955,000**. (The prose quotes 2017 interest-bearing debt as
  $1,185,000, which would give $3,965,000 — a book inconsistency; a calculator should read the
  figure straight from the balance sheet.)
- Derived, not in the book: the 8× value sits ~27% above equity book value; implied EV/EBIT ≈ 5.2×
  ($3,955,000 ÷ $757,500).
- Reliability rests on (a) truly comparable firms — no two are alike, so valuation is "part science
  and part art" — and (b) a normalised earnings figure (checklist below).

**6. EBIT multiple**
- Problem it fixes: two firms with identical operating results can report very different net income
  because of **financing** (interest expense) and **taxes**, neither of which says much about future
  wealth-producing capacity. A P/E lifted from a differently financed comparable misprices the target.

**Table C-1 — Hypothetical income statements of Amalgamated Hat Rack and Acme Corporation** (book p. 253)

| Line | Amalgamated | Acme |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) | $757,500 | $757,500 |
| Less: interest expense | $110,000 | $350,000 |
| Earnings before income tax | $647,500 | $407,400* |
| Less: income tax | $300,000 | $187,000 |
| Net income | $347,500 | $220,500 |

*Printed as $407,400; the arithmetic ($757,500 − $350,000, and $220,500 + $187,000) gives
$407,500. Both firms pay roughly the same effective tax rate (~46%).

- Reading: same EBIT, but Acme's heavier debt cuts net income by 37% ($347,500 → $220,500).
  Capitalising EBIT with an EBIT multiple removes the financing and tax distortion.
- Formula: `Value = Normalised EBIT × Comparable EBIT multiple`. EBIT is pre-interest, so the
  result prices the whole enterprise; subtract interest-bearing debt to reach equity (standard
  practice — the book stops at the substitution).

**7. EBITDA multiple**
- `EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation + Amortisation`. D&A are non-cash accounting allocations that differ
  between otherwise similar firms (asset age, depreciation policy); EBITDA removes that distortion too.
- Formula: `Value = Normalised EBITDA × Comparable EBITDA multiple`, then subtract debt for equity.

### Discounted cash flow (book pp. 253–254)

**8. DCF**
- Why: multiples look backward (last year's earnings); a buyer cares what the business will earn in
  the years ahead under new management.
- Approach: present value of the business's projected future cash flows plus the present value of
  its terminal value, discounted at the buyer's cost of capital. Run on cash flows available to
  both debt and equity holders → enterprise value; run on cash flows left to shareholders → equity value.
- Standard form (not printed in the book; needed for a calculator):
  `Value = Σ CF_t ÷ (1 + r)^t  (t = 1..n)  +  TV ÷ (1 + r)^n`
  where r = buyer's cost of capital (WACC for enterprise cash flows, cost of equity for
  shareholder cash flows) and TV = terminal value at year n. The book cites no discount rate,
  growth rate or terminal-value method.
- Strengths (the book's five): recognises the time value of money; future-oriented — estimates what
  the new owner could achieve; accounts for the buyer's cost of capital; needs no comparable
  companies; uses real cash flows instead of accounting values.
- Weakness: assumes future cash flows and the terminal value can be estimated with reasonable
  accuracy — rarely true far into the future.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- If comparable public companies or recent private deals with known multiples exist → use an
  earnings-based method; it is generally superior to asset-based methods.
- If comparables differ from the target in leverage or tax → use EBIT, not P/E. If they also differ
  in depreciation/amortisation or asset age → use EBITDA.
- If you are the buyer and can forecast cash flows under your ownership → run a DCF at your own
  cost of capital; it is the only method that prices what *you* could make of the business.
- If the business is asset-heavy or the balance sheet is stale (old land, under-recorded
  inventory) → adjust book value before trusting any book figure; the retail LBO moved 69% on
  appraisal alone.
- Treat liquidation value as the floor and replacement value as the buyer's ceiling.
- Always normalise earnings before applying a multiple: strip one-off write-offs and windfall
  sales, restate owner/family salaries to market, check maintenance and depreciation are neither
  starved nor padded.
- Always add interest-bearing debt to an equity value to quote enterprise value (and subtract it to
  go the other way). Never compare an EV multiple with an equity multiple.
- Goodwill on the seller's balance sheet is worth nothing to you unless you can say what it buys.
- Expect methods — and two professionals using the same method — to disagree; run at least two and
  negotiate inside the range.
- Strategic value exceeds market value when the buyer completes a "set" (technology, distribution,
  a collection); price that premium consciously on either side of the table.
- For the sale of a closely held company or an ESOP share price, hire a professional appraiser;
  large firms use the M&A departments of accounting, investment-banking or consulting firms.

## Process / steps
The book presents methods rather than a numbered procedure; this is the order it implies:
1. Fix the context — buyer or seller, closely held firm or public unit, strategic or financial
   buyer — and who bears the estimation risk.
2. Pull the latest balance sheet and income statement (appendix A format).
3. Asset-based pass: equity book value; restate assets and intangibles to market (adjusted book);
   estimate liquidation (floor) and replacement (ceiling) values.
4. Normalise earnings: remove non-recurring items, restate owner salaries, review maintenance and
   depreciation; produce normalised net income, EBIT and EBITDA.
5. Find comparables — public peers by industry and size plus recent private acquisitions; record
   their P/E, EV/EBIT and EV/EBITDA multiples.
6. Earnings-based pass: apply the multiples; convert between equity and enterprise value with
   interest-bearing debt.
7. DCF pass (buyer's view): forecast cash flows under new ownership, set a terminal value, discount
   at the buyer's cost of capital.
8. Lay the results side by side, explain the spread, and bring in a professional appraiser for any
   transaction or ESOP.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions

**Earnings-normalisation checklist (before applying any multiple)** — book pp. 251–252
- Remove one-time write-offs (e.g. obsolete inventory) that depress the latest year.
- Remove one-time gains (e.g. sale of a subsidiary) and non-recurring windfall sales that inflate it.
- Are expenses normal — neither extraordinarily high nor low? Inordinately low maintenance inflates
  near-term earnings and stores up deferred-maintenance cost.
- Closely held company: are owner-manager and family salaries unreasonably high or low? Adjust to market.
- Are depreciation rates valid? If not, adjust reported earnings.

**Balance-sheet adjustment checklist (adjusted book value)** — book pp. 247–248
- Accounts receivable: how much is actually collectible?
- Inventory: spoilage, obsolescence — or undervaluation?
- Property, plant and equipment: market value today versus depreciated historical cost, especially land?
- Intangibles (goodwill, patents): what would a buyer really pay for them?

**Method-selection questions**
- Are there publicly traded companies similar in industry and size with known P/E multiples?
- Has a similar private company been acquired recently, and at what multiple?
- Do comparables differ from the target in debt load or tax position? (→ EBIT) In depreciation
  policy or asset age? (→ EBITDA)
- Can future cash flows and a terminal value be forecast with reasonable confidence? (→ DCF)
- Is the buyer strategic (completes a set) or purely financial?

**Data inputs a valuation calculator needs**
- Balance sheet: total assets, total liabilities, short- and long-term interest-bearing debt,
  per-asset market adjustments.
- Income statement: EBIT, interest expense, pre-tax earnings, income tax, net income, depreciation
  and amortisation, list of non-recurring items, owner-salary adjustment.
- Comparables: P/E, EV/EBIT, EV/EBITDA per peer or transaction.
- DCF: cash-flow forecast by year, terminal value, discount rate.

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Amalgamated Hat Rack** — book value $2,182,500 vs 8× earnings $2,780,000 vs enterprise value
  $3,955,000 — one company, three numbers, depending on method and equity-vs-enterprise framing.
- **Acme Corporation (table C-1)** — identical EBIT to Amalgamated, but $350,000 interest instead of
  $110,000 leaves net income $220,500 vs $347,500 — leverage distorts P/E; use EBIT/EBITDA.
- **XYZ Corporation** — $50 share ÷ $5 earnings = 10× — how a multiple is read off a public comparable.
- **Retail-chain LBO** — equity book $1.3B became $2.2B (+69%) once inventory and property were
  appraised — stale book values hide value.
- **1995 San Francisco land** — worth 10–20× its book figure — historical cost is not market value.
- **Hemingway first editions** — the volume that completes a set is worth more to that collector —
  strategic buyers pay more than the market value of the shares.

## Pitfalls
- Trusting a single method or a single professional's number.
- Taking book value as market value (stale land, dead inventory, uncollectible receivables,
  fictional goodwill).
- Applying a P/E multiple from a comparable with a different capital structure or tax position.
- Capitalising un-normalised earnings (one-offs, deferred maintenance, off-market owner salaries,
  invalid depreciation).
- Confusing equity value with enterprise value when quoting or comparing multiples.
- Believing multi-year cash-flow forecasts and terminal values are precise.
- Paying replacement value for assets you would never rebuild.
- Closing a sale or setting an ESOP price without a professional appraiser.

## Key terms
`equity book value` — total assets minus total liabilities; equals owners' equity on the balance sheet.
`adjusted book value` — book value with assets and intangibles restated to appraised market values.
`liquidation value` — net cash from a quick sale of all assets after settling all liabilities.
`replacement value` — the cost of reproducing the business's assets.
`goodwill` — the premium over book value paid in a past acquisition, carried as an intangible; often worthless to a new buyer.
`capitalising earnings` — valuing a business by multiplying an earnings figure by a multiple.
`P/E multiple` — share price ÷ earnings per share of a comparable public company.
`enterprise value` — equity value plus (interest-bearing) debt; what the whole business is worth to all capital providers.
`EBIT` — earnings before interest and taxes. `EBITDA` — EBIT plus depreciation and amortisation (non-cash charges).
`discounted cash flow (DCF)` — present value of forecast cash flows plus terminal value, discounted at the buyer's cost of capital.
`terminal value` — the value of cash flows beyond the explicit forecast horizon, discounted back as a lump sum.
`closely held business` — a company with only a few owners and no public market for its shares.
`ESOP` — employee stock ownership plan; a common trigger for a formal appraisal of private shares.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Multi-method valuation calculator** — balance sheet, income statement, D&A, debt, comparable
  multiples, cash-flow forecast + discount rate → equity and enterprise value under all eight
  methods side by side with a low/high range → shows the founder why "one number" never exists.
- **Earnings normaliser** — reported net income + itemised one-offs + market owner salary +
  maintenance/depreciation adjustments → normalised net income, EBIT, EBITDA → feeds every multiple.
- **Comparable-multiple builder** — per peer: share price + EPS, or EV + EBIT/EBITDA → P/E, EV/EBIT,
  EV/EBITDA table with median → recommends the multiple family from leverage/tax differences
  (table C-1 logic).
- **Equity ⇄ enterprise value switcher** — equity value + short/long-term interest-bearing debt →
  EV (or back) → stops the most common apples-to-oranges error.
- **Balance-sheet reality check** — line-by-line book vs estimated market value for receivables,
  inventory, PP&E/land, intangibles → adjusted book value and % uplift (benchmark: +69% LBO case).
- **DCF sandbox** — 3–5 year cash flows, terminal value, discount-rate slider → PV and sensitivity
  table → makes the fragility of far-out estimates visible.
- **Method-fit advisor** — five yes/no questions (comparables exist? leverage differs? forecastable?
  asset-heavy? strategic buyer?) → recommended primary and cross-check methods.

## Summing up (book)
- **Asset-based** (equity book, adjusted book, liquidation, replacement): easy to calculate and
  understand; except adjusted book and replacement they miss market values, and all miss the
  intangible value of a going concern — knowledge, skill, reputation.
- **Earnings-based** (P/E, EBIT, EBITDA): generally superior to asset-based, but depends on
  comparable businesses whose multiples are known.
- **DCF**: includes the time value of money, is future-looking, estimates what a new owner could
  achieve and recognises the buyer's cost of capital; its weakness is producing reliable cash-flow
  estimates.
- The approaches will produce different outcomes — even the same method in two experienced hands —
  so most appraisers use more than one.
