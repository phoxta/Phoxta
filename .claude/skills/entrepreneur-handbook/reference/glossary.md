# Glossary

Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook (2018), pp. 263-272. Definitions are paraphrased; figures and thresholds are kept as the book states them. Order follows the book (alphabetical).

**accelerator** — Time-limited cohort programme for early-stage businesses that comes bundled with an equity investment.
**accounts payable** — Balance-sheet liability: money the company owes to suppliers and others.
**accounts receivable** — Balance-sheet asset: money owed to the company by customers and others.
**acid-test ratio** — Quick assets (cash, marketable securities, accounts receivable) divided by current liabilities; unlike the current ratio, inventory is excluded.
**adjusted book value** — Refinement of book-value valuation that restates certain balance-sheet assets at realistic market values.
**amortization** — Non-cash expense that writes down the balance-sheet value of an intangible asset over its presumed useful life.
**angel investor** — High-net-worth individual (usually a successful businessperson or professional) who supplies early-stage capital to a startup as debt, ownership capital, or both.
**assets** — Balance-sheet items a company invests in so it can do business: cash and financial instruments, raw-material and finished-goods inventory, land, buildings, equipment, plus money owed to it by customers and others (accounts receivable).
**balance sheet** — Financial statement listing the assets the business owns and how they are financed, by creditors (liabilities), by owners (equity), or both; also called the statement of financial position.
**bond** — Debt security, usually with a fixed interest rate and a stated maturity date; the issuer is contractually bound to pay periodic interest and redeem the bond at face value on maturity.
**bootstrap financing** — Startup financing in which founders rely on their own money plus that of friends, family, employees, and suppliers to launch.
**breakeven analysis** — Works out how much (or how much more) a company must sell to pay for its fixed investment, i.e. the point at which it breaks even on cash flow.
**business model** — Conceptual description of an enterprise's revenue sources, cost drivers, investment size, and success factors, and how they work together.
**business plan** — Document that explains a business opportunity, identifies the market to be served, and details how the venture will pursue it; ideally also covers the management team's unique qualifications, the resources required, and forecast results over a reasonable horizon.
**capital markets** — Financial markets where long-term debt instruments and equity securities, including private placements, are issued and traded.
**cash-flow statement** — Financial statement detailing why cash (and cash equivalents) changed during the accounting period, split into operating, investing, and financing activities.
**C corporation** — In the US, a state-chartered entity treated as a person under the law; it can have an infinite number of owners, ownership is evidenced by shares of stock, and it is managed on shareholders' behalf (at least indirectly) by a board of directors.
**collateral** — Asset pledged to the lender until the loan is satisfied.
**commercial paper** — Short-term financing instrument used mainly by large, creditworthy corporations as an alternative to short-term bank borrowing; mostly sold at a discount to face value and redeemed at face value on maturity.
**common stock (common shares)** — Security representing a fractional ownership interest in the corporation that issued it.
**cost of goods sold** — Income-statement figure for what it costs the company to produce its goods and services: raw materials, production, and direct labour.
**current assets** — Assets most easily converted to cash: cash equivalents such as certificates of deposit and US Treasury bills, receivables, and inventory; under GAAP, anything convertible to cash within one year.
**current liabilities** — Liabilities that must be paid within one year or sooner, typically short-term loans, salaries, income taxes, and accounts payable.
**current ratio** — Current assets divided by current liabilities; a common gauge of a company's ability to meet obligations that are maturing now.
**debt ratio** — Ratio of debt to either assets or equity in the company's financial structure.
**depreciation** — Non-cash expense that writes down the balance-sheet value of an asset over its presumed useful life.
**discounted cash flow (DCF)** — Valuation method built on the time value of money: value equals the present value of the business's future cash flows.
**due diligence** — For a public securities offering, the investigation of the facts and statements of risk in the issuer's registration statement.
**EBIT** — Earnings before interest and taxes; a measure of a firm's profits.
**employee stock ownership plan (ESOP)** — In the US, a formal plan that acquires company shares on behalf of employees and serves as a tax-qualified retirement plan for them.
**enterprise value** — The value of a company's equity plus its debt.
**equity book value** — Total assets minus total liabilities.
**equity capital** — Capital contributed to a business in exchange for rights of ownership.
**executive summary** — Short opening section of a business plan that compellingly explains the opportunity and why it is timely, how the company will pursue it, the entrepreneur's expected results, and a thumbnail sketch of the company and management team.
**financial leverage** — See leverage.
**fixed assets** — Assets that are difficult to convert to cash, e.g. buildings and equipment; sometimes called plant assets.
**fixed costs** — Costs that stay roughly the same no matter how many goods or services are produced.
**goodwill** — Intangible balance-sheet asset recorded when a company buys another for more than the fair market value of its assets; may also represent the acquired firm's reputation, brand names, or patents, which can have real value.
**gross profit** — Sales revenue minus cost of goods sold; the roughest measure of profitability. Also called gross margin.
**income statement** — Financial statement showing the cumulative results of operations over a specified period; also the profit-and-loss statement, or P&L.
**incubator** — Development programme for new businesses, usually run as a nonprofit or charging rent for coworking space shared with other young companies; not limited to early stages, some specialise in later-phase growth.
**initial public offering (IPO)** — A corporation's first offering of its shares to the public.
**inventory** — Supplies, raw materials, components, and so on that a company uses in operations, plus work in process (goods at various production stages) and finished goods awaiting sale or shipment.
**IPO** — See initial public offering.
**leverage** — Degree to which a company's activities are supported by liabilities and long-term debt rather than owners' capital contributions.
**leveraged buyout** — Purchase of a company using a significant amount of borrowed funds on top of the buyer's own equity (the equity is "leveraged" into more purchasing capital); the acquired company's cash flow is the collateral and repays the loans over time.
**liability** — A claim against a company's assets.
**limited-liability corporation (LLC)** — Hybrid company structure combining benefits of a partnership and a corporation.
**limited partnership** — Hybrid with both general and limited partners: the general partner(s) take management responsibility and unlimited liability and must hold at least a 1 percent interest in profits and losses; limited partners have no voice in management and are liable only for their capital contribution plus any debt they specifically accept.
**minimum viable product** — Initial offering with limited features that lets developers test their assumptions about what customers value, how the product performs in the market, and so on.
**net income** — The "bottom line" of the income statement: revenues minus expenses minus taxes. Also called net earnings or net profits.
**net working capital** — Current assets minus current liabilities; the money a company has tied up in short-term operating activities.
**network effects** — A product becomes more valuable to each user as the number of users grows.
**operating earnings** — On the income statement, gross margin minus operating expenses and depreciation; often called earnings before interest and taxes, or EBIT.
**operating expenses** — Expense category covering administrative costs, employee salaries, rent, sales and marketing, and other costs of business not directly attributable to manufacturing a product.
**operating leverage** — Extent to which a company's operating costs are fixed rather than variable; a business that relies heavily on machinery and very few workers has high operating leverage.
**owners' equity** — Whatever is left after total liabilities are deducted from total assets; equals capital contributed by owners plus their retained earnings. Also called shareholders' equity.
**partnership** — Business entity with two or more owners; in the US it is treated as a proprietorship for tax and liability purposes, earnings are distributed per the partnership agreement and taxed as the partners' personal income, so it is simply a conduit for generating income for its partners.
**pitch deck** — Slide presentation created to describe a new venture to potential investors.
**pivot** — Substantive adjustment to a startup's strategy, business model, or offering, often in response to market feedback or testing.
**platform (multisided platform)** — Business that brings producers and consumers together and facilitates their exchanges and interactions; usually digital (eBay, Uber, Alibaba) but also describes malls and temp agencies.
**preferred stock** — Equity-like security that pays a specified dividend and ranks ahead of common stock in distributions or liquidation.
**present value** — Today's monetary value of a future payment, discounted at some annual compound interest rate.
**price-earnings multiple** — Share price divided by earnings per share.
**private placement** — Sale of company stock to one or a few private investors instead of to the public.
**profit** — Financial gain: revenue minus expenses.
**profit-and-loss statement (P&L)** — See income statement.
**profit margin** — Percentage of every sales dollar that reaches the bottom line: net income after tax divided by net sales. Sometimes called return on sales.
**pro forma financial statement** — Balance sheet or income statement containing hypothetical or forecast data.
**prospectus** — Formal document giving potential investors full disclosure about the company, its business, its finances, and how it intends to use the proceeds of its securities issue; the preliminary version is known as a red herring.
**red herring** — See prospectus.
**replacement value** — Valuation approach that estimates the cost of reproducing an asset, rather than relying on its book value.
**retained earnings** — Annual net profits that accumulate on the balance sheet after dividends are paid.
**revenue** — Money that results from selling products or services to customers.
**road show** — Series of meetings between company officials and prospective investors, usually held in major cities ahead of a forthcoming securities issue; investors can question the CEO or CFO about the company and the offering.
**rounds (funding)** — One way of marking a startup's growth stage: the seed stage is the first round, when the venture first borrows capital to finance growth, typically from family or friends; Series A is the next stage, often involving angel investors; Series B takes the company to scale and often involves venture capital.
**S corporation** — In the US, a closely held corporation taxed like a partnership (profits and losses pass straight through to shareholders' personal income-tax returns) while shareholders keep corporate liability protection, limited to their capital contributions.
**seed investment** — See rounds (funding).
**serial entrepreneur** — Someone who has started multiple businesses over time.
**series (funding)** — See rounds (funding).
**sole proprietorship** — Business owned by a single individual; in the US the owner and the business are one and the same for tax and legal-liability purposes, the business is not taxed separately, and all income and deductible expenses go on Schedule C of the owner's personal income-tax return.
**strategy** — A plan that will differentiate the enterprise and give it a competitive advantage.
**times-interest-earned ratio** — Earnings before interest and taxes divided by interest expense; creditors use it to gauge whether a company can keep making interest payments through fluctuating operating results.
**variable costs** — Costs that rise or fall with the volume of output.
**venture capitalist (VC)** — High-risk investor seeking an equity position in a high-potential startup or early-growth company; in return for capital the VC typically takes a significant percentage of ownership and a seat on the board.
**warrant** — Security giving the holder the right to buy the issuer's common shares at a stated price for a stated period; the stated price is generally set above the shares' current valuation.
**working capital** — See net working capital.
