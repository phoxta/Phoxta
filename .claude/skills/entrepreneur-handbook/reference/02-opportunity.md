# Ch 2 — Shaping an Opportunity
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 2, book pages 23–40.

## Purpose
Turn a raw idea into a tested, evaluated opportunity: define the customer problem, size the
market, experiment before investing, then judge the idea against five criteria (value, profit,
fit, durability, financeability). Load when a user says "I have an idea", "is this a good
opportunity", "how big is the market", "should I validate this first", or when the journey
moves from founder-fit into idea evaluation.

## Core ideas
- A business opportunity (Timmons, 2004) is a product or service that **creates significant
  value for customers and significant profit potential for the entrepreneur**. Start with the
  *problem*, not the gizmo: be sure the problem exists and can be described in detail before
  investing heavily in a solution.
- The classic path (spot need → write plan and forecast → pitch → execute the document) usually
  fails because of unknowns at the outset: the right business model, scale, competitor moves,
  supply-chain glitches, and above all *is there a market and how big is it*.
- The user is not always the payer (product for a user, paid by an advertiser); ask every
  market question for both.
- Guesses stay guesses until tested. Silicon Valley approaches (design thinking, **lean startup**)
  expect pivots and run experiments to understand the market *before* proposing a solution; the
  practitioners say this applies equally to ordinary small businesses.
- Lean startup = business-model canvas (hypotheses on one page) + customer development (get out
  and talk) + agile development (fast MVPs, fast iteration).
- Evaluation is not a one-time event; it is a set of questions asked over and over as you
  experiment and learn.
- An opportunity worth pursuing scores on five characteristics: value to customers, significant
  risk-adjusted profit, fit with the founding team, durability, and amenability to financing.
- Risk and return travel together; reject anything that does not pay for the risk taken.
- Every business rests on an economic structure (margin × volume; fixed vs variable costs) that
  sets its breakeven and its profitability beyond breakeven.
- Revenue projections without customers are "the most dangerous trap" — expenses can be
  estimated from experience, revenue must be experimented into.
- Durability dies two ways: fads, and visible demand with low entry barriers. Network effects
  are a source of durability but require speed, trust, the right users and no disintermediation.
- Always compare the opportunity with every alternative, including doing nothing.

## Frameworks & tables
**Timmons' definition of a business opportunity (2004)** — a product or service that creates
significant value for customers *and* offers significant profit potential to the entrepreneur.
The modern refinement: focus first on defining and refining the problem that creates that value.

**The lean startup** (box, pp. 29–30; Eric Ries, as described by Steve Blank for HBR, May 2013)
1. **Business-model canvas** — a one-page document of your hypotheses (what you cannot know in
   advance). Seeing all unknowns on one page shows how the parts might fit. Standard framework by
   Osterwalder & Pigneur, *Business Model Generation*; blank canvases free (with registration) at
   strategyzer.com.
2. **Customer development** — test hypotheses by interacting with customers, vendors and partners
   on each canvas block. Secrecy before a splashy launch is over: "customer feedback matters more
   than secrecy" and constant feedback beats cadenced unveilings (Blank).
3. **Agile development** — build prototypes quickly to generate feedback. What is the *minimum
   viable product* that tests the idea? How quickly and incrementally can you iterate without
   building unnecessary elements?

**Figure 2-1 — The business-model canvas** (Strategyzer; nine blocks and their guiding questions)

| Block | Guiding questions |
|---|---|
| Customer segments | For whom are we creating value? Who are our most important customers? What are the customer archetypes? |
| Value propositions | What value do we deliver? Which customer problem are we helping to solve? What bundles of products/services per segment? Which customer needs are we satisfying? What is the minimum viable product? |
| Channels | Through which channels do segments want to be reached? How do other companies reach them now? Which work best? Which are most cost-efficient? How are we integrating them with customer routines? |
| Customer relationships | How do we get, keep and grow customers? Which relationships have we established? How are they integrated with the rest of the model? How costly are they? |
| Revenue streams | For what value are customers willing to pay? For what do they currently pay? What is the revenue model? What are the pricing tactics? |
| Key resources | What key resources do our value propositions require? Our distribution channels? Customer relationships? Revenue streams? |
| Key activities | What key activities do our value propositions require? Our channels? Customer relationships? Revenue streams? |
| Key partners | Who are our key partners and suppliers? Which key resources do we acquire from partners? Which key activities do partners perform? |
| Cost structure | What are the most important costs inherent to the model? Which key resources are most expensive? Which key activities are most expensive? |

**The five characteristics of an opportunity worth pursuing** (Timmons 1–4; Alfred E. Osborne
Jr., UCLA Price Center, adds 5)
1. Creates significant value for customers who will pay a premium to solve a significant problem
   or fill an important unmet need.
2. Offers significant profit potential to entrepreneur and investors — enough to meet their
   risk-versus-reward expectations.
3. Fits the capabilities (experience and skills) of the founder and management team.
4. Is durable: profits persist and probably grow over a reasonable time; not a fad or a
   quickly disappearing need.
5. Is amenable to financing — promising ideas do not always find backing.

**Figure 2-2 — The risk-versus-return trade-off** (return on the vertical axis, risk on the
horizontal, a diagonal line through the origin)
- Point A: zero risk, very low return (e.g. Treasuries).
- Points B, C, D: on the diagonal — rewards commensurate with risk.
- Point E, and anything below the diagonal: reject — the return does not pay for the risk.

**Profit structure spectrum** — low margin × huge volume (supermarkets) vs. high margin × few
sales (custom furniture makers). Profit margin = profit ÷ sales revenue.

**Cost structure and breakeven**

| Cost structure | Example | Breakeven point | Profitability after breakeven |
|---|---|---|---|
| High fixed, low variable | Auto engine plant; high-volume manufacturer | High | High |
| Low fixed, high variable | Technical service firm | Low | Relatively low |

Fixed costs (debt payments, insurance, specialised equipment, salaried supervisors) stay flat
whether output is 100 or 10,000 units; variable costs (materials, energy, often labour) move
with output. Knowing both plus revenue per unit gives the breakeven point (see appendix B).

**Sources of durability and its killers**
- Durable: software industry — long-lasting and growing.
- Fleeting: fads and fashion (1970s pet rock, Second Life) — the market moves on before
  customer requirements are even defined and addressed.
- Low barriers to entry: demand stays high but supply floods in, prices fall, distress all round.
- Network effects: value grows with users (eBay, Etsy — more buyers attract sellers and vice
  versa); the first to scale in a market is highly defensible because users will not defect to a
  smaller network. Requires building trust among participants, targeting the right users, and
  avoiding disintermediation.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Confirm the problem before building.** Cesar must prove EV drivers need his service, how
  many there are, and how they behave — not just that the technology gap exists.
- **Rate confidence per answer and plan a test for each.** Every "guess" in the confidence column
  becomes an experiment (e.g. price-test with existing owners; count social followers).
- **Ask who uses and who pays** — they may differ; answer the market questions for both.
- **Expect pivots.** Blue River talked to 100+ customers in ten weeks, dropped golf courses for
  farmers, and had a prototype ten weeks later.
- **Feedback beats secrecy.** Show prototypes early; constant feedback beats big unveilings.
- **Reject any opportunity below the risk-return diagonal.** Why accept a 5% return with
  business risk when ten-year US Treasuries pay almost as much with no default risk?
- **Know your margin-volume model.** Supermarket (thin margin, huge volume) or custom furniture
  (fat margin, few sales) — and plan capacity and marketing to match.
- **High fixed / low variable → high breakeven, high profitability after it. Low fixed / high
  variable → low breakeven, low profitability after it.**
- **Expense lines are credible when they come from your own experience** (labour, benefits,
  interest, rent per square foot). **Revenue lines are assumptions until customers exist** —
  experiment to make them real.
- **Fit test:** do you have the managerial, financial and technical capabilities *and* the
  personal commitment? If not, when can you hire them, will they command a high salary, and what
  can be contracted out instead of brought in-house?
- **Fads: wait.** It can be worth waiting to see if a fad has staying power before investing —
  even in digital industries (Freek Vermeulen, London Business School).
- **Visible opportunity + low entry barriers = deadly combination.** If barriers are high, know
  how you will surmount them and whether they stay high.
- **Network-effect businesses: move very, very fast** to be first at scale — but also build
  trust, focus on the right users, and design against disintermediation.
- **War-game the worst case:** name the single worst thing a competitor could do (e.g. cut
  price 20%), model its effect on you, and decide your response in advance.
- **Document every competition answer** — essential if you will seek outside capital.
- **Financing windows close.** Biotech 2000–2004: plenty of good vaccine/therapy ideas, no
  investor confidence, no money. A great idea is not automatically financeable.
- **Compare against doing nothing.** A money-market fund at an anaemic 2% is a legitimate
  alternative until an opportunity with all the right characteristics appears.
- Non-US note: the "risk-free" benchmark is your local government bond yield, not US Treasuries.

## Process / steps
1. **Recognise** the opportunity from inside your experience (Cesar: EV sales heading to 10% of
   units in five years; dealers keep post-warranty service; independents cannot service EVs).
2. **Define the problem** — answer the ten customer and market questions (below), separating
   user from payer.
3. **Grade each answer** — record what you know, your confidence, and the unknowns (Table 2-1
   format); identify where you need more information.
4. **Experiment** — put hypotheses on a business-model canvas; do customer development with
   customers, vendors and partners; build a minimum viable product and iterate quickly.
5. **Re-evaluate continuously** against the five characteristics as you learn.
6. **Test profit** — answer the six economics questions, then build a pro forma income
   statement for one to three years (lenders and investors will want it; see appendix A/B).
7. **Test fit** — capabilities, commitment, hire-vs-contract plan.
8. **Test durability** — sustained need? barriers to entry? network effects? defensibility?
9. **Map the competition** — answer the six competitive questions with documentation.
10. **Check financeability** — will this idea, in this climate, attract capital?
11. **Ask the two final questions** — still attractive on risk-to-return? better than every
    alternative available to you, including doing nothing?

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Customer and market questions** (p. 25–26; rate confidence and note how you will test each)
1. What is the problem you are trying to solve for your customers or users?
2. How many people have this problem — what is the size of the market?
3. Are potential customers/users aware of the problem, or is the need latent (undiscovered)?
4. Is the market stable or growing? If growing, at what annual rate?
5. How will your solution benefit customers or users?
6. What percentage of the total market could you reasonably capture over the next few years?
7. Is another product or service from competitors available to fill part of this demand?
8. Who exactly are the potential customers? Can you name them? Can you describe them?
9. How can you reach them and make a transaction — directly (own website or bricks-and-mortar),
   through distributors (Apple/Google app stores), or through existing retail channels?
10. How does the utility of your product compare with substitutes? (A tablet is easier to carry
    than a laptop but lacks full functionality.)

**Market evaluation worksheet** (Table 2-1 template) — for each row *Problem · Customer benefit
· Market size · Market growth rate · Market share · Competitors · Customer awareness of need ·
Customers · Reaching customers*, fill in: your evaluation | your confidence | unknowns | test.

**Economics of the opportunity** (p. 34–35; give a complete answer to each)
1. Will the business be a price setter or a price taker? What constrains pricing?
2. What is the supply-and-demand situation for your product or service?
3. Is demand elastic (a price rise sharply cuts demand) or inelastic (demand barely moves) in
   the short run?
4. What substitutes do prospective customers have?
5. Will the business be dominated by fixed or variable costs?
6. To what extent can suppliers and employees enforce cost increases on the business?

**Fit questions** (p. 36)
- What expertise and skills will it take to run this business? Do you have them?
- At what point can you hire for the gaps — and are those skills in high demand (high salary)?
- What can be contracted out versus brought in-house?

**Durability questions** (p. 38)
- Is the need you identified likely to be sustained, or is it a fad?
- Can you defend the solution? Can you take advantage of network effects?
- Do other aspects of your offering make it hard for competitors to emulate or replace?

**Competitive landscape questions** (p. 38–39; answer with documentation)
1. How are customers currently satisfying the need (e.g. going to the dealer)?
2. What are the strengths and weaknesses of the main competitors (quality, service, price)?
3. How would a smart competitor respond to your entry (price cut, bundling, better experience)?
4. Are barriers to entry high or low? Low → competitors keep entering until returns are driven
   low. High → how will you surmount them, and will they stay high?
5. Have current competitors shown themselves agile and responsive to customer needs and
   technical change?
6. What is the single worst thing a competitor could do to you (e.g. drop price 20%)? How would
   it affect your prospects, how would you respond, and what pricing / positioning / service /
   distribution / product strategy gives you a *sustainable* advantage?

**Two questions to ask yourself** (p. 39)
1. Is it still attractive on the risk-to-return relationship (figure 2-2; the book cites 2-1)?
2. Is it more or less attractive than the other opportunities available to you?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Cesar / Electric Car Care Center** (fictional, runs through the chapter) — dealership
  service manager spots post-warranty EV repair gap; sizes it (2M EVs, 32% CAGR, 18% local share
  guess), plans tests, builds a 3-year pro forma (year-1 loss $21K → $140K after tax by year 3)
  — a strong fit, but every revenue number is still a hypothesis; and if EV buyers turn out to
  value convenience over price, the low-price positioning must be rethought before building.
- **Blue River Technology** (Heraud & Redden, Stanford) — robotic mowers for golf courses;
  100+ customer conversations in ten weeks showed golf courses did not care; farmers wanted
  chemical-free weeding; prototype in ten weeks, $3M+ venture funding nine months later,
  robotic lettuce thinner launched by 2017 — customer development finds the real problem.
- **Pet rock (1970s), Second Life** — fads gone before requirements were met — durability test.
- **Software industry** — the durable counter-example: long-lasting and growing.
- **eBay, Etsy** — more buyers attract more sellers and vice versa — network effects reward the
  first to scale.
- **Supermarkets vs custom furniture makers** — same goal, opposite margin-volume structures.
- **Auto engine plant vs technical service firm** — high-fixed vs low-fixed cost models and
  their breakeven behaviour.
- **Biotech 2000–2004** — good ideas starved of capital by lost investor confidence — a great
  idea is not automatically financeable.
- **10-year US Treasuries / 2% money-market fund** — the no-risk alternatives every opportunity
  must beat.

## Pitfalls
- Falling in love with what the gizmo does and forgetting to assess its value to customers.
- Treating the business plan as a straight line from need to solution to execution.
- Assuming the customer's motive (low price) instead of discovering it (convenience).
- Inventing revenue growth with no customers behind it.
- Keeping the product secret until a big launch instead of gathering constant feedback.
- Building unnecessary features instead of the minimum viable product.
- Accepting returns below the risk-return line.
- Ignoring the cost structure and therefore the breakeven point.
- Chasing a fad, or a visible market with low barriers to entry.
- Scaling a network business without trust, the right users, or protection from
  disintermediation.
- Skipping the competitor war-game and the "worst thing they could do" question.
- Assuming a good idea will find financing regardless of the climate.
- Never comparing the opportunity with alternatives, including doing nothing.

## Key terms
`business opportunity` — product or service creating significant customer value and significant profit potential (Timmons).
`latent need` — a problem customers have but have not yet recognised.
`pivot` — a deliberate change of direction as experiments reveal what the market wants.
`lean startup` — Ries's method: find a repeatable, scalable business model via canvas + customer development + agile development.
`business-model canvas` — one-page grid of nine blocks holding your business hypotheses (Osterwalder & Pigneur).
`customer development` — testing hypotheses by direct interaction with customers, vendors and partners from the start.
`agile development` — fast, incremental prototyping to generate customer feedback.
`minimum viable product (MVP)` — the smallest thing you can build that tests the idea.
`profit margin` — profit ÷ sales revenue, as a percentage.
`fixed costs` / `variable costs` — costs flat regardless of output vs. costs that move with output.
`breakeven point` — units you must sell before earning a profit (appendix B).
`price setter` / `price taker` — a business that can set its price vs. one that must accept the market's.
`elastic` / `inelastic demand` — demand that falls sharply vs. barely with a price rise.
`pro forma income statement` — best-estimate future revenues, expenses, taxes and profit for one or more years.
`durable opportunity` — profits persist and grow over a reasonable period; not a fad.
`barriers to entry` — obstacles that stop new competitors flooding in.
`network effects` — offering's value rises with the number of users; first to scale is defensible.
`disintermediation` — platform participants trust each other enough to bypass you as intermediary.
`risk-versus-return trade-off` — higher risk must be matched by higher return; below the line is rejected.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Problem & Market Evaluator** — the 10 customer/market questions with per-answer confidence
  (1–5) and "how I'll test it" → a Table 2-1-style grid plus a ranked list of unknowns → makes
  the founder confront what is a guess before spending.
- **Business Model Canvas board** — nine blocks, each hypothesis tagged untested / testing /
  validated / invalidated, with linked experiments → live canvas + pivot log → operationalises
  lean startup for non-tech founders.
- **Experiment planner** — hypothesis, MVP description, customer-conversation target (e.g. 100
  in 10 weeks), success metric → tracked experiments and outcomes → Blue River-style discovery.
- **Opportunity Scorecard** — score the five characteristics 1–5 with evidence notes → radar +
  go / learn-more / drop verdict → single gate on the journey map.
- **Risk-Return Plotter** — expected return, risk rating, local risk-free rate → point plotted
  vs the diagonal (A–E) → instant "below the line" rejection.
- **Pro Forma Income Statement builder** — revenue assumptions + expense lines (with loan
  amortisation inputs like $300K @ 9%/12 yrs) for 3 years → Table 2-2 output, flags revenue
  lines as untested → lender-ready and honesty-enforcing.
- **Cost Structure & Breakeven analyser** — fixed costs, variable cost per unit, price per unit
  → breakeven units and post-breakeven margin profile → tells the founder which of the two
  economic models they are in (feeds appendix B).
- **Competitor War-Game** — the six competition questions plus a "worst thing" scenario (e.g.
  −20% price) → impact on the pro forma + pre-planned response → documentation pack for
  investors.
- **Opportunity Comparator** — side-by-side scorecards of several ideas plus a "do nothing"
  baseline at the local risk-free rate → ranked list → answers the two final questions.

## Summing up (book)
- A business opportunity (1) solves a real customer problem, (2) offers significant
  risk-adjusted profit, (3) fits the leadership team's capabilities, (4) stays profitable over
  a reasonable span, and (5) is amenable to financing.
- Entrepreneurs spend too little time on the problem they are solving and on testing how users
  and customers actually experience it.
- An experimental or lean approach lowers risk and reveals customer needs and reactions before
  significant investment.
- Evaluate opportunities on the market, current and anticipated competition, underlying
  economics, and the resources needed to succeed.
