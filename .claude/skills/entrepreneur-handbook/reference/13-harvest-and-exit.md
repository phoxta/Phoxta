# Ch 13 — Harvest Time
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 13, book pages 213–224.

## Purpose
When and how to turn the value locked inside the business into personal wealth: why founders
cash out, the harvesting mechanisms (IPO, merger/acquisition, ESOP, management buyout, sale to a
new owner, partial "shearing"), timing, and how the business gets valued. Load when a user asks
"should I sell", "what's my company worth", "what are my exit options", "how do I hand over the
business", or when a mature business enters a "looking to the future" stage.

## Core ideas
- Most entrepreneurs eventually harvest the monetary value locked in the enterprise; a minority
  pass the business to family members (mentioned only in passing — the chapter does not detail
  family succession).
- Three dominant motivations: **diversify wealth** (net worth dangerously concentrated in one
  basket), **the business has reached the end of its line** (at least under this leadership, or
  the competitive wind has turned), and **the urge to begin anew** (loving the build phase, not
  the operating phase). Retirement and an offer "too good to refuse" also trigger exits.
- Choose the mechanism that is most timely and appropriate; each has distinct trade-offs. If you
  only want *some* capital out, shear cash flow instead of selling.
- **IPO**: few firms qualify; insiders face Rule 144 caps and underwriter lockups (up to six
  months); costs, scrutiny and reporting deter founders even when VCs want the fast exit; the
  upside is usually a higher price, especially when investor appetite is high.
- **M&A**: far more harvests happen this way than by IPO. Three issues decide the outcome — how
  the deal is valued, how payment is structured, and the seller's post-deal relationship.
- **ESOP**: a tax-qualified employee retirement plan that buys the owner's shares gradually over
  years — a market for shares where none exists, at the cost of annual appraisals and eventual
  employee majority ownership, and with concentration risk for employees.
- **Management buyout**: managers know the cash-generating potential best; typically financed as
  a leveraged buyout on the company's own assets, or with the seller acting as lender.
- **Sale to a new owner**: brokers connect you to qualified buyers; the asset-vs-stock choice
  decides who keeps the liabilities.
- **Timing**: buyer and investor mood swings between optimism and fear; the same business sells
  for much more when buyers are giddy. Only the ESOP (sales spread over many years) is exempt.
- **Valuation** sits at the heart of every mechanism except shearing. If you cannot answer "what
  is this company really worth", you negotiate from weakness. Two reliable methods:
  earnings-multiple and discounted cash flow; DCF is forward-looking and fairer to growing firms.

## Frameworks & tables
**Why entrepreneurs cash out** (pp. 213–214)

| Motivation | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Diversify wealth | Most net worth is in one company; a technology shift, a powerful new competitor or a setback could wipe it out |
| End of the line | The business has gone as far as it can under you; growth needs investment you won't make; or the competitive environment turns (hardware-store owner facing a national chain's buying power) |
| Begin anew | You love creating something from nothing; once operations dominate your time you are happy to move on |
| Also | Retirement; an offer too good to refuse |

**Harvesting mechanisms — pros and cons** (pp. 214–220)

| Mechanism | How it works | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPO | Public market for shares; founders and investors sell over time within regulatory limits (see ch. 9, app. D) | Often the highest price, especially with strong investor appetite; liquidity over time; VCs get a fast, high-return exit | Few firms qualify (too small, limited potential, moribund industry); Rule 144 monthly caps on restricted shares; lockup up to 6 months; deal costs, public scrutiny, reporting burden |
| Merger / acquisition | Strategic merger or purchase by a company wanting your patents, product lines or capabilities | Most common harvest by far; can be a full cash exit | Complex; needs experienced legal and financial advisers; private-to-private deals need formal valuations; payment may be tied up in acquirer stock; seller rarely keeps a free hand |
| ESOP | Tax-qualified retirement plan buys the owner's shares gradually over years; company may contribute part of the price | Creates a market for closely held shares; retirement plan and ownership stake for employees; gradual, tax-favoured cash-out | Independent appraisal every year (costly for a small firm); employees eventually own the majority; employees are doubly non-diversified (job + retirement in one company — Enron) |
| Management buyout / LBO | Senior managers buy the company, borrowing against its assets; debt-to-equity can reach 10:1 | Buyers already know the business and industry; continuity of experienced management | Post-deal: unit sell-offs, asset sales, headcount and inventory cuts to service debt; junk-bond funding seldom available now, so the seller often takes a collateral-backed note and is paid over many years |
| Sale to a new owner | Business broker finds qualified buyers (typically businesses valued up to $20M); commission mostly paid at close | Access to buyers you can't find yourself; clean break possible | Must negotiate asset sale vs stock sale (liabilities); broker fee |
| Shearing (box, p. 219) | Pocket the cash flow not needed to maintain or expand the business instead of reinvesting it | Periodic capital for living standards, retirement income or diversification without selling; no valuation needed | Limits internally funded growth; if growth still matters, substitute debt for internal cash (changes debt-to-equity) |
| Family transfer | Pass the business to family members | Keeps the business in the family | Not covered in this chapter beyond a mention |

**Three issues in any merger or acquisition** (p. 216)
1. **How the deal is valued** — different methods give different numbers.
2. **How payment is structured** — cash, cash + acquirer stock, or debt; in a merger you may end
   up holding stock of a newly formed company. Cash is ideal; every other form ties your capital
   to the other company for a period. Not all stock deals are bad (Hotmail → 2.7M Microsoft
   shares), but other acquirers' shares may be far less solid.
3. **The seller's relationship with the merged/acquired company** — many deals require a period
   of managerial involvement; approach with care, the acquirer won't give you a free hand.

**Asset sale vs stock sale** (pp. 219–220)

| | Asset sale | Stock sale |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer gets | Physical elements: employees, buildings, equipment, plus intangibles (trademarks, goodwill) | The company itself |
| Liabilities | Stay with you (e.g. a lawsuit from an employee you hired) | Shift to the new owner |
| Better for seller? | Usually not | Usually yes |

**Best candidates for a leveraged buyout** (p. 218)
- High levels of predictable free cash flow.
- Few capital-spending requirements.
- Little existing debt.
- Substantial nonessential assets (can be sold to pay down debt).

**Valuation methods** (pp. 220–222; detail in appendix C)

| Method | Mechanics | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPO pricing | Underwriter weighs investor mood, comparable public P/E ratios, current and forecast performance, proprietary technology, growth potential; issues slightly below anticipated trading level | Captures market appetite | Unscientific; deliberate discount rewards first investors, not you |
| Earnings-based (multiple) | EBIT (or EBITDA = EBIT + depreciation + amortization, the non-cash charges) × a multiple drawn from recent comparable sales. e.g. EBIT $2M × 5 = $10M | Simple; anchored to what buyers actually paid | Not forward-looking — shortchanges a fast-growing firm; multiples float with investor mood |
| Discounted cash flow (DCF) | Forecast several years of cash flows; discount to present value with time-value-of-money maths | Future-oriented; "captures all the elements that affect the value of the company" (Copeland, Koller & Murrin) | Needs a credible forecast and usually professional help |

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Sell when investors and buyers are giddy with optimism**; the pendulum swings to fear and
  the same business fetches far less. Watch the mood before selling shares or the company.
- **Prefer cash**; stock or debt payment ties your capital to the acquirer. Judge the acquirer's
  shares as an investment before accepting them.
- **Never enter an M&A deal without experienced legal and financial advisers** — the typical
  founder has done zero of these transactions.
- **Post-deal management role**: accept only with eyes open; you will not run it the way you did.
- **IPO reality check**: expect Rule 144 monthly sale caps on restricted shares plus a lockup of
  up to six months; you cannot dump shares on day one.
- **ESOP fit**: good when there is no public market and you want a gradual, tax-favoured exit
  plus employee ownership; accept an annual independent appraisal and eventual employee majority.
- **MBO/LBO fit**: strong when the business has predictable free cash flow, low capex, little
  debt and nonessential assets. Debt-to-equity after the deal can hit 10:1; expect the buyers to
  sell non-core units, aircraft and property and to cut heads, costs and inventory.
- **If buyers can't borrow**, you become the lender: a collateral-backed note paid as principal
  and interest over many years — your harvest is spread out and carries the buyers' risk.
- **Broker threshold**: business brokers usually handle companies valued up to ~$20M; fees are
  commission, mostly paid at closing. Find them via IBBA, ACG, AM&AA or APMAA listings.
- **Sell stock, not assets, where you can** — an asset sale leaves liabilities with you.
- **Multiples must come from recent comparable transactions**, never "from outer space"; the
  multiple swings the value enormously (5× on $2M EBIT = $10M).
- **Use EBITDA** rather than EBIT when depreciation/amortization are material, so the multiple
  attaches to cash actually available to the owner.
- **Fast-growing firm → insist on DCF**; a multiple of current earnings undervalues it.
- **Learn enough valuation to work with an appraiser** — it matters only a few times in a career
  but its impact on the harvest is huge (box, p. 222).
- **Shearing rule**: if the company is as big as you can comfortably handle, pocket excess cash;
  if you still want growth, replace the pocketed cash with debt and accept the higher leverage.
- **LBO scale**: US leveraged buyouts still totalled $70.5B of company sales in 2016 — a
  mainstream route, not a relic of the 1980s.
- Non-US note: Rule 144, lockups, ESOP tax qualification and the broker associations named are
  US-specific; a founder elsewhere needs the local equivalents (e.g. UK employee-ownership trusts,
  local listing rules and lock-in periods).

## Process / steps
**Harvest planning sequence** (chapter flow)
1. Name your motivation: diversify, end of the line, begin anew, retire, or an unrefusable offer.
2. Decide full harvest vs partial: if you only want some capital, shear cash flow instead.
3. Pick the mechanism that is timely and appropriate (table above); check the fit rules.
4. Assemble advisers: legal, financial, a business appraiser, a broker if selling outright.
5. Value the business (multiple and/or DCF) so you can negotiate from knowledge.
6. For M&A: settle valuation method, payment structure and your post-deal role.
7. Negotiate asset vs stock sale.
8. Time the sale to buyer/investor optimism.

**ESOP mechanics** (Macmillan Metal Works example, pp. 216–217)
1. Attorney sets up the plan.
2. Business appraiser values the company's shares.
3. Owner sells a first tranche to the plan (Macmillan: 2,000 shares in year one).
4. Qualified employees commit to buying specified numbers of shares each year; the company
   contributes part of the purchase price.
5. Proceeds go to the owner, who diversifies personal assets; repeat annually with a fresh
   appraisal.

**Post-LBO playbook the buyers will run** (p. 218): sell underperforming or non-fitting units;
sell aircraft and nonessential property to pay down debt immediately; raise free cash by cutting
headcount, expenses and inventory.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Motivation check**
- Is most of my net worth concentrated in this one company?
- Has the business gone as far as it can under my leadership? Would the next stage need
  investment I don't want to make?
- Is the competitive environment turning against us?
- Do I prefer building to operating? Am I happy to move on now that operations dominate?
- Do I want all my capital out at once, or periodic withdrawals (shearing)?

**Mechanism selector**
- Is there (or could there be) a public market for the shares? If not, rule out IPO.
- Would a strategic buyer want our patents, product lines or capabilities?
- Do we have a management team able and willing to buy? Does the company have predictable free
  cash flow, low capex, little debt and nonessential assets (LBO fit)?
- Do I want employees to own the company over time and can we afford annual appraisals (ESOP)?
- Is the business below ~$20M in value (broker territory)?

**M&A deal checklist** (p. 216)
- Which valuation method is being used, and what do the alternatives say?
- Payment mix: cash / acquirer stock / debt / new-company stock? How solid is the acquirer's stock?
- What managerial involvement is required of me, for how long, with what authority?
- Do I have experienced legal and financial advisers on the deal?

**Sale structure checklist** (pp. 219–220)
- Asset sale or stock sale? Which liabilities stay with me?
- Broker engaged? Commission terms and timing?

**Valuation prep checklist** (pp. 220–222)
- Current EBIT and EBITDA (add back depreciation and amortization).
- Recent comparable transactions and the multiples paid.
- Multi-year cash-flow forecast and an appropriate discount rate for DCF.
- Is the industry in or out of favour right now (multiple direction)?
- Have I learned enough to brief and challenge a professional appraiser?

**Timing check** (p. 220)
- Are buyers/investors currently optimistic or fearful? What are comparable deals fetching?

**Broker associations** (p. 219): International Business Brokers Association; Association for
Corporate Growth; Alliance of Merger & Acquisition Advisors; Association of Professional Merger
and Acquisitions Advisors.

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Sabeer Bhatia / Hotmail** — sold to Microsoft for 2.7 million Microsoft shares — stock
  payment can be excellent when the acquirer's shares are solid.
- **Macmillan Metal Works (hypothetical)** — 80 employees, founder owned 100%, wealth trapped;
  ESOP gave employees a retirement plan and ownership while he cashed out gradually — ESOP as a
  market for closely held shares.
- **Enron employees (early 2000s)** — lost jobs and retirement savings at once — the double
  non-diversification risk of employee ownership.
- **Hardware-store owner vs a national chain** — competitive wind turning — a valid "end of the
  line" signal.
- **1980s raiders and junk bonds** — bought companies whose parts were worth more than the
  whole; that debt source is seldom available now, so sellers often finance the buyers.
- **Copeland, Koller & Murrin** ("the bible of valuation") — endorse DCF as the comprehensive
  method — use it for growth businesses.

## Pitfalls
- Negotiating without knowing what the company is really worth.
- Doing an M&A deal without experienced advisers.
- Accepting acquirer stock without judging it as an investment; assuming a free hand post-deal.
- Underestimating IPO restrictions (Rule 144, six-month lockup), costs and scrutiny.
- Choosing an ESOP without budgeting annual appraisals or accepting employee majority ownership;
  ignoring employees' concentration risk.
- Financing a management buyout yourself without weighing a decade of repayment risk.
- Selling assets and unknowingly keeping the liabilities.
- Using an arbitrary multiple, or valuing a fast-growing company on current earnings only.
- Selling during a period of buyer fear because you did not watch the market mood.

## Key terms
`harvest` — converting the value locked in the enterprise into personal wealth via an exit mechanism.
`shearing` — pocketing excess cash flow instead of reinvesting it; a partial harvest without a sale.
`lockup agreement` — underwriter-imposed ban on pre-IPO shareholders selling for a period after the IPO (up to six months).
`SEC Rule 144` — US rule capping how many restricted shares an insider may sell in any one-month period (see appendix D).
`ESOP` — employee stock ownership plan: a tax-qualified retirement plan that buys the owner's shares on behalf of employees over years.
`management buyout (MBO)` — purchase of the company by its senior managers.
`leveraged buyout (LBO)` — a buyout financed largely with debt secured on the company's own assets.
`junk (high-yield) bonds` — the outside debt that funded 1980s LBOs; seldom available to buyer groups today.
`business broker` — intermediary who finds qualified buyers, usually for businesses valued up to ~$20M, paid by commission.
`asset sale / stock sale` — selling the business's assets (liabilities stay with the seller) vs selling the shares (the whole company transfers).
`EBIT / EBITDA` — earnings before interest and taxes / plus depreciation and amortization added back (non-cash).
`multiple` — the number applied to EBIT/EBITDA, taken from recent comparable transactions.
`discounted cash flow (DCF)` — valuation by forecasting future cash flows and discounting them to present value.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Exit motivation diagnostic** — five motivation questions → primary driver (diversify / end
  of line / begin anew / income) → recommends full harvest vs shearing.
- **Exit mechanism selector** — public-market feasibility, strategic-buyer interest, management
  capacity, LBO fit, ESOP appetite, size band → ranked mechanisms with pros/cons from the table.
- **Quick valuation calculator** — EBIT, D&A, comparable multiples, optional 5-year cash-flow
  forecast + discount rate → EBIT-multiple, EBITDA-multiple and DCF values side by side, with a
  flag when growth makes the multiple method unreliable.
- **Deal term checker** — payment mix, acquirer-stock quality, post-deal role, asset vs stock →
  risk notes on each M&A issue before signing.
- **LBO candidate score** — free-cash-flow predictability, capex needs, debt, nonessential
  assets → suitability for a management buyout and a note on seller-financing exposure.
- **Shearing planner** — cash flow, maintenance needs, growth plans → how much can be withdrawn
  and the resulting debt-to-equity if growth is debt-funded instead.
- **Market-mood gauge** — recent comparable multiples and IPO activity → optimism/fear reading
  and a "good time to sell?" prompt.
- **Adviser roster** — checklist of legal, financial, appraiser and broker slots with the
  broker associations as a starting list (with a local-equivalents note for non-US users).

## Summing up (book)
- Entrepreneurs harvest to diversify wealth, to take the business to a higher level under
  someone else, or to try something new.
- An IPO gives the team liquidity over time but is available to very few enterprises.
- For most companies, sale by acquisition is far more likely than an IPO; watch how the deal is
  valued, how payment is structured (cash, stock, debt) and how any ongoing relationship is defined.
- An ESOP is a tax-qualified retirement plan that buys the owner's shares over years — in effect,
  a sale to the employees.
- Management groups often buy out the founder, via a leveraged buyout or a debt arrangement with
  the seller.
- The earnings-multiple method values the firm at EBIT × a multiple, which must match what
  investors recently paid for comparable companies.
- DCF gives a better measure of value because it is future oriented.
