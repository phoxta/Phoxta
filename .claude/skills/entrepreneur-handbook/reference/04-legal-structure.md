# Ch 4 — Organizing Your Company (choosing a legal form)
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 4, book pages 63–76.

## Purpose
Which legal form the venture should take — sole proprietorship, general or limited partnership,
C corporation, S corporation or LLC — and how objectives, investors, taxation and liability drive
the choice. Load when a user asks "should I incorporate", "LLC or corporation", "do I need a
partnership agreement", or before any fundraising, equity-compensation or first-hire module.

## Core ideas
- The choice is driven **chiefly by your objectives and those of your investors**; taxation and
  legal liability play a supporting part (p. 63).
- The law builds in a trade-off: the most favorable tax treatment usually costs you **some
  liability protection, some flexibility, or both**.
- Two axes explain every form: **levels of taxation** (one = flow-through to owners; two =
  corporate tax plus tax on dividends) and **exposure of personal assets** (unlimited vs limited).
- Flow-through entities (proprietorship, partnership, LLC, S corp) are conduits: owners are taxed
  on **income earned, not cash distributed** — earnings retained for growth still create a
  personal tax bill.
- Access to capital scales with form: own funds and borrowing (sole prop) → partners' pockets
  (partnership) → a vast pool of individuals and institutions plus successive public offerings
  (C corp).
- Corporations are vastly outnumbered by sole proprietorships yet account for **over 60% of US
  sales** because they include nearly all major companies (p. 70).
- Outside equity investors and venture capitalists will not participate in a sole proprietorship,
  cannot hold S corp shares (most VC firms are partnerships), and want a block of ownership — they
  may not take an unincorporated venture seriously.
- A business may pass through several forms in its life (sole prop → partnership → C corp; LP →
  LLC → C corp); each transition costs considerable legal work and administrative burden.
- High-potential, fast-track ventures should skip those hoops: the corporate form is almost always
  best from the start.
- Everything here is US law and not legal advice; always consult an attorney (box, p. 64).

## Frameworks & tables
**Table 4-1 — Forms of business** (the chapter's own summary, p. 76)

| Form | Key benefits | Key disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Simple to organize and operate; one level of taxation | Full liability of the owner; cannot raise outside equity, limiting potential size |
| General partnership | Brings in additional talent and personal capital; one level of taxation | Full liability of partners; capital limited to partners' pockets and borrowing; dissolves on death or withdrawal of any partner unless the agreement says otherwise |
| Limited partnership | Limited liability (for limited partners); one level of taxation | Complex to set up |
| C corporation | Can attract equity capital through share ownership; preferred form of venture capitalists; can deduct many employee benefit payments; shareholders have limited liability | Complex to set up and operate; income subject to double taxation |
| S corporation | One level of taxation like a proprietorship/partnership; shareholders have limited liability | Complex to set up and operate; limited number of shareholders; venture capitalists cannot be shareholders |
| Limited-liability company (LLC) | Simpler to set up and operate than a corporation; limited liability for members; one level of taxation; unlimited number of members | Cannot attract outside equity capital |

**Full comparison of the six forms** (synthesised from pp. 63–75)

| Form | Liability | Taxation | Ownership & control | Cost & complexity | Fundraising fit | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited — claimants can pursue the owner's personal property, not just business assets | One level: all income and expenses on Schedule C of the personal return; taxed whether or not cash is taken out; no way to shelter income; life/disability/medical premiums may be only partly deductible | One individual with complete control; income pocketed or reinvested at will | Lowest: no government filing or charter (some businesses need local licences, e.g. restaurants, day care; a trading name needs a DBA certificate); dissolves as informally as it began | Personal funds plus what the owner can borrow; no outside equity; cannot offer ownership to attract talent | Ends at the owner's death; assets pass to the estate |
| General partnership | Unlimited and **joint and several** — any partner can be pursued for the whole claim, regardless of capital share or fault | One level: profits allocated per the agreement and taxed as each partner's personal income; some benefits may not be deductible | Two or more owners; shared judgment (ideally complementary skills) but slower decisions; disputes can tie it in knots | Easy apart from the time and legal cost of a written partnership agreement | More than one pool of personal capital plus borrowing; still no outside equity | Ends on withdrawal or death of any partner unless the agreement provides otherwise |
| Limited partnership | General partner(s): unlimited; limited partners: capped at capital contributed plus debts specifically accepted | One level; profits and losses may be allocated differently (e.g. 20/80 profits but 99% of losses to LPs); losses deductible only up to capital at risk | General partner(s) manage and must hold at least 1% of profits and losses; limited partners have no voice in management | Complex to set up | Pairs operators with well-heeled passive investors; the vehicle of VC and real-estate funds | Per the partnership agreement |
| C corporation | Shareholders limited to their investment; the veil is pierced only for fraud (officers remain personally liable for acts such as failing to withhold and pay corporate taxes) | **Two levels**: corporate tax on profits, then shareholders taxed on dividends; cost of certain officer/employee benefits deductible; salaries deductible but policed by IRS "reasonable compensation" rules | Unlimited number of owners holding transferable, giftable shares; managed for shareholders by a board of directors; stock and options attract and retain talent; every share sold dilutes control | Costly to incorporate; must adopt governance rules (stockholder meetings, board meetings, election of officers); monitored by federal, state and some local agencies; public corporations report quarterly | Best: sells stock to individuals and institutions (pension funds, mutual funds), can do successive public offerings, can borrow; the form VCs prefer; lets founders turn paper wealth into cash | Theoretically unlimited life |
| S corporation | Shareholders limited to their capital contribution | One level (partnership-style flow-through to shareholders' personal returns) | Closely held: 35 or fewer stockholders (a married couple counts as one), one class of stock (voting differences allowed), individuals/estates/certain trusts only, all US citizens, domestic company | Complex to set up and operate (it is still a corporation) | Weak: no corporate or partnership shareholders, so VC firms are excluded | Corporate (perpetual) |
| Limited-liability company (LLC) | Members limited (S-corp-level protection) | One level flow-through; operating agreement may split profits and losses in any ratio, not only by capital; members pay self-employment tax | Owners are "members"; no limit on number or type of members; law firms often use it | Simple to run: no statutory minutes, meetings or resolutions; state laws differ | Cannot attract outside equity capital (per Table 4-1) | Dissolves on the death of a member |

### Non-US note (UK equivalents)
The forms above are US creatures (Schedule C, IRS, the S election, the 35-shareholder cap). Rough
UK mapping: sole proprietorship → **sole trader** (register for Self Assessment; unlimited
liability); general partnership → **ordinary partnership** (tax-transparent; joint and several
liability); limited partnership → **limited partnership** (registered; the fund vehicle); LLC →
**limited liability partnership (LLP)** is the closest tax-transparent, limited-liability hybrid
(professional firms use it), though small owner-managers usually just form a Ltd; C corporation →
**private company limited by shares (Ltd)** (corporation tax at company level, dividends taxed again
personally; a **plc** is the listed form); S corporation → no direct equivalent — the owner-managed
Ltd paying salary plus dividends plays a similar role. Other countries have analogous ladders
(GmbH/AG, SARL/SAS, Pty Ltd). The agent must always check the user's jurisdiction, never quote US
thresholds as if they applied elsewhere, and point the user to a local accountant or solicitor.

**Partnership agreement — six issues to set down in writing** (pp. 67–68)
1. Amount and nature of each partner's capital contribution (cash, a patent, property).
2. How profits and losses are allocated.
3. Salaries and draws against profits.
4. Management responsibilities.
5. Consequences of a partner's withdrawal, retirement, disability or death.
6. Means of dissolution and liquidation.

**S corporation qualification requirements** (p. 73)
- Only one class of stock (differences in voting rights allowed).
- Domestic corporation, wholly owned by US citizens, no more than 80% of revenue from non-US sources.
- 35 or fewer stockholders (husband and wife count as one).
- No more than 25% of revenue from passive sources (interest, dividends, rents, royalties).
- Shareholders are only individuals, estates and certain trusts — no corporations or partnerships
  (this is the rule that excludes venture capitalists).

**Limited partnership economics** (pp. 69–70)
- General partner: management plus unlimited liability; must hold at least 1% of profits and losses.
- Limited partners: no management voice; liability capped at capital contributed (plus debts
  expressly accepted); losses deductible only up to capital at risk.
- Allocation can be asymmetric: e.g. profits 20% GP / 80% LPs while LPs take 99% of losses
  (attractive to investors who can offset other taxable income).
- "Creative" waterfalls seen in VC and real-estate funds: LPs take 99% of profits until their entire
  capital is returned, then the split moves to 30% GP / 70% LPs.

### Box — "Tips for starting a sole proprietor business" (p. 65)
- You can start simply by doing it (consulting, buying and reselling, a subscription newsletter).
- Keep household and business finances separate: open a dedicated business bank account and run
  every cheque and receipt through it.
- Use QuickBooks or similar; track expenses under the same categories as the business-expense
  section of Schedule C so deductions are easy to itemise; scan or snap every receipt from your phone.
- Trading under a name other than your own (e.g. "Surfside Management Consulting") may require a
  "fictitious name" / "doing business as" (DBA) certificate in the city where the business is
  domiciled — check the name is not already taken first.
- Most US states forbid Corporation, Corp., Incorporated, Inc. — and even Company and Co. — after
  the name of an unincorporated business.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Large early losses** and investors who can use them to offset other income → partnership or LLC.
- **Value created mainly by generating cash and distributing it** to investors → partnership or LLC.
- **Long-term cash investment needed and value harvested through a sale or public offering** → C corp.
- **High-potential, fast-track venture** → incorporate from the start; use stock and options to lure
  an experienced team and conserve cash, even paying consultants in stock instead of cash.
- **Any plan to raise venture capital** → C corp: VCs won't join a sole proprietorship, can't hold
  S corp stock, and want a block of ownership.
- **Want to share ownership with employees** → impossible in a sole proprietorship beyond simple
  profit-sharing; requires a change of form.
- **Two or more owners and no written agreement** → by default the partnership ends on any partner's
  death or withdrawal, and every partner is exposed to the others' actions.
- **Flow-through entity retaining earnings for growth** → owners still owe tax on that undistributed
  income; budget for it.
- **C corp owner-employee tempted to pay all profit as salary** (deductible, so not double-taxed) →
  IRS reasonable-compensation rules limit this.
- **Corporate veil** holds unless there is fraud; officers stay personally liable for duties such as
  withholding and paying corporate taxes.
- **Each share sold** dilutes ownership and personal control — price that against the growth
  capital it buys.
- **S corp caps to watch**: 35 shareholders, one class of stock, 25% passive revenue, 80% non-US
  revenue, no entity shareholders.
- **Corporations dominate**: over 60% of US sales despite being far fewer than sole proprietorships.
- **Plan the evolution first**: choose for the stage you are entering *and* the next one; each
  form change is a legal and administrative project.
- **Before deciding**, consult a qualified tax attorney or accountant (p. 75).

## Process / steps
The chapter gives no single numbered procedure; this sequence follows "Which form makes sense for
you?" (pp. 74–75) in the book's order.
1. Write down your objectives and your investors' objectives (control, liability shelter, tax
   treatment, exit).
2. Project the venture's financial shape: early losses or profits? cash paid out to owners or
   reinvested? long-term outside cash required? exit by sale or IPO?
3. Map that shape to a form using the decision rules above (losses or cash-out → partnership or
   LLC; long-term capital and exit → C corp; fast track → C corp now).
4. Check eligibility and fit constraints (S corp requirements, VC involvement, employee equity,
   number and type of owners, non-US owners, passive income).
5. Sketch the likely evolution (e.g. sole prop → partnership → C corp) and weigh the cost of each
   transition against starting in the later form now.
6. Consult a qualified tax attorney or accountant.
7. Execute the setup: DBA and licences (sole prop); written partnership or operating agreement
   (partnership/LLC); charter, governance rules and officer elections (corporation); a separate
   bank account and Schedule-C-aligned bookkeeping for everyone.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Form-choice diagnostic** (answer yes/no; derived from the chapter's decision factors)
- Will more than one person own the business?
- Will you raise outside equity from angels, funds or the public?
- Will you seek venture capital specifically?
- Do you intend to give employees or consultants a share of ownership (stock, options)?
- Do you expect large losses in the early years — and could your investors use them against other income?
- Is the business a cash generator that will pay out most profits, rather than reinvest for growth?
- Will the business need cash investment over the long term?
- Is the intended payoff a sale or public offering?
- Does the business carry real liability exposure (products, premises, professional advice)?
- Are any owners non-US citizens, or is a large share of revenue from outside the US?
- Will passive income (interest, dividends, rents, royalties) exceed a quarter of revenue?
- Are you willing to keep minutes, hold stockholder and board meetings and elect officers?
- Do you want the business to outlive you or survive a co-owner's exit?
- Do you need life, disability and medical premiums to be fully deductible?

**Partnership agreement checklist** (pp. 67–68) — capital contributions; profit/loss allocation;
salaries and draws; management responsibilities; withdrawal/retirement/disability/death;
dissolution and liquidation.

**S corporation eligibility checklist** (p. 73) — one class of stock; domestic, US-citizen owners,
no more than 80% non-US revenue; 35 or fewer stockholders; no more than 25% passive revenue;
individuals/estates/certain trusts only.

**Sole proprietor launch checklist** (p. 65) — separate business bank account; accounting software
with Schedule C categories; receipt capture; name search and DBA filing; local licences (health
etc.); no Inc./Corp./Company/Co. in the name.

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Mat Café (Matthew and Mathilde)** — 50/50 partnership earned $140,000; Matthew reports $70,000,
  Mathilde $90,000 ($70,000 plus $20,000 from a part-time job) — a partnership is a conduit to
  personal returns; and if Matthew harms a customer, the customer can sue Mathilde too (joint and
  several liability).
- **Deepwater Horizon (BP, Halliburton, Transocean, 2010)** — even had damages exceeded the
  companies' net worth, courts could not pursue individual shareholders — the corporate veil.
- **Amalgamated Hat Rack / Angus McDuff** — $647,500 pre-tax profit, a little over 46% ($300,000)
  in state and federal corporate tax, $347,500 after tax; a $10,000 dividend to founder-CEO McDuff
  is taxed again on his personal return — double taxation (minor exception: dividends a corporation
  receives from other corporations).
- **Surfside Management Consulting** — trading under a name other than your own triggers a DBA
  filing and a name-availability check.
- **Law firms** — commonly organized as LLCs: limited liability, flow-through tax, flexible profit split.
- **VC and real-estate partnerships** — 99% of profits to LPs until capital is returned, then 30/70
  — profit allocation in a partnership is a design choice, not a formula.

## Pitfalls
- Optimising for tax alone and giving up liability protection the business actually needs.
- Forgetting that flow-through income is taxed whether or not it is distributed — a growth-stage
  tax bill with no cash to pay it.
- Paying all corporate profit out as salary to dodge double taxation; the IRS polices reasonable
  compensation.
- Running a partnership on a handshake: no agreement means dissolution on any exit and no rules for
  disputes, draws or contributions.
- Underestimating joint and several liability — one partner can endanger the others without their
  knowledge or consent.
- Electing S corp status and then courting venture capital.
- Corporation owners tripping over the formalities (minutes, meetings, resolutions) that LLCs avoid.
- Officers assuming the veil covers them personally for unpaid withholding taxes.
- Mixing household and business money, or losing receipts, in a sole proprietorship.
- Using Inc., Corp., Company or Co. in an unincorporated business name.
- A fast-track venture burning time and focus on successive form conversions instead of
  incorporating once.
- Limited partners expecting to deduct losses beyond their capital at risk.
- Leaving a sole proprietorship's continuity unplanned — the entity dies with the owner.

## Key terms
`sole proprietorship` — a business owned by one individual; owner and business are one for tax and liability.
`Schedule C` — the US personal-return schedule on which a proprietor reports business income and expenses.
`general partnership` — two or more owners; treated like a proprietorship for tax and liability.
`jointly and severally liable` — each partner can be pursued for the whole claim, regardless of fault or share.
`limited partnership` — hybrid with managing general partner(s) (unlimited liability) and passive limited partners (liability capped at contribution).
`C corporation` — state-chartered entity treated as a person in law; unlimited shareholders; taxed at the entity level.
`corporate veil` — the shield limiting shareholder liability to their investment; pierced only for fraud.
`double taxation` — profits taxed at the corporate level and again as dividends on shareholders' returns.
`S corporation` — closely held corporation electing partnership-style flow-through tax while keeping limited liability.
`limited-liability company (LLC)` — hybrid giving S-corp-level liability protection with proprietorship/partnership flow-through tax and no owner-count limits.
`member` — an owner of an LLC (neither proprietor, partner nor shareholder).
`operating agreement` — the LLC's equivalent of a partnership agreement; may allocate profits and losses in any ratio.
`flow-through (conduit) entity` — one whose income is taxed on the owners' returns, not the entity's, whether or not cash is distributed.
`DBA / fictitious name certificate` — local filing needed to trade under a name other than your own.
`reasonable compensation` — IRS rules limiting how much corporate profit can be paid to owner-employees as deductible salary.
`dilution` — the reduction in a founder's ownership share and control with every share sold.

## Summing up (book)
The chapter's summing up is Table 4-1 (above), condensed:
- Sole proprietorship — simplest, one tax level; full personal liability, no outside equity.
- General partnership — adds talent and capital, one tax level; full liability, capital limited to
  partners, dissolves on exit unless the agreement says otherwise.
- Limited partnership — limited liability for investors, one tax level; complex to set up.
- C corporation — attracts equity, VC-preferred, deductible benefits, limited liability; complex
  and double-taxed.
- S corporation — one tax level with limited liability; complex, capped shareholders, no VCs.
- LLC — simpler than a corporation, limited liability, one tax level, unlimited members; cannot
  attract outside equity.
