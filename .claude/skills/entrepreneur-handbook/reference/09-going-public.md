# Ch 9 — Going Public
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 9, book pages 149–168.

## Purpose
Should a growing company raise money through an IPO, is it even a candidate, what does the
deal involve, and what is the alternative? Load when a user asks about "going public", "IPO",
"listing", "underwriters", "private placement", or when a late-stage business (~$100M revenue,
profitable quarters) is weighing its next financing. Fewer than 5% of founders will ever need
this directly (p. 149) — for everyone else it sets expectations and points to alternatives.

## Core ideas
- An IPO swaps paper ownership shares for hard cash from individual and institutional investors
  (pension funds, mutual funds). It signals the company has "made it" and makes it accountable
  to a far wider set of stakeholders, analysts and regulators (p. 149).
- Conventional bar: **~$100M annualized revenue plus several consecutive profitable quarters**.
  Only a tiny fraction of startups get there; "an IPO is a pipe dream for all but a few".
- Post-WWII norm: prove a record of sales and earnings first. Exception: proprietary technology
  plus a tested management team. The dot-com boom let "clever idea" companies float; many
  failed and the hangover still shapes the process (p. 153).
- Ability to launch = **investor mood and expectations × ability to meet regulatory requirements**.
- Public status is a mixed blessing: cash, liquidity and stability versus expense, management
  time, scrutiny, dilution and pressure for short-term results.
- Successful IPO companies **act like public companies long before the deal** (Ernst & Young).
- Timing is a judgement: too soon and you under-exploit potential; too late and you miss the
  bullish window. VC-backed companies now stay private longer (Figure 9-1), partly because
  Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) made being public more expensive.
- You cannot do this in-house: an investment bank (an agent and deal-maker, not a deposit-taking
  lender) gets you through registration and distributes the shares for 6–10% of the offering price.
- Insiders do not get full liquidity on IPO day — US rules and lockup agreements restrict
  insider sales.
- If an IPO is infeasible or unwanted, a **private placement** (or selling the company — covered
  in a later chapter) brings large capital without SEC registration or public scrutiny.
- Non-US note: written for US SEC rules; a UK founder would look at LSE Main Market or AIM (FCA
  prospectus rules, a Nominated Adviser on AIM), a Nigerian one at NGX / SEC Nigeria — same
  logic, local rulebook.

## Frameworks & tables
**Table — Pros and cons of going public (pp. 150–152)**

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Personal wealth for founders, key employees and early investors — and (restricted) liquidity for it | **IPO expense**: legal, accounting, printing, advisory, SEC and state filing fees, exchange fees, underwriter commission, process upgrades — expect **~$2M out of pocket, up to $100M** on large deals |
| Pay off costly interest-bearing debt | **Management time and attention**: months of preparation, the road show, then ongoing investor and analyst inquiries; may need an investor relations manager |
| Financial capacity to develop new products and the marketing to sell them | **Public scrutiny**: results and executive pay are public; the 10-K hands competitors key suppliers, product-development plans and strategy |
| Improved debt-to-equity ratio → debt on better terms later | **Loss of control**: founder and managers usually lose a major portion of ownership; institutions hold blocks, thousands of small holders own the rest |
| Cash plus marketable stock as currency for strategic acquisitions | **Pressure for short-term gain**: analysts expect predictable year-to-year earnings; long-term moves get deferred |
| Financial stability that attracts talent, suppliers and joint-venture partners | |
| Opens the door to future stock and bond rounds | |

**The IPO candidate profile — seven characteristics (pp. 153–154)**
1. **Reasonable deal size** — given the cost, there is little point seeking less than **$10M**, and you need a solid plan for using it.
2. **Evidence of growth** — double-digit sales growth with earnings growing faster (or all signs pointing to substantial profitability ahead); enough to support a **P/E multiple above the historical S&P 500 or Russell 2000**.
3. **Outstanding products or services that are difficult to copy.**
4. **A credible CEO** who can communicate the vision to cautious outsiders.
5. **At least three years of audited financial statements** (a "look-back" audit works if records are solid).
6. **High-quality employees.**
7. **A logical growth strategy and a predictable revenue stream.**

**Hallmarks of "already acting public" (Ernst & Young study of successful IPOs, p. 154)** —
pre-IPO improvements in: employee incentive programs; strategic planning; internal controls;
financial accounting and reporting; executive compensation; investor relations policies.
Investors were buying a firm that already looked professionally managed.

**Figure 9-1 — Waiting game (p. 156)**: median time from founding to IPO for VC-backed companies
(y-axis 0–8 years, 1995–2015) climbs steadily across the two decades; higher Sarbanes-Oxley
requirements are partly responsible. Reference points from the box: Netscape 16 months; Google
~6 years; Facebook 8 years; Pure Storage 6 years and six private rounds.

**What the investment banker does for its 6–10% (pp. 164–165)**
1. **Gets the regulatory act together** — steers the stringent hurdles of issuing securities, including developing the prospectus (preliminary form = the *red herring*: company, business, finances, use of proceeds).
2. **Sets the price** — no market-clearing price exists yet; the company wants the highest price the market will bear, investors expect a bargain versus seasoned securities; the banker mediates.
3. **Arranges distribution** — assembles a syndicate of broker-dealers, typically buys the shares from the issuer at a set price, marks them up, and sells through its own and partners' channels to pension funds, mutual funds and individuals — i.e. it *underwrites* the selling risk.

**Pricing inputs at step 6 (p. 160)**: the range printed in the prospectus (e.g. $15–$20);
observed demand; prices comparable companies got in recent IPOs; the company's projected
earnings; a deliberate discount below expected after-market trading so first buyers have a
better-than-even chance of a gain. "The answer is based as much on art as on science."

**Private placement instruments (pp. 166–167)**: senior debt; subordinated debt; asset-backed
debt; equity; negotiated hybrids — e.g. below-market-rate debt (good for the company) with
warrants attached (good for the investor). Buyers are usually sophisticated institutions
(insurers, pension funds, endowments) seeking higher returns than public markets.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Revenue bar**: ~$100M annualized revenue and several consecutive profitable quarters before an IPO is necessary or feasible.
- **Minimum raise**: do not go public for less than $10M — the fixed costs swamp the proceeds.
- **Cost**: budget ~$2M out of pocket; large deals reach $100M. Banker fee 6–10% of offering price.
- **Timeline**: 4–5 months from underwriter selection to close. Pure Storage's readiness work (board, CFO, systems, bank relationship) ran roughly two years before that.
- **Audited history**: three years minimum, or commission a look-back audit now.
- **Growth test**: double-digit sales growth, earnings growing faster; the story must justify a P/E above S&P 500 / Russell 2000 history.
- **Banker selection**: invite 3–5 candidates to present; pick on industry fit, distribution and analyst coverage, not just valuation flattery.
- **Price**: the issuer can halt the offering if it dislikes the final price — that veto is your leverage.
- **Quiet period**: filing the S-1 with the SEC triggers strict limits on public statements; competitors may spread "fear, uncertainty and doubt" while you cannot answer. Time the filing for when rivals are distracted if you can.
- **Lockups**: vested insiders cannot sell for several months after the IPO; paper wealth can evaporate before it is realised (Akamai, 1999–2000).
- **Window**: markets cool quickly; complete every step so you can "hit while the IPO window is open". At least five companies planned around Pure's IPO delayed or pulled.
- **Valuation trap**: nobody wants to go public below the last private round. If the next private round would set a price the IPO could not match, IPO instead. A CEO's job is a fair deal with investors who will be good advisers, not the maximum valuation every round (Dietzen).
- **Stay private longer when**: you want to keep control and fend off M&A (harder as a small public company); Sarbanes-Oxley costs bite; late-stage mutual-fund money (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price) is available and brings future public-market holders as advisers.
- **Go sooner when**: customers prefer to buy from public companies (visible financials, higher scrutiny = trust); employees and investors need liquidity; growth needs capital you cannot raise privately.
- **Employee retention**: offer selective liquidity in private rounds (a set % of vested shares sold to institutions) to relieve pressure for an early IPO; after the IPO, re-sell the vision so newly wealthy staff stay.
- **Control**: adopt a two-class share structure before filing so founders and management can resist a hostile buyer.
- **Private placement**: exempt from SEC registration (some states have requirements), so sizeable capital without the time, expense or scrutiny of a public offering — often the better route.
- **Always get an experienced investment banker**, whichever route you take; expensive, but they hold the technical expertise and investor contacts (box, p. 165).

## Process / steps
**The eight steps of an IPO (pp. 158–161)** — some sequential, some parallel:
1. **Select an underwriter** — the investment bank handles the details with management; larger deals have one lead underwriter plus one or more comanagers.
2. **Prepare the registration statement for the SEC** — required by federal law; discloses past results, company information and intended use of proceeds.
3. **Conduct due diligence** — verify every fact and risk statement in the registration statement and that nothing relevant is omitted; the responsibility of those who prepare and sign it.
4. **Print and distribute the prospectus** — the preliminary prospectus (red herring) is part of the registration statement and goes to prospective investors to build interest.
5. **Prepare and conduct a road show** — meetings in major cities where potential investors grill the CEO and/or CFO on the company and the offering.
6. **Agree the final price and number of shares** — the most important step (see pricing inputs above); the issuer can put the brakes on if the price is wrong.
7. **Commence trading** — once price is set and regulatory loose ends are tied, shares trade on the chosen exchange.
8. **Close the purchase and sale** — certificates delivered to shareholders; underwriter delivers proceeds less fees and expenses; the company has its money.
Afterwards the underwriter supports the stock with ongoing research for investors. Beyond these
steps sit publicity restrictions before, during and after filing, and lockup agreements — the
rules are "many and arcane", so professional help is essential (p. 161).

**Getting ready — Pure Storage's preparation sequence (pp. 157–158)**
1. Expand the board with large-company operating experience; recruit a public-company finance executive to chair the audit committee (Mark Garrett, Adobe CFO).
2. Build an investment-bank relationship — ideally the bank that led the late private rounds (Allen & Company).
3. Create a two-class stock structure to protect founder/management control.
4. Hire a CFO with public-company experience (Tim Riitters, ex-Google) and install systems for financial visibility.
5. When another private round adds no advantage (Pure: >$3B valuation in 2014), start IPO planning.
6. Accept that filing the S-1 opens the quiet period and makes you vulnerable — pick the moment.

**Inside the deal — the IPO-day view (Annie Bourne, Akamai, pp. 162–164)**: bankers and lawyers
write the business-and-risk document → build a financial model of existing and expected revenue
→ file with regulators, wait for comments, respond → two-week road show across Europe and the US
→ bankers line up buyers for the "book" of shares → pricing decided "in a seemingly unscientific
frenzy in a paneled room on Wall Street" → ticker goes live; staff watch bid/ask numbers change;
vested employees locked up for months.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Go / no-go: is an IPO right for us?** (pp. 150–152)
- Do we need more than $10M, and do we have a solid plan for using it?
- Which pros apply — debt payoff, product and marketing capacity, cheaper future debt, acquisition currency, stability for talent/suppliers/partners, future rounds?
- Can we absorb ~$2M+ in costs and 6–10% underwriting fees?
- Are we ready for financial results, executive pay, suppliers, product plans and strategy to be public via the 10-K?
- Can the founders accept losing a major portion of ownership? Have we structured share classes for control?
- Can the CEO and CFO give months to preparation and the road show, then ongoing investor time? Who will run investor relations?
- Can we resist sacrificing long-term moves to hit predictable quarterly earnings?

**IPO candidate checklist** (yes/no; all seven plus the E&Y hallmarks should be yes)
- ~$100M annualized revenue and several consecutive profitable quarters
- Double-digit sales growth; earnings growing faster or clearly imminent
- Growth story supports a P/E above S&P 500 / Russell 2000 history
- Products or services that are outstanding and hard to copy
- A CEO who can communicate the vision to cautious outsiders
- Three years of audited statements, or a look-back audit is feasible
- High-quality employees
- A logical growth strategy and a predictable revenue stream
- Already acting public: employee incentives, strategic planning, internal controls, financial reporting, executive compensation, investor relations policy

**Timing questions (from the "When to go public" box, pp. 155–159)**
- Are we big enough now? Would waiting increase or decrease value?
- Can we carry Sarbanes-Oxley compliance costs?
- Do we want to stay independent long term — could we fend off M&A as a small public company?
- Are customers asking for the transparency and security of a public supplier?
- Would another private round set a valuation the IPO could not match?
- Do employees and investors need liquidity — can selective liquidity in private rounds buy time?
- Is the IPO window open now, and what if it cools while we prepare?
- Who could attack us with FUD during the quiet period — are they distracted?
- Do we have the board, audit chair, CFO, systems, bank relationship and share structure in place?

**Due-diligence questions (step 3, p. 160)**
- Is the company using an unorthodox accounting convention?
- Is it involved in any current lawsuits?
- Have patents been granted, or are patents pending?
- Is every fact and risk statement in the registration statement accurate, with nothing relevant omitted?

**Choosing an investment banker (p. 165; 3–5 candidates present)**
- Good fit with our industry?
- The sales and distribution capabilities we need?
- Good analyst coverage for us once public?
- Their take on the current market?
- What they think our valuation should be?
- Do they confirm we are ready to go public?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **eBay / Pierre Omidyar (1998)** — held ~44M common shares with no market, rented house, old Jetta; next day they traded on NASDAQ and climbed past $4B — an IPO converts paper into wealth, but the process has real challenges.
- **Pure Storage / Scott Dietzen (Oct 2015, NYSE)** — six years old, six private rounds, ~1,200 employees, ~$500M annualized revenue; could have listed in 2013 but waited; IPO at $17 a share, just over $3B valuation, still reporting losses — waiting worked, and luck (Dell buying EMC, HP splitting) muted competitor FUD during the quiet period.
- **Google (~6 yrs), Facebook (8 yrs), Netscape (16 months)** — the first two staying private longer worked out so well it changed the conventional wisdom from "IPO as soon as you can" to "many choose to wait".
- **Akamai (1999)** — priced at $26, opened at $114.50, closed at $145.19 on day one; most staff not yet vested and all locked up; the bust months later took much of the paper wealth away — day-one price is not money.
- **Danny Lewin (Akamai cofounder, 29, >$1B on paper)** — pulled everyone out of the IPO party into a conference room to whiteboard the future — the same idea that makes investors buy must make employees stay.
- **Dot-com boom** — companies with nothing but a clever idea sold public shares, then failed to prove their worth — the reason investors now demand a working "money-making machine".
- **Fidelity / T. Rowe Price** — mutual funds that missed Facebook-style growth now do pre-IPO private placements — a new way to stay private longer and build public-market relationships early.
- **At least five companies near Pure's IPO date** — delayed or pulled their offerings as the market cooled — windows close.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the IPO as a goal in itself rather than one financing option among several.
- Going too early (under-exploiting potential, exposed to M&A) or too late (missing the window).
- Underestimating the out-of-pocket cost, the 6–10% fee and the months of CEO/CFO time.
- Forgetting that the 10-K publishes suppliers, product plans and strategy to competitors.
- Taking a private round at a valuation the IPO market will not match.
- Filing the S-1 without a plan for the quiet period — rivals spread FUD while you are gagged.
- Chasing the highest valuation instead of fair terms with investors who will be good advisers.
- Letting newly rich employees drift away after the IPO; not re-selling the vision.
- Mistaking day-one paper wealth for realised wealth while locked up.
- Sacrificing long-term investment to protect predictable quarterly earnings.
- Attempting the deal without an experienced investment banker.

## Key terms
`IPO (initial public offering)` — first sale of a company's shares to the public, exchanging ownership for cash.
`underwriter` — the investment bank that manages the offering, prices it and takes on the risk of selling the shares; `comanagers` assist on larger deals.
`investment bank` — agent and deal-maker for capital seekers (not a deposit-and-loan bank); fee 6–10% of offering price.
`registration statement (Form S-1)` — federally required SEC filing disclosing past results, company information and use of proceeds.
`due diligence` — verifying that the registration statement's facts and risk statements are accurate and complete.
`prospectus` / `red herring` — the disclosure document for investors; the preliminary version sent to build interest.
`road show` — investor meetings in major cities where the CEO/CFO are grilled on the offering.
`book` — the bank's ledger of investor orders for the shares.
`quiet period` — post-S-1 window with strict limits on public statements.
`lockup agreement` — restriction stopping insiders selling shares for several months after the IPO.
`10-K` — the annual public filing that discloses results, pay, suppliers, plans and strategy.
`P/E ratio` — price-earnings multiple; the growth story must justify one above the S&P 500 / Russell 2000.
`Sarbanes-Oxley` — 2002 US act raising public-company controls and compliance costs.
`two-class stock` — share structure giving founders/management voting control against hostile buyers.
`syndicate` — group of broker-dealers assembled by the underwriter to distribute shares.
`bid / ask` — what a buyer will pay and what a seller will accept for a share.
`look-back audit` — retroactive audit of prior years built from existing records.
`investor relations manager` — role created to handle investor and analyst inquiries post-IPO.
`private placement` — sale of stock (or debt) to one or a few private investors, exempt from SEC registration.
`warrant` — right to buy common shares at a stated price for a stated period, usually set above current valuation.

## Summing up (book)
- An IPO is a pipe dream for all but a few corporations.
- It brings much-needed cash and lets owners liquidate and diversify their wealth.
- Downside: expense, absorbed management time, ownership dilution, ongoing public scrutiny and pressure for short-term gains.
- Choose the time carefully, weighing market conditions and your interest in keeping control.
- Do not consider an IPO without: a CEO who communicates, deal size of $10M+, double-digit revenue and earnings growth (or earnings clearly to follow), outstanding hard-to-copy products, quality employees, a logical growth strategy.
- Eight US steps: select underwriter → registration statement to SEC → due diligence → red herring → road show → price and share count → trading → close.
- An investment bank supplies two necessities: technical knowledge to clear registration and a sales network to distribute the shares.
- A private placement is often a good alternative to an IPO.
