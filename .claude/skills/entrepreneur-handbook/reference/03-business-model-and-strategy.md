# Ch 3 — Building Your Business Model and Strategy
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 3, book pages 41–62.

## Purpose
Once early findings and experiments suggest a real opportunity, how do you solidify (a) a
**business model** — who your customers are and how you profitably serve them — and (b) a
**strategy** — how you do better than competitors? Load when a user asks "what's my business
model", "how will I make money", "how am I different", "how will customers find me", "should I
join an incubator/accelerator", "when should I scale", or when building the model/strategy
module of the launch journey.

## Core ideas
- **Model ≠ strategy.** A business model identifies customers and describes how you profitably
  address their needs; strategy is how you beat competitors. You need both (p42).
- Every founder must have concise answers to **five questions**: create value for customers,
  make a profit for us and investors, differentiate, defend assets/position, be discovered (p41).
- A business model is a variation of the value chain: **making** (design, purchasing,
  manufacturing) + **selling** (finding/reaching customers, transacting, delivering) (Magretta, p42).
- Two tests: the **narrative test** (does the model tell a logical story?) and the **numbers
  test** (is the pro forma income statement profitable with reasonable projections?) (p43).
- Many model elements are **hypotheses, not decisions** (Osterwalder's canvas). Test the ones
  you're unsure of before building the product or approaching investors (p46–47).
- **Marketing belongs at the centre of the model from day one**, not downstream. If you can't
  reach customers or the product doesn't fit them, nothing else in the model works (p48).
- Competitive advantage lives in **differences** (Henderson, Porter). But different alone isn't
  enough — the difference must be **perceived as valuable** by customers (p50, 52).
- Strategy is a **system of mutually reinforcing activities**, not a collection of parts (p54).
- Platform businesses run on **demand-side economies of scale (network effects)**; the classic
  five-forces "moat" logic treats external forces as depletive, but for platforms they can be
  accretive (p55–56).
- **Every startup is an experiment.** Expect initial strategy to miss; recognise bad news and
  pivot quickly; then be a master of incrementalism (p57–58).
- There is no sustainable competitive advantage any more (McGrath): build to exploit
  **transient** advantages through deliberate, continuous change (p60).
- Some argue a strong strategy helps more than an excess of lean-startup validation tests (p50).

## Frameworks & tables
**The five questions every founder must answer (p41)**
1. How will the business create value for customers?
2. How will it make a profit for us and our investors?
3. How will it differentiate itself from competitors?
4. How will it defend its assets and position from competitors?
5. How will it be discovered?

**Magretta's two-part value chain and two tests (p42–43)**
| Part | Activities |
|---|---|
| Making | Designing, purchasing raw materials, manufacturing, etc. |
| Selling | Finding and reaching customers, transacting a sale, distributing/delivering |

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Narrative test | Does the model tell a logical, sensible story? |
| Numbers test | On a pro forma income statement with reasonable revenue/expense projections, is it profitable? |

**Hamermesh & Marshall — four groups of business-model decisions (p43)**
| Group | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue sources | Where the money comes from | Sales, service fees, advertising |
| Cost drivers | What creates cost | Labour, goods purchased for resale, energy |
| Investment size | Measurable capital to launch, plus working capital to keep operating | — |
| Critical success factors | What must go right | Sustained new-product rollout; reaching critical mass within a set time |

**Table 3-1 — Business model analogies** (adapted from Mark W. Johnson, *Seizing the White
Space*, p45). Prompt: how might each be applied to the problem you're solving?

| Analogy | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affinity club | Pay royalties to a large organisation for the right to sell exclusively to its members | MBNA |
| Brokerage | Bring buyers and sellers together; charge a fee per transaction | Century 21, Orbitz |
| Bundling | Package related goods and services together | Fast-food value meals; iPod + iTunes |
| Cell phone | Charge different rates for discrete service levels | Sprint, Better Place |
| Crowdsourcing | Users contribute content free in exchange for access to others' content | Wikipedia, YouTube |
| Disintermediation | Sell direct, sidestepping intermediaries | Dell, WebMD |
| Fractionalization | Sell partial use of something | NetJets, time-shares |
| Freemium | Basic service free; charge for premium | LinkedIn |
| Leasing | Rent rather than sell high-margin, high-priced products | Cars, MachineryLink |
| Low-touch | Lower prices by decreasing service | Walmart, IKEA |
| Negative operating cycle | Lower prices by getting paid before delivering | Amazon |
| Pay as you go | Charge for actual metered usage | Electric companies |
| Razor/blades | Sell the companion product below cost to drive volume of the consumable | Printers and ink |
| Reverse razor/blades | Sell the low-margin item below cost to drive the high-margin companion | Kindle; iPod/iTunes |
| Reverse auction | Set a ceiling price; participants bid as the price drops | Elance.com |
| Product to service | Sell the service the product performs, not the product | Zipcar |
| Standardization | Standardise a previously personalised service to cut cost | MinuteClinic |
| Subscription | Charge a recurring fee for a service | Netflix |
| User communities | Grant network access; charge membership fees and advertising | Angie's List |

**Porter — three sources of strategic position (p51–52)**
| Position | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Variety-based | Choose a narrow subset of the industry's offerings; win by delivering faster, better or cheaper | Starbucks — premium coffee, convenient outlets, no breakfast/sandwiches at launch |
| Need-based | Serve all or most needs of an identifiable customer set (price-sensitive, high-touch, or customised) | USAA — active/retired military officers and families; handles transfers and remote postings |
| Access-based | Position on access to customers (location, channel) | Discount chain only in low-income neighbourhoods; Cracker Barrel along US expressways with a route trip-planner |

Generic bases of strategy: **low-cost leadership, technical differentiation, focus** (p51).

**Porter's five forces of competition (p52)** — threat of new entrants; threat of substitute
products/services; rivalry among existing competitors; bargaining power of suppliers; bargaining
power of buyers. (The printed text repeats "suppliers"; buyers is the intended fifth force.)

**Osborne's 4 S's (p55)** — "structure follows strategy, and staffing follows structure, and
you hold the strategy together with systems" (Alfred E. Osborne Jr., UCLA).

**Box — Network effects and strategy** (Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary, HBR April 2016, p56)
| | Supply-side economies (pipelines) | Demand-side economies (platforms) |
|---|---|---|
| Source of market power | Control resources, ruthless efficiency, fend off the five forces | Network effects: bigger network → better supply/demand matches, richer matching data → higher value per transaction |
| Goal of strategy | Build a moat that channels competition elsewhere | Grow participation; external forces are accretive, adding value |
| Outcome | Defended position | Virtuous loop that produces monopolies |
Evidence: Alibaba >75% of Chinese e-commerce transactions; Google 82% of mobile OS and 94% of
mobile searches; Facebook the dominant social platform. Platform margins: eBay gross margin 70%,
Etsy 60% (p55).

**Incubator vs accelerator (p47–48)**
| | Incubator | Accelerator |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Less structured, less time-bound | Time-limited cohort program |
| Money | Nonprofit or charges rent for shared coworking space | Funding in exchange for equity (see ch 6) |
| Stage | Any; some specialise in later-phase growth | Almost exclusively early-stage |
| Selectivity | Lower | More competitive, especially strong programs |
| Sponsor | Independent, big firm, university, government arm, nonprofit | Independent programs |
Both provide space plus coaching, mentoring, networking, funding and education to test business
models and assumptions quickly.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- If the model fails the narrative test **or** the numbers test, fix it before doing anything else.
- Answer the four model groups unambiguously (revenue, costs, investment, success factors)
  **before approaching any investor** (p46).
- Know which costs are **fixed** and which **vary with sales volume**; calculate launch capital
  and operating capital separately.
- For each essential factor you're unsure about, find a way to test it **before building the
  offering or pitching** — confidence in success rises sharply (p47).
- For each hypothesis ask what could change it (e.g. no competitors today, competitors tomorrow)
  → that is your risk list and mitigation plan.
- App discovery: the app stores are the only scale channel (Apple 2.2M+ apps, Google Play
  2.8M+); one in four apps is found via search; app-store optimisation and Facebook/Instagram
  ads are **table stakes** — competitors do them too. Add creative reach, endorsements and
  direct customer interaction outside those ecosystems (p48–49).
- Two competitors cannot coexist doing business the same way; differentiate on price, product
  mix, ambiance or another axis, or one perishes (Henderson, p50).
- "Different" must be valued: lower cost, convenience, reliability, faster delivery, aesthetic
  appeal, etc. Ask what value the strategy provides and whether you can deliver it (p52).
- Resources constrain strategy choice: don't pick a strategy your capital and know-how cannot
  execute — a startup with few people and assets must be honest here (p53).
- Prioritise threats/opportunities first (Christensen's "driving forces"), then address each
  in broad strokes (p53).
- Generate **many alternatives**; the best parts of two strategies often combine into a stronger
  third. Check every fact, question every assumption, fetch missing information, and vet the
  leading choices with the wisest heads you know to avoid groupthink (p54).
- Alignment test: every employee at every level (1) understands the strategy and (2) knows
  their role in making it work. Also align marketing (right customers), compensation/bonuses
  (strategy-advancing behaviour) and physical assets (p55).
- A startup's clean slate is an advantage: hire, equip and structure around the strategy (p55).
- **Don't scale a network business too early.** VRBO preceded Airbnb, eBay preceded Alibaba in
  China; early scaling locks in an untested model and forfeits revenue, margin or customers
  (Hagiu, p57).
- **When to scale** (Hoffman): you have data, understand the competition and have ironed out
  product-market fit. Typical transition: 10–100 → 100–1,000 employees; 100K–1M → 1–10M users;
  revenue > $10M (p57).
- If something works, **do more of it**; use a niche win to enter the next niche, adapting the
  product as needed (p58).
- Expect copycats: imagine a rival raising $10M from a VC while you're assembling round two
  (p59). Expect incumbents: what if Amazon or Facebook adds your idea as a feature? (p59)
- Big tech may pretend to acquire you "just to screw up their business for 18 months"
  (Andreessen, p59–60).
- Even a winning strategy has an end date: plan ahead for abandoning it (McGrath, p60).
- Test and verify each assumption before launch; the market is the ultimate test. Explain the
  model and strategy to as many trusted, experienced people as possible (p60).
- Worked breakeven (Cesar): crew of five, 8,000 service-hours/year (~40 weeks); everything
  above breakeven is profit (p44).
- Asset-light pricing (Airbnb): no owned property → no big capital to scale → prices ~30% below
  hotels; hosts carry maintenance, so risk and operating cost are lower (p47).

## Process / steps
**Solidifying the business model (p41–49)**
1. Write concise answers to the five questions.
2. Describe the concept in the four groups (revenue sources, cost drivers, investment size,
   critical success factors); split fixed vs variable costs; size launch and operating capital.
3. Try each Table 3-1 analogy against your problem; adapt the one(s) that fit.
4. Run the narrative test and the numbers test (build the pro forma income statement).
5. Mark which elements are decisions and which are hypotheses; test uncertain factors before
   building or pitching (an incubator/accelerator can speed this).
6. Put the discovery/marketing plan inside the model, with unique features and approaches for
   reaching customers (not just table stakes).

**Six steps for formulating strategy (p52–55)** — built around the five forces
1. **Look outside** to identify threats and opportunities (new entrants, demographic shifts,
   suppliers cutting you off, substitutes, technology that obsoletes your solution or the
   customer's problem, macro trends that reduce ability to pay, a rival with same quality at
   much lower price or a much better product at the same price; opportunities such as
   new-to-the-world technology or an unserved market).
2. **Look inside** at resources, capabilities and practices — they constrain the strategy.
3. **Consider strategies** for addressing threats and opportunities (prioritise, create
   alternatives, check facts, fill information gaps, vet externally).
4. **Build fit** among strategy-supporting activities so they reinforce each other and lock out
   imitators (Southwest: rapid gate turnaround → frequent departures + aircraft utilisation →
   low-cost/high-convenience; enabled by motivated gate/ground crews, no meals, no interline
   baggage transfers).
5. **Create alignment** between people, activities, marketing, compensation, assets and the strategy.
6. **Be prepared to implement** — organise around the strategy (4 S's). Note: the chapter's
   Summing up lists only steps 1–5; step 6 appears in the body.

**Responding to a disappointing strategy (p57–58)**
1. Recognise the bad news — admit the mistake.
2. Search energetically for what went wrong.
3. Revise the strategy (pivot) and get back into the game quickly.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Business model check (p46–47)**
- How would you describe the concept in terms of revenue sources, cost drivers, investment
  size and critical success factors?
- Have you nailed down your revenue sources and the factors that drive costs?
- Which costs are fixed and which vary with sales volume?
- Have you calculated the capital needed to launch and to operate?
- What factors are essential for success?
- Of those essential factors, which are you unsure about? How can you test them before
  building the offering or approaching investors?
- What might change about your hypotheses (e.g. competitors appearing later)?
- Which Table 3-1 analogies could apply to the problem you're solving?

**Discovery plan questions (p48–49)**
- How will customers find out about you? Do you know them well enough to reach them?
- If you depend on others for distribution (e.g. app stores), how do you get discovered there?
- Beyond table stakes (ASO, social ads), what creative reach, endorsements or direct
  interactions will connect you with customers?

**Strategy check (p52)**
- What is your strategy for gaining competitive advantage?
- Will it differentiate your company in ways that attract customers from rivals?
- Will it draw new customers into the market?
- Will it give you a tangible advantage?
- What value does your strategy aim to provide? Can it deliver?

**Step 1 — outside questions (p53)**
- What is the economic environment we must operate in? How is it changing?
- What opportunities are there for profitable action?
- What are the risks associated with these opportunities?

**Step 2 — inside questions (p53)**
- What are our competencies as an organisation? How do they give us an advantage?
- Which resources support or constrain our actions?

**Step 3 — strategy-development checklist (p54)**
- Create many alternatives (combine the best parts of two into a third).
- Check all facts; question all assumptions.
- Identify what information is missing; then get it.
- Vet the leading choices with the wisest heads you know (avoid groupthink).

**Step 5 — alignment checklist (p55)**
- Does every employee understand the strategy and their role in it?
- Is marketing focused on the customers the strategy defines?
- Do compensation and bonuses reward strategy-advancing behaviour and performance?
- Are physical assets deployed toward the organisation's highest goals?

**Competition-readiness questions (p58–60)**
- What if a copycat raised $10M from a VC while you were still preparing round two?
- What if Amazon or Facebook added your idea as a feature?
- What if a large company feigned an acquisition to distract you for 18 months?
- (Cesar's set) What happens when the next technology shift arrives (self-driving cars)? Will
  your team have the capabilities? What if a web platform inserts itself between you and your
  customers? What if underlying demand declines (car ownership falling with ride-share)?
- When your current advantage expires, what will replace it?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Cesar's Electric Car Care Center** (box, p44; strategy box, p58–59) — repair/maintenance of
  electric cars after warranty; four-part blueprint (local ads + owner mini-seminars, in-house
  diagnostics for all makes, regional parts pipeline, outsourced body shop and A/C); breakeven
  at 5 staff / 8,000 hours; differentiated as the only EV specialist in town; aligns tools,
  trained mechanics, teaching-capable staff, seminars and social media; plans for self-driving
  cars, platform intermediaries and falling car ownership — model + strategy + foresight.
- **Airbnb** (box, p46–47) — more rooms than InterContinental or Hilton; 19.5% of NYC room
  supply and 5.4% across 192 countries in 2016 (from 3.6% in 2015); owns no property, takes a
  percentage of rent, prices ~30% below hotels — a new model is the interface between what
  technology enables and what the market wants.
- **Eat24** (p49) — rejected by Benchmark, Redpoint, Excel, Insight and Alibaba in a
  GrubHub-dominated market; targeted small family restaurants, knocked on doors, gave free fax
  machines, no charges on tiny orders, absorbed non-payment risk, bought cheap ads on sites
  popular with young men; acquired by Yelp for $134M — creative discovery beats table stakes.
- **Southwest Airlines** (p50, 54) — most profitable North American carrier via low fares,
  frequent point-to-point departures, service; rapid gate turnaround as the hub of a fit system.
- **Toyota Prius** (p50) — targeted buyers wanting green, cheap-to-run or cutting-edge cars, and
  bought a learning lead in hybrid technology.
- **Apple iPod/iTunes** (p50–51) — not first to market (Rio and Cabo failed); won with a
  hardware + software + service ecosystem.
- **Two men's clothing stores** (Henderson, p50) — formal vs leisure can share a block;
  identical stores cannot.
- **Starbucks / USAA / discount chain / Cracker Barrel** (p51–52) — variety, need and
  access-based positions respectively.
- **Alibaba, Google, Facebook** (p56) — network-effect monopolies.
- **VRBO → Airbnb; eBay → Alibaba (China)** (p57) — the eventual winner was not first.
- **Webvan** (p57) — web grocery delivery for affluent, time-starved households; customers
  balked at higher prices; bankrupt 2001 — the experiment can surprise the best planners.

## Pitfalls
- Using "business model" and "strategy" interchangeably, or having one without the other.
- A model that tells a good story but doesn't add up — or adds up but makes no sense.
- Treating assumptions as decisions; not testing the shaky ones before building or pitching.
- Approaching investors with ambiguous answers on revenue, costs, capital or success factors.
- Treating marketing/discovery as a downstream step.
- Relying only on table-stakes discovery (ASO, social ads) that rivals can copy.
- Substituting endless validation tests for a real strategy.
- Being different in ways customers don't value.
- Choosing a strategy your resources and capabilities can't support.
- Considering only one strategy; groupthink; unchecked facts and assumptions.
- Treating strategy as a list of parts instead of a mutually reinforcing system.
- Stopping at strategy without creating alignment or preparing implementation.
- Applying five-forces moat thinking to a platform whose value comes from external participants.
- Scaling a network business before the model and offering are tested.
- Refusing to admit a strategy missed, or responding slowly.
- Chasing "sustainable" competitive advantage instead of planning the next one.
- Keeping the model and strategy to yourself instead of vetting them widely.

## Key terms
`business model` — identifies customers and describes how the business will profitably address their needs; a variation of the value chain.
`strategy` — a plan to differentiate the enterprise and give it a competitive advantage over rivals.
`narrative test` / `numbers test` — Magretta's two tests: does the model tell a logical story, and is it profitable on a pro forma income statement?
`pro forma income statement` — projected revenues and expenses used to model a business before launch.
`business-model canvas` — Osterwalder's tool; frames model elements as hypotheses to test, not decisions.
`incubator` — less structured, less time-bound support program (rent or nonprofit; any stage).
`accelerator` — time-limited cohort program that invests for equity; early-stage; competitive.
`app-store optimization` — understanding store search algorithms to rank higher; table stakes.
`strategic position` — Porter's variety-based, need-based or access-based sources of position.
`five forces` — Porter's competitive forces: new entrants, substitutes, rivalry, supplier power, buyer power.
`fit` — activities combined into a chain that reinforce one another and lock out imitators.
`alignment` — every employee understands the strategy and their role; resources deployed to it.
`4 S's` — structure follows strategy, staffing follows structure, systems hold strategy together.
`platform business` — enables exchanges between producers and consumers rather than producing itself.
`network effects` / `demand-side economies of scale` — value rises with the number of participants; accretive external forces.
`supply-side economies` — market power via resource control and efficiency; moat-building.
`driving forces` — Christensen's term for prioritised threats and opportunities.
`pivot` — a revised strategy adopted quickly after recognising the original missed.
`incrementalism` — doing more of what works; moving niche to niche with what you've learned.
`transient competitive advantage` — McGrath: advantages are temporary; build to create and abandon them continuously.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Five Questions card** — five short answers → one-page summary → the founder must be able
  to answer these for anyone; gate before investor prep.
- **Business Model Builder** — four groups (revenue sources; cost drivers tagged fixed/variable;
  investment size split launch/working capital; critical success factors) + Table 3-1 analogy
  picker → model summary with narrative-test prompt → the book's core definition of a model.
- **Numbers Test calculator** — unit price, volume, variable cost, fixed costs, labour → pro forma
  income statement and breakeven (Cesar: 5 staff, 8,000 hours) → sensitivity to price/margin/cost.
- **Hypothesis tracker** — each model element marked decision vs assumption, confidence, test
  plan, "what could change it" → risk register and pre-pitch readiness score.
- **Positioning picker** — variety/need/access + value type (cost, convenience, reliability,
  speed, aesthetics) + "will customers value it?" → positioning statement and strategy check.
- **Strategy Formulation wizard** — six steps with the book's question lists, threat scan,
  alternatives board, vetting log, alignment checklist → strategy document.
- **Discovery Plan canvas** — channels, table-stakes vs creative tactics, Eat24-style prompts
  → marketing embedded in the model from day one.
- **Scale-readiness gauge** — data/competition/PMF checks + Hoffman thresholds (employees,
  users, >$10M revenue) → scale / hold flag; special note for network-effect businesses.

## Summing up (book)
- A business model describes revenue sources, cost drivers, investment size and success factors.
- Strategy differentiates the enterprise and gives it a competitive advantage.
- Porter: strategic positions come from variety-based, need-based or access-based positioning.
- Strategy formulation: (1) look outside for threats and opportunities; (2) look inside at
  resources, capabilities, practices; (3) consider strategies for the threats/opportunities;
  (4) build fit among strategy-supporting activities; (5) align people and activities with the
  strategy (the body adds a sixth: be prepared to implement).
- A startup is an experiment; if it doesn't produce the desired result, change — quickly.
