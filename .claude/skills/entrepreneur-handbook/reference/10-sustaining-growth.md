# Ch 10 — Sustaining Entrepreneurial Growth
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 10, book pages 171–180.

## Purpose
What must change in the *business* (strategy, markets, capacity, hiring rules, structure) once it
has broken out of the startup phase and revenue is growing. Load when a user says "we're growing
fast", "how do we scale", "should we outsource", "how do we keep competitors out", or when a
journey enters the scaling stage. Chapter 11 covers the matching changes in the founder's leadership.

## Core ideas
- **Growth is a mixed blessing, especially rapid growth.** Keeping pace with demand normally needs
  outside capital, and every dollar of it has a cost: debt raises fixed expenses (more risk);
  outside equity dilutes the founders' ownership — and control.
- For platform and web-marketplace businesses fast scaling can be existential (Hoffman's
  **blitzscaling**): scale creates value for users (LinkedIn's professional base, eBay's buyers
  and sellers, Amazon's low margin / high volume) and wins **first-mover advantage** — securing
  customer loyalty in a segment before anyone else.
- Growth in sales triggers growth in everything that supports sales: customer service, marketing,
  transaction accounting, after-sales service; for physical product also purchasing, inventory,
  manufacturing and logistics. It also forces you to study new channels, product-line extensions
  and new markets, and to build retention strategies for new customers.
- Head count must grow with sales, plus the HR capacity to recruit, comply with labor law and run
  benefits — and finance: without a knowledgeable CFO and accounting staff the enterprise "could
  easily capsize and sink". Many promising ventures fail by not managing their way through
  initial success.
- Rule number one (learned by Hewlett and Packard): **management is getting results through
  people**; the founders' own skills are insufficient — you must marshal many employees' talent.
- Few strategies are sustainable long term. Regulation, deregulation, a superior technology, a
  faster/cheaper process, or entrants into a market you created will erode the advantage. Keep
  looking several steps ahead and bar the door to new competitors.
- Sustained growth does not always require invention: new uses, new niches and new geographies
  recharge the growth engine at far lower risk and cost than new-product development.
- Capacity scales differently by model: service firms scale by hiring scarce, expensive skilled
  people; product firms commit capital a year or more ahead of sales, which needs conviction that
  demand will actually be there.
- Outsourcing lets you scale without fixed assets — but never outsource the links to customers.
- As the business scales it must change: strategy, offering, structure and how you hire.

## Frameworks & tables
**The three post-startup growth questions** (p. 174) — revisit and recalibrate once revenue grows:
1. Is our strategy sustainable?
2. Do we have unique advantages that would let us expand into other markets?
3. Is scaling up the business a practical possibility?

**Why fast scale matters for platforms** (Hoffman, blitzscaling, p. 171–172):
- Scale itself creates value for users (network and volume effects).
- Reaching customers faster than competitors gives first-mover advantage — loyalty locked in
  before anyone else arrives.

**Growth triggers — what must scale with sales** (p. 173–174)

| Trigger | What must grow |
|---|---|
| More customers and transactions | Customer service, marketing, transaction accounting, after-sales service, retention strategies |
| Physical product | Materials purchasing, inventory management, manufacturing, logistics |
| Growing sales | Study of new distribution channels, product-line extensions, entry into new markets |
| Head count | Developers, security engineers, marketers, operations, sales, sales support, customer service; an HR function to recruit, comply and run benefits |
| Money flow | A knowledgeable CFO plus accounting staff keeping payments, collections and spending on an even keel |

**Four mechanisms for sustaining growth / barring the door to competitors** (p. 175–176)

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| Exploit the learning curve | If first to market, continual improvement in design and manufacturing efficiency lets you sell for less at the same profitability; late entrants that can't catch up are doomed to slim or no profits |
| Don't price for maximum profit | High margins attract rivals; if you and your investors accept a modest margin, would-be competitors stay away |
| Continually refresh the offer | New features, colour choices, lower price, easier purchase, fewer quality problems, amazing service — and reinvent the product for an as-yet unmet need |
| Be constantly vigilant about competition | Who will notice you and try to stop you? How can you change course or refine strategy to avoid or beat their challenge? |

**Three routes into new markets without invention** (p. 176–177)

| Route | Condition / method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Geographic expansion | Needs in unserved regions are the same or similar; via your own sales & marketing, or indirectly via distributors or a sales-representation arrangement | — |
| New uses for the same product | Raise consumption by existing customers and attract new ones | Baking soda: open box in the fridge, changed monthly; mixed into cat litter |
| Customise the product for other niches | Same core, different exterior for each segment, at very low cost | Swatch: dozens of watches on identical internal movements; only the case changes |

**Scaling capacity by business model** (p. 177–179)

| Model | Constraint | Scaling lever |
|---|---|---|
| Service (e.g. consulting) | Production = employee output; people with the unique skills are scarce and expensive; training may be needed | Hire people who can both sell and deliver; ask "can we scale human assets fast enough?" |
| Product / manufacturing | Substantial capital committed a year or more before the first unit ships; requires conviction that demand will exist then | Contract out to manufacturers selling unused capacity (short-run antidote), as Boston Beer did |

**Blitzscaling hiring hack** (Hoffman on Uber, p. 178): ask each newly hired engineer "who are the
three best engineers you've worked with?" — then send those three offer letters. No interview,
no reference check. Creative rule-bending lets you grow faster than predetermined process allows
while still trying to hire quality and keep the culture.

**Box — Tips on outsourcing** (p. 179). Outsourcing helps you scale without fixed assets you can't
afford (or that would drag you down if demand faltered) and frees managerial attention for what
really differentiates the company. Two cautions:
- **Never outsource activities that connect you directly with customers** — sales, customer
  service, market research, product or service development. These links let you learn about
  customers and them about you; outsource them and your customers become your partner's customers.
- **Never depend too much on any single outsource partner.** Ask what happens if a manufacturing,
  assembly or distribution partner fails or stops doing business with you; hedge by diversifying.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- If you run a platform or marketplace where scale creates user value → speed of scaling is a
  strategic necessity; plan capital and hiring for it rather than growing organically.
- Every dollar of outside capital has a negative side: debt → higher fixed cost and risk; equity →
  diluted ownership and control. Weigh both before financing growth.
- Robust revenue growth proves the strategy works *today*; the question is how much longer. Test it
  against competitors improving, technology maturing, new technology, and changed context.
- If you created a profitable, expanding market, assume other entrepreneurs will enter — build
  barriers now, not later.
- If first in market: ride the learning curve and price for a modest margin rather than maximum
  profit, so late entrants face slim profits and stay away.
- Prefer new uses, niche customisation and geographic expansion before betting the growth engine on
  risky, expensive new-product development.
- Expand geographically only where customer needs are the same as or similar to those you serve now.
- Manufacturers: plan facilities a year or more before first output. If the capital or the
  conviction about demand is missing, buy contract capacity instead of building.
- Outsource peripheral tasks; keep sales, customer service, market research and product
  development in-house.
- Diversify outsource partners; model the failure of any single one before signing.
- When growth outruns process, deliberately relax guidelines (e.g. referral offers without
  interviews) — but only where you can still protect quality and culture.
- Put a knowledgeable CFO and accounting staff in place before growth strains payments,
  collections and spending.

## Process / steps
1. Confirm you have broken out of the startup phase and revenue is growing.
2. Re-ask the three growth questions (strategy sustainable? unique advantages for other markets?
   scaling practical?) and recalibrate the plan.
3. Strategy: name what could undermine your advantage; choose barriers — learning curve, modest
   pricing, refreshed offer, competitor vigilance.
4. Markets: list unserved geographies with similar needs; list new uses and niche variants of the
   existing product; write the plan for recharging the growth engine.
5. Capacity: services — plan hiring and training of sell-and-deliver staff; products — decide build
   vs outsource with lead times and capital timing.
6. Support functions: scale service, marketing, accounting, HR and finance in step with sales.
7. Revisit strategy, offering, structure and hiring rules as scale changes them; then move to
   chapter 11 for the leadership changes.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
Growth readiness (p. 174):
- Is our strategy sustainable?
- Do we have unique advantages that would let us expand into other markets?
- Is scaling up the business a practical possibility?

Strategy stress-test (p. 174–176):
- What is our advantage based on — a new/superior product or technology, a lower price, or
  extreme convenience?
- What happens if competitors improve their offerings, the technology matures, new technology
  arises, or the context changes in some other way?
- Could what happened to Blockbuster happen to our business?
- Which patterns in our industry let us anticipate the solutions competitors will offer?
- As we grow, who is going to notice us and try to stop us? How can we change course or refine
  strategy to avoid or beat that challenge?
- Which barriers can we build: learning curve, modest pricing, a continually refreshed offer?
- How can we reinvent our product to solve an as-yet unmet customer need?

New-market scan (p. 176–177):
- Which geographic regions have no distribution from us, and are needs there similar to ours?
- For those regions: own sales and marketing, distributors, or sales representation?
- What new uses could existing customers find for the same product?
- Which niches could a customised version serve at low cost?
- What plan does our enterprise have for recharging the growth engine?

Capacity check (p. 177–179):
- Service: can we scale our human assets fast enough to satisfy demand and our own growth
  expectations?
- Product: what capital must be committed how far ahead of sales, and how certain is demand then?
- Which activities can be outsourced without touching customer contact?
- What happens if any one outsource partner fails or walks away?
- Do we have HR capacity to recruit, comply with labor law and manage benefits at scale?
- Do we have a CFO and accounting staff able to keep payments, collections and spending steady?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Hewlett-Packard** — 1938 garage, two employees, $5,369 revenue; Disney's eight oscillators for
  *Fantasia*; 111 people and ~$1M by 1943; 19 years to 1,000 employees; IPO only in 1957; grew to
  ~90,000 employees — deliberate pace, "results through people", the HP Way built to attract talent.
- **Amazon** — revenue $5.1M in 1996, $1.64B three years later — explosive scale.
- **Airbnb** — founded Aug 2008; 47,000 summer guests in 2010, 17 million by 2015.
- **Uber** — founded 2009; ride-share bookings $688M (2013) to $10.84B two years later.
- **Blockbuster vs Netflix** — market leader by 1993; Netflix's 1999 mail service (no late fees,
  far more titles), then Redbox and on-demand cable, ate its business; stores closed from 2006,
  operations ceased 2013 — few strategies last.
- **Netflix** — used the DVD base to introduce customers to streaming, then built original
  content: owns both content and distribution, a virtuous circle rivals can't break — bar the door.
- **Baking soda** — a leading supplier promoted fridge deodorising (change monthly) and cat-litter
  use — new uses grew sales to existing customers and created new ones.
- **Swatch** — dozens of watches for men, women, teens, sports fans on the same movement; only the
  case design changed — niche customisation at very low cost.
- **Uber engineering hiring** — offer letters to each new hire's three best former colleagues, no
  interview or references — hack the process to scale hiring.
- **Boston Beer / Jim Koch** — sixth-generation brewer, left consulting; R&D in an abandoned Boston
  brewery; brewing and bottling contracted to a quality Pennsylvania brewery under his own
  brewmaster; repeated as US distribution grew — keep the distinctive features, outsource the
  capital-intensive capacity.

## Pitfalls
- Failing to manage through initial success — no CFO, no HR, no accounting discipline.
- Assuming a strategy that works now will keep working.
- Pricing for maximum profit and inviting competitors into the market.
- Betting the growth engine on new-product invention alone.
- Expanding into geographies where customer needs differ.
- Building fixed assets a year ahead of unproven demand.
- Outsourcing sales, customer service, market research or product development.
- Depending on a single outsource partner.
- Ignoring the control cost of equity and the risk cost of debt when funding growth.

## Key terms
`blitzscaling` — Hoffman's term for growth at the aggressive rate platform and marketplace businesses require.
`first-mover advantage` — connecting with customers and securing their loyalty in a segment before anyone else does.
`strategy` — what differentiates a business in a way that confers a competitive advantage.
`learning curve` — falling unit cost from continual improvement in design and production efficiency as cumulative output grows.
`outsourcing` — buying other firms' unused capacity to scale output without owning the fixed assets.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Growth gate** — the three post-startup questions with evidence fields → go / recalibrate flag
  per question → decides whether the scaling journey unlocks.
- **Strategy durability check** — advantage type + threat scan (competitor improvement, maturing
  tech, new tech, regulation, new entrants) → risk score + barrier recommendations (learning curve,
  modest pricing, refresh, vigilance).
- **Market-expansion ideator** — product, current geography, customer profile → candidate new
  uses, niche variants and geographies (with a similar-needs test) → ranked growth-engine plan.
- **Capacity planner** — service vs product model, demand forecast, lead times → hiring plan or
  build/outsource decision with capital timing.
- **Outsourcing screener** — list of activities → flags customer-facing ones as keep-in-house;
  partner-concentration warning when one partner carries a critical function alone.
- **Support-function scaling checklist** — revenue and head-count bands → which functions
  (service, marketing, accounting, HR, CFO) must be added now, before they capsize the business.

## Summing up (book)
- Growth forces companies through transitions.
- Continued growth depends on a sustainable strategy, the ability to expand into other markets,
  and mechanisms for scaling up output.
- Three mechanisms sustain growth: exploit the learning curve for a cost advantage; don't price for
  maximum profit (high profits attract competitors); continually refresh the offer to customers.
- To scale, businesses often change guidelines around processes like hiring to become more nimble.
- Outsourcing peripheral tasks can meet rising demand; outsourcing core tasks — especially those
  that put the partner in direct contact with customers — can have very bad consequences.
