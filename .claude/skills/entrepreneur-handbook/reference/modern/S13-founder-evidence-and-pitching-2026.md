# S13 — Founder evidence and modern pitching (2026 layer)

## What changed since 2018
- **The founder-age myth was settled by administrative data, then mis-summarised.** Azoulay, Jones,
  Kim & Miranda (AER: Insights, 2020) put the mean age at founding of the 1-in-1,000 fastest-growing
  US new ventures at **45.0**, with similar results in high-tech sectors, entrepreneurial hubs and
  successful exits [1][2]. It is a *tail* statistic about US employer firms, not a prescription.
- **Solo founding went mainstream.** On Carta, solo-founded new incorporations rose from 23.7% (2019)
  to 36.3% (H1 2025) [3][4][5] — but solo founders took only 14.7% of cash raised in priced rounds
  [3][4]. The norm moved; the capital did not follow at the same rate.
- **The seed bar rose to what Series A used to be.** CRV (2026) targets **$500K-$1.5M ARR** for a
  strong B2B SaaS seed [20]; median seed on Carta is ~$4M at ~$20M post, and median seed→Series A
  time stretched to **2.1 years** from 1.5 in 2019 [6].
- **Failure got measured, not just anecdotal.** Carta counted **966** customer shutdowns in 2024 vs
  769 in 2023 [27]; from Q1 2024, *funded* companies began shutting down more often than unfunded
  ones [26]. BLS puts 5-year establishment survival in the low-to-mid 50s across cohorts [28][29][30].
- **The 40-page plan lost its investor audience; the *reading* artefacts multiplied.** Decks, memos,
  one-pagers and data rooms now tell one story at four depths [31][45][47] — while banks, SBA lenders
  and grant bodies still want the long document (see Regional notes).
- **Deck reading is now instrumented.** Papermark's 2026 report covers **24,541 decks / 358,672
  investor views / 15.2M page-level data points**: median **18 minutes** total attention per deck,
  **4.0 min** per view, 16% of views over within 10 seconds, fewer than 50% reach the last slide [31].
- **Acquisition entrepreneurship stopped being exotic.** Tracked US/Canada search funds grew from
  ~20 in the mid-1990s to **800+** by 2026, and SBA 7(a) acquisition lending hit **$8.29B in FY2025**,
  up ~35% YoY across ~7,000 deals [23][24].
- **AI made the deck cheap and the *insight* expensive.** Generic AI-drafted decks are now
  pattern-recognised by VCs, some of whom pre-screen with AI themselves [48][49][60].

## Core ideas (current consensus)
- **Experience, not youth, is the measurable edge.** The Azoulay et al. result is specifically that
  *prior experience in the specific industry* predicts much greater rates of entrepreneurial success,
  and that youth-centric hypotheses are "strongly rejected" [1]. Read it as an argument for
  founder-market fit, not as "wait until you are 45".
- **Founder-market fit is the earliest legible signal.** Before revenue exists, investors substitute
  the founder's lived exposure to the problem, domain network and credibility with buyers [54][61];
  CRV states it as "authentic problem insight over market trends" and "coachability over complete
  answers" [20].
- **There is no single founder personality.** McCarthy et al. (2023, 21,000+ startups) find founders
  differ from the general population on Big Five facets (openness to adventure, activity level, lower
  modesty) but cluster into **six** types, not one; personality-*diverse* teams do better, and the
  "Hipster, Hacker, Hustler" combination was about **twice as likely to succeed** [17][18] (cf. PNAS
  2023 on founder personality and outcomes [19]). This updates the handbook's single trait checklist.
- **Co-founding is a portfolio decision, not a virtue.** Carta: two-founder teams are the modal
  venture-funded shape, yet ~1 in 4 co-founder teams sees a departure by year four [6]; median
  ownership at exit was **75% greater** for solo founders — which is mostly arithmetic (no split),
  not proof of better outcomes [3][4].
- **Repeat founders are advantaged but not linearly.** Gompers, Kovner, Lerner & Scharfstein (JFE,
  2010) measured success as taking a VC-backed company public: **30%** for previously successful
  founders vs **18%** for first-timers; returns to further repetition decline [8].
- **Most "founder myths" are base-rate errors.** Tamaseb's *Super Founders* (2021) compared ~200
  billion-dollar companies with a random control of startups that had raised ≥$3M and found no
  penalty for solo founding or a non-technical CEO, that most had strong incumbent competitors, and
  that <15% went through an accelerator [9]. It is descriptive, not causal.
- **Being a founder is an occupational-health risk.** 44% high stress, 37% anxiety, 36% burnout, 13%
  depression, 10% panic attacks; only 10% talk to investors about it [15]. Brad Feld's 2025 work
  extends the finding to teams [16].
- **The plan is dead; the *thinking* is not.** No serious early-stage investor reads 40 pages, but
  the same five questions — opportunity, solution, market, model, team — survive in Sequoia's ten
  prompts [38] and YC's demo-day skeleton [39]. The handbook's core elements list is still correct;
  its *format* is not.
- **Clarity beats sophistication.** Kevin Hale (YC): make the idea *legible* — understandable by
  someone who knows nothing about the business — because a legible idea is what gets retold [40];
  YC works a company's two-sentence answer to "what do you do?" every week of the batch [41].

## Frameworks & playbooks

|---|---|---|---|
| Age & high-growth entrepreneurship | Azoulay, Jones, Kim & Miranda — *AER: Insights* 2(1), 2020 [1][2] | Mean founding age 45.0 for the top 0.1% fastest-growing US ventures; prior same-industry experience is the strong predictor; youth hypotheses rejected | Countering "too old to start"; arguing for domain choice |

| Super Founders base rates | Ali Tamaseb, 2021 [9] | 30,000 data points across unicorns vs a ≥$3M-raised control; debunks solo-founder, first-mover, accelerator and industry-experience "requirements" | Sanity-checking founder folklore before it becomes a rule |
| Founder Mode vs Manager Mode | Paul Graham, Sept 2024, after a Brian Chesky talk [50][51] | Scaling need not mean delegating into a management layer; founders can stay hands-on with skip-level detail | Leadership transition at 30-150 people; contested — see Pitfalls |

| Legibility pitch | Kevin Hale, YC Startup Library [40] | Problem + solution + unique insight, stated so a non-expert can repeat it; "why" and "how" often obstruct clarity | Writing the one-liner and the first two slides |

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **If you have 5+ years in the industry you are entering, weight that above age, pedigree or
  passion** — it is the variable the strongest founder-age evidence actually isolates [1].
- **If you are over 40, delete "too old" from the analysis.** The strongest US tail-growth evidence
  puts the mean at 45.0 [1]. If you are under 25, the same evidence says your edge must come from
  somewhere other than age.
- **If you cannot name three people who will take your call in this industry, you do not yet have
  founder-market fit** — fix that before the deck [54][61][20].
- **If you are choosing a co-founder, optimise for personality and skill complementarity, not
  friendship** — diverse-type teams outperform, and ~1 in 4 co-founder pairs breaks by year four
  [17][18][6]. Vest everything, 4 years / 1-year cliff, before you write a line of code.
- **If you go solo, expect the funding penalty, not a performance penalty.** 36% of new companies but
  14.7% of priced-round cash [3][4]; plan a bootstrap or angel path and a stronger traction story at
  the same stage.
- **If you need capital to hire a technical co-founder, you are not seed-ready** — CRV calls it an
  immediate rejection [20].
- **If your B2B SaaS has under ~$300-500K ARR, treat "seed" as pre-seed** and price/plan accordingly
  [20]; median seed→A is now 2.1 years, so raise 30 months of runway, not 18 [6].
- **If you are building a deck, target 9-16 pages and put Traction in the first three slides**
  (+20% views) [31]. Below 8 or above 16 you lose completion.
- **If the first slide does not land, nothing else will.** The biggest drop-off is slide 1 → 2 and
  16% of views end within 10 seconds [31]; open with what you do in one legible sentence [40][41].
- **If you are pre-seed/seed, keep the data room to roughly five documents** (deck, model, cap table,
  founders'/IP agreements, summary) and stage deeper access [45][47].
- **If any past contributor has not signed an IP assignment, fix it before you open a data room** —
  it is the most commonly flagged seed diligence defect [47].
- **If you are raising, send a monthly update whether or not anyone replies**, including to
  prospective investors: the causal evidence is weak but the cost is an hour a month [44].
- **If you have operating skill, capital access and no novel idea, price the ETA path against the
  startup path** — but read search-fund returns as dollar-weighted and tail-driven, with ~26% of
  acquisitions showing a loss [21][22][23].
- **If a bank, SBA lender, grant body or visa route is in your funding mix, write the long plan
  anyway** — the handbook's chapter-5 format is what those readers expect (Regional notes).
- **If you show signs from the Startup Snapshot list (chronic anxiety, burnout, panic), treat it as
  an operating risk with a plan**, not a character flaw: 77% of founders never see a therapist [15][16].

## Process / steps
1. **Founder-market fit audit (before the idea).** Write, in one page: the problem you have lived,
   how long you were exposed to it, who you can call in that market tomorrow, what you know that
   outsiders do not, and what you would still be working on in seven years [54][61][20].
2. **Base-rate check.** Test each assumption about "what founders must have" against published base
   rates (age [1], solo founding [3][4][9], accelerators [9], competitors [9]). Delete the folklore.
3. **Team shape decision.** Solo, pair or trio; map complementary types and the capability gap list;
   if co-founding, sign vesting, roles and IP assignment on day one [17][18][6][47].
4. **Choose the entry path.** Start vs buy: capital required, time to cash, personal risk appetite
   and skill fit (ETA vs startup) [21][24][25].
5. **Write the one-liner.** Company name + what you do, in language a non-expert can repeat. Iterate
   it weekly; it is the linchpin of everything downstream [40][41].
6. **Build the narrative spine.** Big change in the world → old mindset (the enemy) → promised land →
   your capabilities → proof [42][43].
7. **Draft the Lean Canvas**, not the 40-page plan: problem, customer segments, UVP, solution,
   channels, revenue, costs, key metrics, unfair advantage [59].
8. **Convert the canvas into a 9-16 page deck** on the Sequoia/YC skeleton: purpose → problem →
   solution → why now → market (bottoms-up) → traction (early!) → product → model → competition →
   team → ask [38][39][31].
9. **Write the 2-4 page memo** — the same story with the reasoning and numbers a deck cannot carry,
   for asynchronous readers and partner meetings [31].
10. **Instrument distribution.** Send tracked links (not attachments), watch time-per-page and
    completion, and re-cut the slides where readers stall [31][32][33].
11. **Stage the data room.** Level 1 on interest, Level 2 on term-sheet track: clean cap table, IP
    assignments, contracts, model [45][46][47].
12. **Run the monthly update from day one** — metrics, wins, lowlights, asks — to existing and
    prospective investors [44]; and put a founder-health routine (peer group, named professional,
    recovery block, co-founder check-in) in the operating plan [15][16].

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Founder-market fit score (0-5 each, replaces the trait quiz as the primary screen):**
- Years of direct exposure to this problem or industry.
- Number of potential customers who would take your call this week.
- A non-obvious insight you hold that most people in the market do not.
- Depth of your hiring network in this domain.
- Would you still choose this problem if it took ten years?
- Do you have a distribution advantage (audience, channel, relationships)?

**Team-shape checklist:** complementary types identified? vesting + cliff signed? IP assignments from
every contributor? roles and final-decision rights written down? a documented way to break a tie?
co-founder "pre-nup" for departure? [6][17][47]

**Founder wellbeing check (monthly, private):** stress level 1-10; sleep; one thing you are hiding
from your team or investors; who you last spoke to honestly; when you last took two consecutive days
off; is there a professional you could call today? [15][16]

**Deck QA (run before every send):**
- Is the one-liner on slide 1, readable in five seconds? [40][41]
- Is traction in the first three slides, and the deck 9-16 pages? [31]
- ≤7 words on any slide that will be presented live; bottoms-up market sizing? [39]
- Team slide present (71% include it; it is the most-read slide)? [31][35]
- Financials included (only 40% do, and they are read above median)? [31]
- Does every slide carry a specific fact an AI could not have produced? [48][60]

**Diligence-readiness checklist (Level 1 / Level 2):** deck; 2-4 page memo; financial model; cap
table; incorporation docs; founders' agreement and vesting; IP assignments (every contributor);
customer contracts; key metrics export; org chart; regulatory approvals [45][46][47].

**Monthly investor update template:** headline metric + delta; 3 wins; 3 lowlights; cash and runway;
hiring; 2 specific asks; one-line ask-for-intros list [44].

## Regional notes
- **US.** Deepest seed market and the source of nearly all the founder data above — remember the
  Carta/DocSend/Papermark samples are venture-track and US-heavy [3][6][31]. SBA 7(a) makes the
  buy-a-business path uniquely financeable: $8.29B of acquisition lending in FY2025 [24]. Black
  founders received 0.32% of US VC in 2025 [11]; female-founded companies a record 27.7% of deal
  value, flattered by two AI megadeals [10].
- **UK.** The long-form plan survives for bank lending, Innovate UK grants and Innovator Founder
  visas. Investing in Women Code signatories put 32% of VC into teams with at least one female
  founder vs 15% in the wider market; all-female teams 6% vs 2% [14]. SEIS/EIS shape deck and
  cap-table structure from the first round (see `S06-financing-2026.md`).
- **EU.** Grant and public co-investment programmes (EIC, national agencies) still require full
  written plans, work packages and budgets — the handbook's chapter-5 format is *live* here, not
  obsolete. All-female European teams took 1% of deal value in 2026-to-date, down from 1.3% [10].
- **Africa (Nigeria especially).** Total 2025 funding was ~$3.2B; all-female-founded teams got 0.9%
  ($28.8M), female-CEO-led 2.2%, mixed teams 8% [12][13]. Assume a longer raise, build a
  revenue-first plan, and expect diligence to focus on governance, FX exposure and customer
  concentration. Founder mental-health support networks are thin and under-reported [62].
- **India.** ~$10.5-11B in 2025 with deals down 39% and seed down 30%; first-time-funded companies
  fell from 678 to 459, while "operator-founders" (ex-senior operators at scaled startups) attracted
  ~11% of total funding despite being <1% of companies founded 2023-25 [55][56]. Founder pedigree is
  doing unusually heavy lifting in this market.
- **Southeast Asia.** $5.4B across 461 deals in 2025; seed collapsed to $110M in the first nine
  months (-72% YoY) and Singapore captured 91% of regional capital [57][58]. Plan for a Singapore
  holding structure and a longer pre-seed bridge.
- **Middle East / LatAm.** Sovereign and corporate-backed funds dominate, pushing founders back
  toward formal written plans and board-grade models; no 2024-26 primary dataset verified here —
  treat regional benchmarks as unestablished.

## AI-era notes
- **The deck is now free to produce, so it no longer signals effort.** VCs report pattern-recognising
  AI-generated decks on sight — identical visual language, boilerplate competition slides, templated
  market narratives — and firms including EQT and Moonfire are pre-screening decks with AI before a
  partner opens them [48][49]. The scarce input is a specific, earned fact per slide.
- **Practical rule:** use AI for the first draft, the structure and the tightening; supply it with
  your own raw material (call transcripts, usage data, customer quotes) and hand-write every line
  that contains an insight or a number [48][60].
- **Investor attention is measured, so optimise against measurement, not taste.** Tracked-link
  analytics (Papermark, DocSend) tell you exactly where readers stop; re-cut those slides [31][32].
  Do not confuse an investor's AI summariser reading your deck with an investor reading it.
- **"Team" is rising in attention precisely because AI made everything else easy to fake** — the
  2024 DocSend shift (+40% seed team-slide time, -48% competition) is consistent with investors
  re-weighting toward the least-copyable signal [35].
- **Solo founding and "tiny teams" are the AI-era story**, and the Carta trend line is real (23.7% →
  36.3% solo) [3][4]. The viral revenue-per-employee anecdotes circulating in 2026 are *not* from
  audited datasets; treat one-person-unicorn claims as marketing until a primary source exists.
- **Founder-fit self-assessment is a good AI use case; diagnosis is not.** An AI advisor can score
  founder-market fit and run a base-rate check; it must not be positioned as mental-health care —
  route to a human professional, since 77% of founders never reach one [15][16].
- **Where the hype outruns the evidence:** AI plan/deck generators claim fundraising uplift with no
  controlled evidence; the only well-powered public datasets measure *reading behaviour*, not
  conversion [31][32]. Nothing in the 2024-26 literature shows an AI-written narrative beating a
  founder-written one.

## Pitfalls
- **Quoting "45" as a target age.** It is a mean for the top 0.1% of US growth firms, not an optimum,
  a median, or a claim about the odds of any individual founder [1].
- **Reading Carta as "all startups".** Carta measures venture-track US companies on one cap-table
  platform; shutdown and ownership figures are not economy-wide [3][6][26].
- **Confusing solo founders' higher exit ownership with better outcomes.** Not splitting equity
  mechanically raises your share; it says nothing about the size of the exit [3][4].
- **Treating repeat-founder data as personal destiny.** The 30% vs 18% finding was measured on
  *previously successful* VC-backed founders taking companies public, in a 2010 paper [8].
- **Building the 40-page plan for a VC.** No dataset in 2024-26 shows an investor reading one; the
  measured median attention is 18 minutes across an entire deck [31]. Write it only for lenders,
  grant bodies and your own budget.
- **Shipping an AI-default deck.** Generic phrasing now reads as an absence of insight, not polish
  [48][49][60].
- **Taking "founder mode" as licence to micromanage.** Graham's essay is an observation about a
  poorly-studied way of running a company, contested since publication; it is not a management method
  with evidence behind it [50][51].
- **Treating the investor-update multipliers as established fact.** The widely-quoted "40% higher
  follow-on" and "2.3x faster" figures come from vendor content, not a primary study [44].
- **Hiding founder distress.** 81% of founders are not open about it and only 10% tell investors —
  which is exactly why it shows up as a company-level failure rather than a personal one [15].

## Key terms
`founder-market fit` — the alignment of a founder's lived exposure, network and obsession with the
market they are entering; used as a pre-traction investment screen.
`FOALED types` — the six founder personality clusters (Fighters, Operators, Accomplishers, Leaders,
Engineers, Developers) identified by McCarthy et al. (2023).
`ETA (entrepreneurship through acquisition)` — becoming an owner-operator by buying an existing small
business rather than starting one.
`search fund` — a vehicle raising "search capital" for an entrepreneur to find and acquire one
company, then operate and exit it.
`investment memo` — a 2-4 page prose document carrying the reasoning, numbers and risks a deck cannot.
`staged data room` — a diligence room with access tiers released as an investor's interest deepens.
`strategic narrative` — Raskin's structure: name the change, the enemy (an old mindset), the promised
land, your capabilities, and the evidence.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Founder-Market Fit Score** — inputs: years in domain, callable contacts, non-obvious insight,
  hiring network, 10-year conviction, distribution advantage → output: 0-100 score, the weakest
  dimension, and a 30-day plan to raise it → replaces the trait quiz as the primary onboarding gate.
- **Base-Rate Buster** — input: a founder belief ("I need a technical co-founder", "I'm too old",
  "I must be first to market") → output: the relevant published base rate with the source and what
  the study actually measured [1][8][9] → kills folklore before it becomes strategy.
- **Co-founder Compatibility & Paperwork Wizard** — inputs: each founder's type, skills, decision
  rights, equity intent → outputs: complementarity map, tie-break rule, vesting/IP checklist and
  generated founders'-agreement prompts [17][6][47].
- **Start vs Buy Comparator** — inputs: capital available, operating experience, risk tolerance, time
  to income needed → outputs: side-by-side startup vs ETA path with financing routes (SBA 7(a),
  seller note, search fund) and the honest loss distribution [21][23][24][25].
- **One-Pager → Deck → Memo → Data Room generator** — one canvas as the source of truth, producing a
  9-16 page deck (traction in the first three slides), a 2-4 page memo, a one-pager and a staged data
  room checklist, all kept in sync [31][38][39][45].
- **Deck Doctor** — input: an uploaded deck → outputs: length vs the 9-16 page band, slide-order
  score, words-per-slide flags, missing Team/Financials, generic-AI-language detector, and the three
  slides to rewrite first [31][35][48].
- **Investor Update Engine** — inputs: metrics feed + a five-minute monthly form → outputs: a sent
  update, a running investor CRM, and a pre-raise warm-up drip list [44].
- **Founder Health Check** — inputs: monthly private self-report (stress, sleep, isolation,
  disclosure) → outputs: trend chart, peer-group and professional-referral prompts, and an explicit
  "this is not therapy" boundary [15][16].
