# Ch 5 — Writing Your Business Plan
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 5, book pages 77–100.

## Purpose
What a business plan is for, which format fits your audience (traditional plan, executive summary,
elevator pitch, pitch deck, demo, canvas), exactly what goes into each section, and how to write it
so a funder who gives you two minutes keeps reading. Load when a user asks "write my business plan",
"what goes in a pitch deck / executive summary", "how long should X be", or whenever the app generates
any funding or planning document.

## Core ideas
- **Definition (p77):** a business plan explains a business opportunity, identifies the market to be
  served, details how the organisation will pursue it, describes the unique qualifications of you and
  your team, states the resources needed and forecasts financial results over a reasonable horizon.
- **Funding is the obvious reason, not the only one.** Banks, angels, VCs — even relatives — won't
  take you seriously without a solid plan (p78). Three further benefits: a deep reality check and
  blueprint, advice from experienced outsiders, and projections that become your first budget (p78–79).
- **The numbers are the weakest part.** "No business plan survives first contact with customers"
  (Steve Blank). Padding plus enthusiasm make month-by-month projections years out unrealistic.
  Sahlman (HBR, 1997): the best plans focus on the *people* and the *business model* (p79).
- **Plans are evolving.** Tech founders use shorter, narrative, visual formats — pitch decks, demos,
  elevator pitches, the business-model canvas (Baehr & Loomis) — that openly admit the future can't
  be predicted. Pick the approach for your business type (p80).
- **Whatever the format, the same core elements must be thought through:** the opportunity, the
  solution, the market, the model, the team (p80).
- **Readers are overloaded.** Many VC firms review 1,000+ plans a year and fund a few; banks and angels
  are the same. Grab attention, keep it, and answer the reader's concerns in a well-organised way (p80).
- **Show your work.** Because the numbers will be wrong, the plan must demonstrate professionalism,
  expertise and trustworthiness — not just optimism (p80).
- **Know your audience.** Nothing about the prototype format is sacred; tailor it to the reader's
  interests the way you'd tailor a résumé to a job (p81).
- **Style is half the plan.** Most founders shortchange it, making plans dull and hard to read. Use
  words sparingly, keep sentences simple, use design elements, use graphics judiciously (p92).
- **Tell readers how they get their money out.** Investors want an exit — sooner rather than later (p99).

**Section-by-section specification (the generator schema)**

*Contents (p81).* A table of contents so readers see at a glance what the plan offers and how to navigate.

*I. Executive summary (p82).* The most important part for capturing backers; many readers never go
further. Assume one or two minutes to convince them to keep reading.
- Traditional: 2–3 pages. Not a preface or introduction — a snapshot of the entire plan that
  explains the business to an intelligent reader in a few minutes.
- Must explain (from the chapter's summary, p99): the opportunity; why it is timely; how the company
  will pursue it; expected results; a thumbnail of the company and the management team.
- Newer variants: an elevator pitch of ~100 words or a single sentence, often story-based to reach
  the reader's emotions; in a pitch deck, an overview slide saying what the company is and what
  problem it solves, big-picture; a separate 2-minute video elevator pitch on a private YouTube
  channel for shareability.
- Test: is it memorable enough that the reader can easily recount it to others?

*II. The opportunity (p82–83).*
- Outline the problem, its scope, and the market trends that will shape how the market experiences
  the problem in the years ahead; then introduce your solution.
- Describe the opportunity in clear, compelling terms; help readers see and appreciate it.
- Highlight the economics underlying the problem and the factors that will drive your solution's
  success (e.g. market penetration, product innovation).
- State the magnitude of funding sought and how it will be used.
- Keep it brief, focused and upbeat. Document with objective data but do not data-dump: summarise
  the data, explain its implications for investors, push detail to the appendix.
- Lo-Sugar example content: obesity figures for the US and Western Europe and their effect on
  health, quality of life and care costs; research that 29 million Americans and 8 million Europeans
  follow low-sugar diets (the chapter intro cites 4.5M / 1.2M — the company is fictional); independent
  studies confirming effectiveness; consumer spending on health and weight-control foods; funding
  ask of $2.75 million, mostly to exploit current success and a national high-protein/low-carb
  vendor's interest in existing products.

*III. The company and its offering and strategy (p83–86).* Four sub-parts.
1. *Solution and company* — the product or service, the company and how it is organised, and any
   traction data so far. Lo-Sugar: Colorado corporation founded 2018; three test-marketed products
   (Mellow Mornings — whole-wheat/barley cereal with 90% less sugar than leading cereals; Crackle
   Brackle — crisped steel-cut oatmeal for breakfast and baking; Yesgurt — dairy), differentiated by
   being tasty and satisfying as well as low-sugar; sold through two regional health-food chains;
   more in development; market-test details referenced to the appendix.
2. *Goals* — how you plan to grow. If the company could become an acquisition target for a larger,
   less innovative competitor, say so. Lo-Sugar's three goals: broaden the product line; expand
   penetration through stores and a private-label agreement with a major diet company (in
   negotiation); become the dominant healthy-food niche player or be acquired by a packaged-food
   giant (small companies with one or two hit products are often bought at premium prices).
   Product readiness: not yet market-ready → rollout plans, snapshots of viable prototypes, an
   artist's rendering of the final product; market-ready → high-quality photos.
3. *Business model and strategy* (its own section) — business model = how you make money; strategy
   = differentiation and competitive advantage. Answer: What are your costs? Where will revenue come
   from? What makes the product, service or delivery different and more desirable to customers? How
   does that difference become a *sustainable* advantage producing profits and growing equity value?
   Differentiation can sit in the product/service (a technically superior way of servicing electric
   cars) or in the approach to customers (Uber's platform bypassing taxis). Describe competitors:
   why customers will choose you, proof you understand how the industry operates, how competitors
   will react to your success, what happens if they copy your best seller, how you keep loyalty.
4. *Ownership* — current owners and percentages; how ownership is evidenced (common vs preferred
   stock); options, warrants or convertible bonds that could expand ownership; which owners work in
   the business day to day.

*IV. The team (p86–89).* One of the most important sections. "Without the right team, none of the
other parts really matter" (Sahlman).
- No lengthy biographies; highlight the experiences and qualifications that make these the right
  people for this mission. Résumés go in the appendix.
- Say how the team must grow and what capabilities the strategy will need.
- Add a table of names, titles and salaries (Table 5-1) and an org chart if reporting lines aren't
  obvious.
- For a corporation, list the board: names, board positions, professional backgrounds, history with
  the company.
- Lo-Sugar pattern for a credential line: name + years + function + named prior employers + one
  concrete accomplishment (e.g. the extrusion process behind two hit snack brands) or PhD/role left
  to co-found.

*V. Marketing plan (p89–90).* A close second to the team in reader attention: marketing is the
activity most associated with success or failure; a company fails if customers never hear of it.
Cover the ten items in the marketing checklist below, summarise the supporting market intelligence
here and point to the research in the appendix.

*VI. Operating plan (p90–91).* Applies to manufacturers, distributors, websites, apps and platforms
alike: the details of converting inputs into outputs customers value — supplier relationships
(current and envisioned), inventory required, which day-to-day tasks are internal vs outsourced.

*VII. Financial plan (p91).*
- An operating company has three statements: **balance sheet** (assets and how they are financed —
  liabilities and owners' funds — at a date); **income statement** (revenues, what was spent to earn
  them, interest and taxes over a period); **cash-flow statement** (sources and uses of cash over the
  same period). Basics in appendix A of the book.
- Put full statements in the appendix; use this section for the big-picture data, chiefly the sales
  and expense projections (the pro forma income statement).
- Break out key items: revenue by distribution channel (Table 5-2) and key operating-expense
  categories such as marketing (Table 5-3), each with growth and percentage of sales.
- State the assumptions behind every projection and why you made them — experienced investors will ask.

**Document formats compared (p80, p82, p94)**

| Format | Length / shape | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional business plan | ~40 pp text + numbers + appendix (Figure 5-1) | Banks, formal investors, industries expecting rigour |
| Executive summary | 2–3 pages; snapshot of the whole plan | Every plan; often the only part read |
| Elevator pitch | ~100 words or one sentence; story-based | Openers, emails, memorable retelling |
| Video elevator pitch | 2 minutes, private YouTube link | Sharable intro for tech/consumer ventures |
| Presentation pitch deck | Visual aid; ≤1 sentence per slide, often a word or phrase | Live pitches |
| Reading deck | Sent to read; each slide states point + evidence; scannable in 15 s | Investors reviewing remotely |
| Demo | Working product shown live | Tech sector; proves rather than predicts |
| Business-model canvas | One-page model | Thinking through the model (Baehr & Loomis) |

**Box — Intellectual property (p86)**
Readers want to know what protects the advantage and keeps know-how inside the company: legal
protections (patents, copyrights, trademarks — and when they expire) or strategic defences such as
first-mover advantage (first to scale in a market) or a business model that is hard to replicate.

**Box — The board of directors (p90)**
Every corporation must have a board by law, but a good board is a sounding board and a source of
sage advice. Recruit hard: abundant business experience, scientific/engineering know-how if
technology is essential, respect in the wider business community. Their capabilities and integrity
"speak volumes" to financiers reading the plan. Non-US note: board requirements differ (e.g. a UK
private limited company needs at least one director, not a full board); check local company law.

**Box — Pitch deck style (Baehr & Loomis, *Get Backed*, p94)**
- *Presentation decks:* very few words, no more than one sentence per slide; complete sentences not
  needed — one word or a short phrase to introduce the idea you'll carry forward. Test: take your
  reading deck, delete everything except the headers, and check whether the headers alone still
  convey each slide.
- *Reading decks:* slides must do the work you'd do in person — catch attention fast, state the
  point, back it with evidence, move on. Kill sentences that sound impressive but mean nothing
  ("We plan to pursue an effective marketing strategy"). Put "Marketing Strategy" in the corner, then
  the strategy in 15 or fewer words; for multi-phase strategies, a heading per phase plus a short
  explanation. Reading decks are scanning decks: 15 seconds should give a good idea of the whole.

**Figure 5-2 — How people get downtown (p97):** car 54%, walk 35%, public transport 9%, bike 2% —
one pie chart replaces a four-sentence paragraph and is more memorable and sharable.

**Figure 5-3 — Persuasive charts (Berinato, HBR June 2016, p98):** a chart of "change in health
spending and GDP" (1990–2015, two series, dense labels) is accurate but unclear; "annual growth in
health-care spending is declining" (2003–2013, one line from 8.6% to 3.6%) makes the point instantly.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- Seeking any outside money → you need a coherent plan in *some* format; without one you look like
  you haven't identified customers or how to serve them profitably.
- Choose format by audience, business and industry: tech investors → deck/demo/story; banks and
  traditional investors → fuller written plan. Tailor like a résumé.
- Executive summary: 2–3 pages traditional; elevator pitch ~100 words or one sentence; video 2
  minutes; assume the reader gives you one or two minutes.
- Presentation deck: ≤1 sentence per slide. Reading deck: strategy statements ≤15 words; whole deck
  scannable in 15 seconds.
- Paragraphs should average no more than 200 words. Long blocks → headings, subheads, lists, white space.
- Numbered list when sequence matters; bulleted list for items, parts of a whole, or three key ideas;
  never long complex bullets (they're just another text block).
- One message per chart. Decide the point *before* clicking "insert chart"; sketch by hand first;
  drop distracting series and detailed labels.
- Detailed data, full financial statements, résumés, market research → appendix. Body = summary +
  implications for investors.
- Every projection must carry its assumptions and the reasoning behind them.
- Products not market-ready → rollout plan + prototype snapshots + artist's rendering; market-ready →
  high-quality photos.
- Corporation → name the board; non-obvious reporting lines → org chart.
- If a larger, less innovative competitor might acquire you → say so in Goals.
- Always answer "how will investors cash out?" — buyout by management, acquisition, IPO. Even if you
  plan to stay for the long haul, investors want liquidity, the sooner the better.
- Treat the plan as a thinking exercise, not fact (Blank); the numbers are not a strong predictor of success.
- Use Word/PowerPoint features (bold, italics, fonts, colour, 3-D, clip art, animation) sparingly —
  overuse looks amateurish.
- "Shorter is always better if it communicates the required information" (p92).

## Process / steps
1. Decide the format(s) for your audience: traditional plan, executive summary, elevator pitch,
   pitch deck (presentation vs reading), video, demo, canvas.
2. Think through the core elements regardless of format: opportunity, solution, market, model, team.
3. Draft each section in the Figure 5-1 order (opportunity → company/offering/strategy incl. goals,
   model, ownership, IP → team incl. board → marketing → operations → financials).
4. Move detail into the appendix: résumés, market research, sales projections, full statements,
   market-test data.
5. Write the executive summary as a snapshot of the finished plan, then compress it further into an
   elevator pitch / overview slide / video script.
6. Style pass: omit needless words; split complex sentences; add headings, subheads, lists, white
   space; convert numeric paragraphs into single-message charts.
7. Reader pass: stop and ask — Is this a real opportunity? Do I understand the market and customers?
   Can we really make this work? Confirm funding ask, use of funds and exit strategy are stated.
8. Expose the draft to trusted, experienced outsiders to find missed opportunities, unsupportable
   assumptions and over-optimistic projections; fix them on paper.
9. After launch, use the projections as the initial budget; investigate and correct when actuals fall short.

Chart-making sub-process (Berinato, p97): decide the point → sketch formats by hand (pie, bar, tree
map) → pick the one that makes the point most clearly → include only the data needed → keep text minimal.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions

**Executive summary must-answer list (p82, p99)**
- What is the opportunity, and why is it timely?
- How will the company pursue it?
- What results do you expect?
- Thumbnail: what is the company, who is the team?
- Can a reader retell it to someone else after one read?

**Opportunity section checklist (p82–83)**
- Problem, its scope, and the market trends shaping it in coming years
- The proposed solution
- Underlying economics and the drivers of success (penetration, innovation)
- Funding sought and use of funds
- Data summarised with investor implications; detail in appendix

**Business model & strategy questions (p85)**
- What are your costs? Where will your revenue come from?
- What is different about your approach to the marketplace?
- What makes your product, service or delivery different and more desirable in customers' eyes?
- How will that difference translate into a sustainable competitive advantage, profits and growing equity value?
- Who are your competitors, and why will customers choose you instead?
- How does your industry operate?
- How might competitors react to your success? What if they copy your best-selling product?
- How will you maintain customer loyalty?

**Ownership questions (p86)**
- Who are the current owners and what percentages do they control?
- How is ownership evidenced (common vs preferred stock)?
- Have you issued options, warrants or convertible bonds that could expand ownership?
- Which owners are involved in the day-to-day workings of the business?

**Intellectual property questions (p86)**
- Is your competitive advantage based on a proprietary technology or process?
- Is it patented or patentable?
- Does the company own patents, copyrights or valuable trademarks? When do they expire?
- What steps keep technical and market know-how inside the organisation?

**Box — Questions about your team every business plan should answer (Sahlman, p88)**
- Where are the founders and other key team members from?
- Where did they go to school?
- Where have they worked — and for whom? What are their current roles?
- What have they accomplished — professionally and personally — in the past?
- What is their reputation within the business community?
- What aspects of their experience are directly relevant to the opportunity they are pursuing?
- What skills, abilities and knowledge do they have?
- How realistic are they about the venture's chances for success and the tribulations it will face?
- Who else needs to be on the team?
- Are they prepared to recruit high-quality people?
- How will they respond to adversity?
- Do they have the mettle to make the inevitable hard choices?
- How committed are they to this venture?
- What are their motivations?
Plus: how will the team need to grow, and what capabilities does the strategy require? (p87)

**Board of directors entry (p89)** — name; position on the board; professional background; history
of involvement with the company.

**Marketing plan checklist — be clear about all ten (p89–90)**
1. Who your customers / primary market are — the customer type you must reach for full market potential
2. Market size — number of potential customers and projected potential sales revenues
3. Requirements of each customer segment (purchase convenience, rapid delivery, customisation, etc.)
4. Ways to access each segment (distributors, e-commerce, captive sales force)
5. Sales and promotion approaches (social media campaigns, content marketing, freemium, direct email)
6. How your customer makes purchase decisions
7. Customer price sensitivity
8. Acquisition cost per customer and cost of retaining customers
9. Competitors' strengths and weaknesses and their likely reaction when you enter
10. Supporting market intelligence — summarised here, detailed in the appendix

**Operating plan questions (p90–91)**
- What supplier relationships do you have or envision?
- How much inventory will be required?
- Which day-to-day tasks are handled internally, and which are outsourced?
- How, in detail, do inputs become outputs customers value?

**Financial plan checklist (p91)**
- Balance sheet, income statement, cash-flow statement (full versions in appendix)
- Pro forma income statement: sales and expense projections
- Revenue breakout by channel with growth and % of sales (Table 5-2 pattern)
- Key expense categories with % of sales (Table 5-3 pattern)
- Assumptions behind each projection and why you made them

**Style checklist (p92–98)**
- Every word tells; needless words omitted (Strunk & White rule 17)
- No sentence beyond its "optimal carrying capacity"; extra information gets its own sentence
- Headings and subheads carry the key ideas — a skimmer gets the gist from them alone
- Paragraphs average ≤200 words; lists used for sequence, items, parts of a whole, three key ideas
- Each chart makes one point; text in graphics minimal
- Design features used sparingly; document looks professional, not decorated

**Reader-lens self-check (p98–99)**
- Is this a real opportunity with substantial growth possibilities and major upside?
- Have I shown my homework — the market, the right customers, a sound strategy to transact profitably?
- Does the team have the knowledge, experience and drive to turn the opportunity into profits?
- Can we really make this thing work?
- How will investors get their money out?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Lo-Sugar Foods Company** (fictional prototype, p81–92) — unsweetened breakfast/snack maker; three
  products in two regional chains; $2.75M ask; three growth goals ending in dominance or acquisition;
  channel revenue from $112K to $520K in three years — the worked template for every section.
- **Uber** (p85) — differentiation from the approach to customers (a platform connecting drivers and
  riders, bypassing taxis), not from a superior product.
- **Electric-car servicing** (p85) — differentiation residing in a technically superior product/service.
- **Gunther Schwartz's credential** (p87) — twelve years at two named employers plus the extrusion
  process behind two successful snack brands — how to write a team bio: concrete, relevant, short.
- **Downtown transport survey** (p96–97) — four sentences of percentages vs one pie chart; the chart wins.
- **Health-spending chart** (p98) — two series and dense labels obscure the message; one line, one
  message ("annual growth is declining") lands.
- **Weight-loss four steps** (p96) — eat less → exercise more → try diet programmes → settle on a
  long-term diet — a numbered list makes a sequence unmissable.
- **Steve Blank** (p79) — plans are full of untested assumptions; valuable for thinking, not fact.
- **William Sahlman** (p79, p87) — judge plans by people and business model; the team is decisive.

## Pitfalls
- Mistaking the plan for fact; padding numbers; detailed month-by-month projections years ahead.
- Turning the opportunity section into a data dump that buries the story.
- Lengthy biographies instead of relevant qualifications.
- Ignoring how competitors will react or what happens if they copy you.
- Omitting the funding ask, use of funds, or the exit strategy.
- Projections without stated assumptions.
- Empty impressive-sounding sentences; long complex sentences; long text blocks; long complex bullets.
- Charts that carry several messages or distracting extra data.
- Overusing fonts, colours, 3-D, clip art and animation — looks amateurish.
- Concentrating on substance and ignoring style entirely, producing a dull, hard-to-read plan.
- Sending a presentation deck to be read (too sparse) or presenting a reading deck (too wordy).

## Key terms
`business plan` — document explaining an opportunity, its market, how the company will pursue it, the team's qualifications, resources needed and forecast results.
`executive summary` — 2–3 page snapshot of the whole plan; the section most readers stop at.
`elevator pitch` — ~100-word or one-sentence, often story-based statement of the business; also a 2-minute video variant.
`pitch deck` — slide-based plan; a *presentation deck* supports a live talk (≤1 sentence/slide), a *reading deck* stands alone (point + evidence per slide, scannable in 15 s).
`business model` — how you plan to make money (costs, revenue sources).
`strategy` — how you differentiate and sustain competitive advantage.
`pro forma income statement` — projected sales and expenses; the core of the financial plan section.
`balance sheet` / `income statement` / `cash-flow statement` — what you own and how it's financed at a date / revenues, costs, interest and taxes over a period / sources and uses of cash over that period.
`first-mover advantage` — being first to scale in a market; a strategic (non-legal) IP defence.
`private-label agreement` — making product sold under another company's brand; a channel in Lo-Sugar's goals.
`exit strategy` — how investors get liquidity: management buyout, acquisition, IPO.

  exit strategy → red flags before the plan goes out.

## Summing up (book)
- Choose a format that makes sense for your audience, your business and the industry.
- Whatever the format, tell readers persuasively everything they need to make a decision.
- Funding is only one reason to write a plan; the act of writing forces you to think through every key element.
- The executive summary should compellingly explain the opportunity, why it is timely, how you'll
  pursue it, expected results, and a thumbnail of the company and team.
- State the company's goals and explain how investors will eventually cash out.
- Pay attention to style: as few words as necessary; avoid long, complex sentences.
- Make it skimmable: simple data visualisations, headings, subheads, white space, numbered and bulleted lists.
