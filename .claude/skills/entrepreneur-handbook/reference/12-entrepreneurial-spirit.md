# Ch 12 — Keeping the Entrepreneurial Spirit Alive
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 12, book pages 195–210.

## Purpose
How a growing company keeps innovating once it has customers, headcount, functions and approval
processes. Load when a user says "we've stopped innovating", "the company feels bureaucratic",
"how do I balance running the business with building the next thing", or when a scaling business
is setting its innovation strategy, hiring plan or reward system.

## Core ideas
- Entrepreneurial ventures *are* innovation: a technical advance (a thin, high-performance
  monitor), a new service (smartphone taxi hailing) or an invisible process innovation (Ford's
  assembly line; cheaper, denser semiconductor processes). Newness customers value is the wedge
  into a profitable niche; it is hard to name a successful entrepreneurial firm that is bad at it.
- Big firms are seen as slow and rigid, yet Honda/Toyota (the hybrid car), 160-year-old Corning
  (smartphone glass) and 3M keep innovating. For every one of them, dozens of large firms have
  forgotten how and **buy innovation** by acquisition or licensing — usually from startups.
- Growing firms lose the spark for three reasons: **size**, **the existing-customers problem**
  and **complacency**. A new company starts free of all three; success and growth reintroduce them.
- Size forces specialization, coordination overhead and review/approval systems that frustrate
  creative people. What the founders settled over coffee now needs formal meetings; the more
  people involved, the slower the agreement and the more likely it is a compromise.
- Serving existing customers diligently has two side effects: customers protect their installed
  base and push you toward incremental upgrades (the "tyranny of served markets"), and
  leadership attention drifts to people, marketing, finance, operations and service.
- Success breeds complacency (Pascale's **paradox of success**): success plants the seeds of
  failure. Threatened incumbents typically invest *more* in the mature technology.
- Seven remedies, all leadership jobs: innovation-friendly culture, vision and strategic
  direction, personal involvement, a continually improved idea-to-commercialization process,
  portfolio thinking, hiring entrepreneurial attitudes, and an ambidextrous organization.
- Innovation must be **bounded by strategy**: creative energy and money are scarce; ideas outside
  the boundaries eat resources, get rejected before commercialization and demoralise idea people.
- Today's business runs on certainty, efficiency and cost control; tomorrow's depends on an
  uncertain, inefficient, costly innovation process. Few executives can live in both worlds, and
  the immediate business usually wins — so separate them structurally.

## Frameworks & tables
**The three challenges to innovation in growing firms** (pp. 196–198)

| Challenge | Mechanism | Symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Specialization of functions; communication/coordination gaps; review boards and approval requirements | Coffee-table decisions become multi-party meetings; compromises replace optimal solutions |
| Existing customers | (a) Tyranny of served markets: customers urge parts and incremental upgrades to protect their investments; (b) leadership focus shifts to operations | Only incremental innovation; breakthroughs left to rivals; innovation slips off the radar; customers eventually defect to more innovative rivals anyway |
| Complacency | Paradox of success: "keep doing what we're doing and all will be well" | Doubling down on the mature technology as the new one gets better and cheaper monthly (sail vs steam; gas vs electric light; piston vs jet) |

**Seven levers for keeping the spirit alive** (chapter structure, restated on p. 210)
1. Preserve an innovation-friendly culture.
2. Establish vision and strategic direction.
3. Be personally involved with innovation.
4. Continually improve the idea-to-commercialization process.
5. Apply portfolio thinking.
6. Hire people with entrepreneurial attitudes.
7. Create an ambidextrous organization.

**Three culture levers** (p. 199): the physical environment; risk taking and learning;
incentives and rewards.

**Physical-environment tactics** (pp. 199–200)
- Notice where people already gather informally (coffee machine, kitchen, copy room) and make
  those spots inviting: comfortable chairs, staircases wide enough to stop and chat, beanbags in
  conference rooms, snacks every week or two with an invitation to just talk.
- Put creativity tools in unexpected places: whiteboards, markers and flip charts in the
  kitchen; crayons and white paper on conference tables to invite doodling and diagrams.
- Build in play (foosball, stress relievers) — the conscious mind takes a break and returns with
  a fresh approach.
- Balance open spaces with quiet zones: a silent company library, rooms whose doors shut out
  distraction.

**Risk-and-learning tactics** (pp. 200–201)
- Communicate that reasonable risks are not only acceptable but necessary; innovation is normal
  business, not an exception.
- Share knowledge across the organization; tightly controlled information cannot recombine.
- Foster communities of interest across functions; send people to professional meetings,
  conferences, customer visits and experts.

**Four types of rewards that sustain inventive effort** (pp. 201–202)

| Reward | Nature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Recognition | Progress is a powerful motivator | Public announcement or award; a senior executive thanks the team; recognise people who worked outside their preferred style or function |
| Control | Autonomy and involvement | Involve people in decisions that affect them; let the team choose the next marketing opportunity; assign progressively harder projects matching their interests |
| Celebration | Shared applause | Small party; toast a product launch; team dinner after the redesigned website ships |
| Rejuvenation | Time away | Extra vacation days for a win (e.g. taking the core cereal brand into a new international market); industry conferences to build skills and return energised |

Extrinsic rewards (pay, vacation, recognition) target a goal distinct from the work; intrinsic
rewards target self-actualization, challenge, deep interest, curiosity and enjoyment. Use both.
**Idea champions**: leaders' visible attention signals importance, and executive backing gives a
project moral support, protection and resources.

**Attributes of a good innovation process** (pp. 204–205)
1. Generates a sufficient number of good ideas.
2. Free of bottlenecks that impede development and frustrate innovators.
3. Free of politics.
4. Encourages calculated risk taking.
5. Not arbitrary.
6. Creates cheap failures.
7. Channels resources to the worthiest projects.
8. Involves people who understand the company's capabilities, strategy and customers.

**Figure 12-1 — Innovation portfolio map** (pp. 205–207)
- Horizontal axis: market/technology characteristics, **Mature → New**.
- Vertical axis: technical challenge, uncertainty and economic opportunity, **Low → High**.
- Each circle is a funded project; circle size = resources currently committed.
- Lower-left = cautious: mature tech and market, least challenge, least risk, least upside.
- Upper-right = aggressive: new markets, high technical risk, greatest economic opportunity.
- Typical picture: the biggest circles sit cautiously in the lower-left; the small ones in the
  upper-right carry the future.

**Qualities of entrepreneurial new hires** (p. 208)
- Comfortable with change.
- View unmet needs as opportunities.
- Adopt appropriate time horizons.
- Comfortable with failure.
- Experimental mindset.
- Enjoy collaborative work.
- Think and act like entrepreneurs.
Plus: think beyond their own role, understand industry patterns, internalise the strategy and
connect it to their work. Managers and supervisors must be comfortable with participative
management.

**The ambidextrous organization** (Tushman & O'Reilly, p. 209) — an organization that can
"get today's work done more effectively and anticipate tomorrow's discontinuities". Christensen
and Govindarajan: house discontinuous innovation in a separate area with its own goals, values
and processes, so high-performing operators are not distracted and innovators are not judged by
operating metrics.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Any "yes" on the seven creative-edge questions** (below) → the culture needs a serious
  evaluation and adjustment; start with environment, risk/learning and rewards.
- **Portfolio skew**: most projects and resources in the lower-left → too risk-averse, doing too
  little about future opportunities, technologies and markets. Most in the upper-right → very,
  perhaps dangerously, aggressive. Define and articulate the balance you want.
- **Set strategy boundaries for ideas** (e.g. an active-women's-apparel e-commerce site welcomes
  ideas inside "better connections with our customers" and "fast and accurate order
  fulfillment"). Set them right and creative energy focuses itself where the payoff is highest.
- **Cannibalize yourself before someone else does.** Apple let the iPhone eat iPod revenue; Apple
  as a whole gained.
- **Don't let operational humdrum detach you from innovation.** Leaders can't make R&D decisions
  in a vacuum or treat innovation as a mysterious force; know the technical hurdles and the
  pipeline.
- **Ad hoc innovation is waste.** Once you have grown past the informal stage you need a process
  for generating, experimenting, evaluating, selecting and commercializing ideas.
- **No friends or yes-people** on the leadership team or board; you need blunt feedback before
  investing in an idea.
- **Cut losses like a poker player**: you can't win without playing, but don't play every hand to
  the end.
- **Double-check assumptions** — a rosy case is a disaster if they are unrealistic.
- **Hire managers who accept participative management**; anything else grows the hierarchy and
  bureaucracy that kill the spirit. Liberal-arts backgrounds (big ideas, complexity, ambiguity,
  writing, communication) can suit innovative roles.
- **Separate the future business from the current one** structurally rather than asking one
  team to be certain and efficient *and* exploratory.
- Too many ideas outside strategy = money lost and idea generators' energy dissipated; the fix is
  a clear mission, not fewer ideas. Reid Hoffman: blitzscaling orgs are chaotic, held together by
  "excitement about what's happening and the vision of a great future".

## Process / steps
**Culture check and repair** (p. 199)
1. Answer the seven creative-edge questions honestly.
2. If any is yes, audit the physical environment, risk taking/learning, and incentives/rewards.
3. Apply the tactics above; establish a reward system using all four reward types.

**Portfolio thinking** (pp. 205–207)
1. List every funded project with its market/tech newness, technical challenge and resources.
2. Plot them on the Figure 12-1 map, sizing circles by resources.
3. Read the distribution (lower-left heavy = risk-averse; upper-right heavy = over-aggressive).
4. Decide the risk-to-reward balance that suits the company.
5. Articulate that balance to employees and investors; rebalance funding.
6. Make each investment decision using the "Tips for making good innovation decisions" box.

**Leader involvement routine** (pp. 203–204)
1. Visit the research/product people regularly.
2. Have lunch with project teams.
3. Get to know key people one-on-one.
4. Understand the technical hurdles between appealing ideas and commercialization.
Payoffs: signals that innovation matters; lets you restate strategic direction and boundaries;
keeps you current on technology, customer and market trends.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Is the company losing its creative edge?** (p. 199; any yes = act)
- Is our current success making us self-satisfied and complacent?
- Are we inwardly focused?
- Do we punish risk takers who fail?
- Are creative people and new ideas unwelcome or unappreciated here?
- Do we fail to reward acts of creativity?
- Do we handle new ideas too bureaucratically?
- Are hierarchy and its symbols creeping into our culture?

**Innovation process scorecard** — rate the current process on each of the eight attributes
(sufficient ideas; no bottlenecks; no politics; calculated risk; not arbitrary; cheap failures;
resources to the worthiest; involves people who know capabilities, strategy and customers).

**Portfolio questions** (p. 207)
- Where do most projects and most resources sit on the map?
- What would constitute a suitable risk-to-reward balance for this company?
- Can you articulate that balance to employees and investors?

**Box — Tips for making good innovation decisions** (p. 207)
- Exclude friends and "yes people" from the leadership team and board.
- Surround yourself with complementary skills and different ways of analysing; listen even when
  you disagree.
- Learn when to cut your losses.
- Double-check your assumptions.

**Entrepreneurial-hire screen** (p. 208) — score candidates on the seven qualities, on thinking
beyond their role, and (for managers) on comfort with participative management.

**IBM warning signs** (Tushman & O'Reilly, pp. 198–199): inward focus; extensive consensus and
"push back" procedures; arrogance bred by past success; a sense of entitlement to jobs without a
quid pro quo. If these describe you, your best innovators are already looking elsewhere.

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Honda and Toyota** — introduced the hybrid car, matching a real need for emissions and fuel
  savings — large firms can still make the biggest innovation of a half-century.
- **Corning / 3M** — decade-after-decade serial innovators — age is not the problem; culture is.
- **IBM before Gerstner** — inward focus, consensus procedures, arrogance, entitlement — the
  culture on which innovation "fell on infertile soil".
- **Sail vs steam, gas vs electric lighting (1800s), piston vs jet (1940s)** — incumbents kept
  improving the old technology — the complacency reflex.
- **Apple iPhone vs iPod** — deliberate self-cannibalization; iPod revenue fell, Apple won.
- **PayPal, Google, eBay, Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter** (Hoffman) — chaotic blitzscaling orgs
  held together by excitement and vision — vision is the glue when roles are fuzzy.
- **Henry Ford's assembly line; semiconductor process advances** — innovation customers never
  see still builds the advantage.

## Pitfalls
- Slavishly giving existing customers what they ask for and ceding breakthroughs to rivals.
- Letting operations consume all leadership attention until innovation drops off the radar.
- Punishing failed risk-takers; rewarding nothing; handling ideas bureaucratically.
- Allowing hierarchy and its symbols to creep in; hiring command-and-control managers.
- Running innovation ad hoc, politically or arbitrarily; funding by who shouts loudest.
- A portfolio that is all lower-left (safe) or all upper-right (reckless).
- Ideas without strategic boundaries: wasted money and demoralised idea people.
- A board of friends; playing losing hands to the end; unchecked assumptions.
- Asking the operating team to also own discontinuous innovation.

## Key terms
`tyranny of served markets` — existing customers' pressure to keep supplying the old business, which traps a vendor in incremental innovation.
`paradox of success` — Pascale's observation that success plants the seeds of eventual failure via complacency.
`idea champion` — an influential insider whose visible backing gives a project attention, protection and resources.
`intrinsic vs extrinsic rewards` — motivation from the work itself (challenge, curiosity) vs from goals distinct from it (pay, vacation, recognition).
`communities of interest` — cross-organization groups with shared passions who exchange ideas.
`portfolio thinking` — managing all funded projects as a set with risk-versus-return characteristics.
`ambidextrous organization` — one that executes today's business efficiently while anticipating tomorrow's discontinuities (Tushman & O'Reilly).
`discontinuous innovation` — radically new concepts aimed at creating new markets, as opposed to incremental improvement.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Creative-edge audit** — seven yes/no questions → red/amber/green culture score + which of
  the three levers to work on first → early warning as headcount passes ~30–50.
- **Innovation portfolio mapper** — per project: market newness, technical challenge, resources
  → Figure 12-1 bubble chart + skew diagnosis (risk-averse / balanced / over-aggressive) and a
  target-balance statement to share with the team and investors.
- **Innovation process scorecard** — rate the eight attributes 1–5 → weakest links and fixes
  (e.g. stage gates for cheap failures) → turns ad hoc innovation into a repeatable process.
- **Reward planner** — pick a win, choose from recognition/control/celebration/rejuvenation →
  a concrete reward action list so creative work keeps getting reinforced.
- **Entrepreneurial-hire scorecard** — interview rubric on the seven qualities + participative
  management → hiring that preserves the culture as the team grows.
- **Strategy boundary setter** — define 2–3 innovation boundaries from the mission → an
  idea-intake filter that tags proposals in/out of bounds before they consume resources.
- **Founder involvement cadence** — schedule lab visits, team lunches, 1:1s → streak tracker so
  operations doesn't crowd out innovation.
- **Ambidexterity check** — do future-business projects have separate goals, metrics, values
  and processes? → prompts to spin up a separate unit when the answer is no.

## Summing up (book)
- Growth challenges the entrepreneurial spirit: size brings specialization, communication
  problems and control systems that frustrate creativity.
- Once a company has customers, the tyranny of served markets can block innovation.
- Success breeds complacency, the opposite of the entrepreneurial spirit.
- Set the strategic direction within which innovation should happen.
- Leaders keep the spirit alive by (1) preserving an innovation-friendly culture, (2) setting
  strategic direction, (3) staying personally involved, (4) continually improving the
  idea-to-commercialization process, (5) applying portfolio thinking, (6) hiring entrepreneurial
  attitudes, and (7) building an ambidextrous organization that runs today and anticipates
  tomorrow.
