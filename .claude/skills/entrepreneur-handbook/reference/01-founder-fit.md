# Ch 1 — Is Starting a Business Right for You?
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 1, book pages 11–20.

## Purpose
Self-diagnosis before committing: do you have the traits, background and motivation to start
and run a business? Load when a user asks "should I start a business", "am I founder material",
or when onboarding a new user into a launch journey.

## Core ideas
- Success is **personal qualities × quality planning**. Great qualities with a poor plan fails;
  a great plan with weak motivation or fear of uncertainty also fails.
- The three must-haves: **a solid plan, the ability to execute it, and high motivation** that
  makes business success an important personal goal.
- Not required: a genius "killer idea" (most successful startups begin with incremental
  innovation), fearlessness (prosperous founders have a *healthy* aversion to risk), or technical
  business know-how (learn as you go, or enlist an experienced co-owner).
- Ideas alone are one step; the process also needs people skills, work style and financial savvy.
- Self-scoring tests (e.g. the US SBA "Is entrepreneurship for you?" test) are a useful rough
  gauge, not a verdict.
- Entrepreneurship runs in families and social circles; exposure to a small business is a
  meaningful predictor.

## Frameworks & tables
**Table 1-1 — Common entrepreneurial traits (five clusters)**

| Cluster | Traits |
|---|---|
| Ideas and drive | Creativity; vision; ability to identify opportunities; passion |
| People skills | Leadership; persuasion; influence; network building; ability to excite people by vision; ability to close a deal; ability to listen, trust, take advice |
| Work style | Goal oriented; comfortable with uncertainty; self-challenging; solitary (prefer being own boss); rarely satisfied or complacent, can't sit still; driven to plan and be prepared; experimental mindset (OK starting small, moving past failures); perseverance in adversity; continuously looks for a better way |
| Financial savvy | Comfortable with finance; comfortable with financial governance |
| Entrepreneurial background | Family members started businesses; friends started businesses; worked at a small business or startup |

**The entrepreneurial process** (Gergen & Vanourek): understand a problem → grasp its full
context → connect previously unconnected dots → have the vision, courage, resourcefulness and
persistence to see the solution through.

**The investor's three questions** (Baehr & Loomis) — in any funding meeting the investor asks:
1. Do I like you? 2. Do I trust you? 3. Do I want to do business with you?

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Passion is not a predictor.** Research: passion doesn't correlate with success years out, and
  it fades within the first months. Professional funders prefer strong preparation and a calm
  demeanor; passion only helps with less experienced funders (relatives, semi-pro angels).
  "Passion is an emotion that blinds you" (Isenberg): too much attachment stops you seeing
  problems and correcting course.
- **Start small and experiment** before locking in a business model to scale; test product,
  price and service with real customers before spending large capital.
- **Decide under uncertainty.** Corporate managers wait for more information; an entrepreneur
  must make the best of uncertainty and move. Standing still is not an option.
- **Fail fast beats failing long and slow.** "Any fool can carry on, but only the wise man knows
  how to shorten sail" (Conrad, via Isenberg).
- **Listen to complaints.** The most common selling mistake of new founders is discounting
  customer criticism because of passion and ego.
- **Know your deal limits.** Successful founders know exactly what they must give up and what
  they can get away with when closing under pressure (Kuemmerle).
- **Stretch rules creatively, not legally.** Freedom from normal rules is a competitive advantage
  (Hoffman), but skirting regulation or tolerating misconduct produces long-lasting reputational
  damage inside and outside the company.

## Process / steps
1. Take a self-assessment (SBA test or the Table 1-1 clusters).
2. Weigh background: family/friends who founded businesses, time spent inside a small business.
3. Confirm the three must-haves: plan, ability to execute, motivation.
4. If a cluster is weak, decide the mitigation: learn it (finance → appendixes A and B), or
   bring in a co-owner who has it.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
Founder-fit self-check (score each 1–5, group by cluster):
- I can spot problems others miss and imagine new solutions.
- I have a clear vision of the change I want to make.
- I am comfortable reaching out to strangers (customers, investors, hires) with confident optimism.
- I can persuade and lead; people get excited by my vision.
- I can close a deal under pressure and know my walk-away points.
- I listen to criticism and change course when the evidence says so.
- I challenge and motivate myself without a boss.
- I am comfortable acting with incomplete information.
- I prefer starting small and testing to betting big up front.
- I persevere through adversity but can recognise a dead end.
- I am comfortable reading financial statements and governing finances.
- Family or friends have started businesses; I have worked in a small business or startup.
External: SBA "Is entrepreneurship for you?" quiz (sba.gov).

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Airbnb** (intro) — roommates rented air mattresses, funded via novelty cereal, joined Y
  Combinator, learned by using their own service, fixed listing photos — iterate and learn.
- **Webvan** — built a 330,000 sq ft automated warehouse, raised $850M+, started 26 more, never
  broke even, bankrupt in two years — too much too fast; experiment first.
- **Uber / Airbnb regulation** — rule-bending fuelled growth but drew lasting scrutiny — creativity
  without disregarding civil society.
- **Paul Newman, Jim Koch (Boston Beer), Dan Bricklin (VisiCalc)** — grew up in business-owning
  families — background shapes entrepreneurial drive.

## Pitfalls
- Treating passion as the main qualification.
- Waiting for more information instead of deciding.
- Ignoring customer complaints because of ego.
- Carrying on with a failing project instead of shortening sail.
- Mistaking rule-breaking for strategy.

## Key terms
`entrepreneur` — someone who perceives an opportunity **and creates an organization to pursue it** (Bygrave).
`creative destruction` — Schumpeter's term for entrepreneurs sweeping away old technologies and ways of doing things.

## Summing up (book)
- Ideas matter but are not sufficient; consider background, inclinations, motivation and skills.
- Suitability tests exist; use them as a rough gauge only.
- Entrepreneurship runs in families; children of business owners are more likely to start or buy one.
