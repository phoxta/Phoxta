# Ch 8 — Angel Investment and Venture Capital
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 8, book pages 131–148.

## Purpose
Which outside equity source, if any, fits a venture — angel investors or venture capital — how to
find and pitch them, what they take in return (ownership, convertible preferred stock, board
influence) and what it really costs. Load when a user asks "should I raise money", "angel vs VC",
"how do I find investors", "what is a term sheet / preferred stock / dilution", or when the journey
reaches the seed, Series A or Series B step.

## Core ideas
- Most businesses never need outside equity: internally generated cash plus loans can grow them to
  a size the founders find satisfactory, with no ownership sold. Only firms with broader growth
  opportunities must seek outside equity, because debt and internal cash rarely fund that growth (p. 131).
- Equity capital buys ownership rights, a share of future fortunes and, in most cases, a voice in
  how the business is run. Accepting it means ceding some measure of control (p. 131).
- Silicon Valley vocabulary: **seed stage** (startup phase) → **Series A** (gearing up to release
  the first product; often angels) → **Series B** (continued growth; probably VC) (p. 132).
- Angels, not VCs, are the realistic source for most founders: fewer than 1% of US companies have
  ever raised VC; angels fund 16× as many companies and the VC share is shrinking (p. 132).
- Angels are high-net-worth, usually self-made businesspeople or professionals investing their own
  money as debt, equity or both; they also bring advice, feedback and local networks (pp. 132–134).
- VCs are high-risk investors who take a significant ownership stake plus a board seat in
  high-potential companies, expect ~10× within 5–10 years, and harvest at IPO or sale (pp. 136–141).
- Both audiences weigh **people over idea**: founder credibility, character, trustworthiness and
  coachability outrank competence and traction (pp. 136, 138, 144).
- VC is the most expensive capital you can use — especially if you succeed (eBay) — and it comes
  with loss of control; self-fund or delay wherever you can (pp. 145–147).
- The more solid and less desperate the business when you negotiate, the better the terms (p. 147).

## Frameworks & tables
**Funding-round vocabulary** (p. 132)

| Round | When | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| Seed | Startup phase of fund-raising | Founders; friends and relatives (considered before angels) |
| Series A | Gearing up to release the first product | Angel investors — the first stage of serious equity |
| Series B | Company keeps growing and needs scale capital | Venture capital |

**Angels vs. VCs at a glance** (compiled from pp. 132–143)

| | Angel investors | Venture capitalists |
|---|---|---|
| Who | High-net-worth individuals, usually successful businesspeople/professionals; often self-made millionaires investing their own money | Firms structured as limited partnerships investing other people's money (pension funds, endowments, corporations, insurers, wealthy individuals) |
| Round | Series A | Series B |
| Fit | Firms too small for VCs, too limited in revenue at maturity, or too risky for banks and most VCs; any industry, any region | Risky firms with proven management and substantial growth potential, mostly tech-linked (software, hardware, biotech, medical devices, media/entertainment); often pre-product; IPO-bound |
| Scale (2014) | ~$24.1B across 73,400 deals | ~$48.3B across 4,356 deals |
| Instrument | Debt, equity or both | Convertible preferred stock (or convertible debt with warrants) |
| What they take | An ownership interest; offer advice and a local network | A significant % of ownership plus a board seat; a role in strategic management; may hold enough control to fire you |
| Return goal | Exceptional returns; want a credible exit | ~10× within 5–10 years; harvest at IPO/acquisition after 4–5 years |
| Visibility | Don't advertise; keep activity inside trusted circles | Highly visible; hunt deals at MIT labs, Stanford, accelerators/incubators, Silicon Valley, Research Triangle, San Diego biotech |
| Decision style | Solo angels decide fast; angel groups are more professional but more bureaucratic and slower | Syndicates: the lead investor does due diligence and takes the board seat; others contribute smaller passive cheques |
| How to find | AngelList; Angel Capital Association directory; referrals via lawyer, accountant, entrepreneurs, patent attorneys | Pratt's Guide; VCgate; Forbes Midas List; top accelerators; entrepreneurial forums; warm intros |
| What sways them | The people: founder profile (school, prestigious prior employers), coachability, character over competence | A first-rate person with a good idea beats a good idea with second-rate management; "to the moon" potential |

**The three kinds of firms angels finance** (p. 133)
1. Too small to get the attention of VCs.
2. Too limited in revenue potential at maturity to interest VCs.
3. Too risky for bank loans and for most VC appetites.

**What angels actually decide on** (Bernstein, Korteweg & Laws study of AngelList, p. 136)
- Ranking of signals: **founders' profiles** (school, previous work at prestigious firms) > initial
  traction > initial investors.
- Credibility hypothesis: if a Harvard MBA is betting a career on this rather than other attractive
  options, there must be something to it.
- Related research: angels weigh **coachability**, and **trustworthiness and character over competence**.

**Figure 8-1 — The flow of venture capital** (Bygrave & Timmons, p. 140)

| Actor | Role | Receives |
|---|---|---|
| Limited partners — pension funds, individuals, corporations, insurance companies, foreign investors, endowments | Provide most of the capital; venture is a small slice of their portfolios | Return of principal + 75–85% of capital gain |
| Gatekeepers | Intermediaries between LPs and funds | 1% annual fee |
| VC firm (general partner: new MBAs, securities lawyers, experienced deal makers) | Identify and screen opportunities; transact and close deals; monitor and add value; raise additional funds | 2–3% annual fee + 15–25% of capital gains |
| Portfolio companies (entrepreneurs) | Opportunity creation/recognition and execution; value creation; harvest | Equity capital; return cash to the fund via IPOs, mergers, alliances |

**Portfolio math** (p. 140): a VC diversifies across many deals knowing some will fail, others only
break even, and maybe **1 in 15** is the bonanza. Syndicates spread the bets further.

**Convertible preferred stock — what the VC gets** (pp. 140–141)

| Feature | Meaning for the founder |
|---|---|
| Voting rights | The VC gains a measure of control over the enterprise and its officers |
| Conversion right | VC may convert to common at its discretion, at 1:1 or another stipulated ratio — so it is really taking ownership |
| Cumulative dividends | Must be paid before any dividend reaches common shareholders |
| Liquidation preference | Preferred stands ahead of common if the company is liquidated — downside protection |
| Net effect | "Best of both worlds": protection if you fail, full upside if you succeed |
| Common alternative | Convertible debt with warrants |

**The three things a VC does after investing** (p. 141)
1. Monitors the progress of its portfolio companies.
2. Uses its network of contacts to strengthen their technical and management teams.
3. Shapes company plans and strategies through its influence on their boards.

**Balachandra's three presentation rules** (Babson research, p. 144)

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Maintain a calm demeanor | Professional funders equate equanimity with leadership strength; passion helps only with less formal sources |
| Build trust | They assess character more than competence — skills are teachable or hirable, personality barely changes; projected trustworthiness raised funding odds by 10% |
| Listen actively; show openness to new ideas | Early-stage investors want to coach and mould you; ego is a red flag |

**Mullins' four downsides of venture capital** (p. 145)
1. **Distraction from your day job** — fund-raising is another full-time job.
2. **Onerous terms** — VCs protect themselves; the term sheet's concise language becomes legal detail
   that may be worse than you expected.
3. **Burdensome advice** — you must take the funder's advice whether or not you agree; little evidence it works.
4. **Dilution of ownership and returns** — VC can be the most expensive form of capital you use.

**Four ways to avoid or delay outside equity** (pp. 138–139)
1. Rely as heavily as possible on bootstrap financing — it gives up no ownership.
2. Manage growth at a pace existing financing can handle.
3. Be tightfisted: keep expenses low and do more with less.
4. Outsource non-essential functions — the modern economy has contractors for every part of a business.

### Box: Corporate venture capital (p. 137)
Large organisations invest in startups as a strategic move: intelligence on what's new in the
industry plus a first look at a company they might acquire. US corporate VCs grew from 1,068 (2011)
to 1,501 (2015) and their dollars invested quintupled to $75B. They increasingly demand financial
performance as well as strategic value. (Source: "Corporate VCs Are Moving the Goalposts", HBR, Nov 2016.)

### Box: An alternative to venture capital (p. 139)
GoViral (Claus Moseholm, Denmark, 2005) funded steady growth from the proceeds of one viral video
campaign after another, became Europe's leading platform for such content, and sold in 2011 for
$97M without a single krone of investment capital — grown entirely on customers' cash.
(Source: John Mullins, "VC Funding Can Be Bad for Your Start-Up", HBR.org, Aug 2014.)

### Box: VC locators (p. 143)
- **Pratt's Guide to Private Equity & Venture Capital Sources** (ed. Stanley E. Pratt) —
  comprehensive, organised so you can filter by characteristics and interests; ~$1,000; print and
  digital; updated periodically.
- **VCgate** — directory of ~3,800 VC, private-equity, merchant-banking and other investment firms
  across the US, Canada, Europe and Asia.
- **Forbes Midas List** — annual ranking of the world's individual VCs.
- Non-US note: AngelList, the Angel Capital Association directory and Pratt's Guide are US-centric;
  use your national angel association or VC association directory as the local equivalent.

### Box: How much of your company should the VC firm get? (p. 146)
- The VC's convertible instrument is really a share of ownership. Should $5M buy 20%? 40%? 51%?
  It hinges entirely on the **estimated value of the firm**.
- If the VC says "we've estimated the value of your company at $6 million", ask for a detailed
  explanation of how that figure was determined.
- Valuation is part science, part art, and the VC is far more experienced at both — a negotiating advantage.
- Level the playing field: bring in professional help to build your own assessment of enterprise
  value (valuation methods are in appendix C).

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Don't sell equity if you don't have to.** If internal cash plus loans can reach the size you
  want, stay self-funded (p. 131).
- **VC only for "to the moon" ventures.** If you cannot credibly return ~10× in 5–10 years, the
  VC search will be fruitless (pp. 137–138); don't pursue VC unless you have *all* the characteristics
  they look for (p. 147).
- **Plan on angels.** <1% of US companies raise VC; angels fund 16× more companies; ~300,000 US
  angels put ~$25B into 71,000+ ventures in 2015 (pp. 132–134).
- **Angels are getting pickier.** Deal count dipped in 2015–16 as selectivity rose — arrive with
  your act together (p. 135).
- **Angel investing is growing abroad**: Europe's market doubled 2011–16; Canada's tripled (p. 134).
- **AngelList gate**: post your profile only once at least a third (better, half) of your target is
  committed by quality investors; their due diligence draws the rest (Baehr & Loomis, p. 134).
  Startups raise >$10M a month through the platform.
- **Local or regional business → find a local angel** (p. 134).
- **Target angels whose wealth came from your industry**: a Sun Microsystems founder wrote Google a
  $100,000 cheque after a prelaunch demo because he could see the technology's potential; a
  medical-device startup should get the word out to local physicians (p. 135).
- **Angel groups**: easier to find and contact, deeper expertise and more money, but more
  bureaucratic and slower than solo angels (p. 135).
- **Lead time**: line up venture money **6–8 months** before it is needed; never wait until you
  really need the cash (p. 142).
- **Send the summary, not the plan**: executive summary, YouTube pitch link or brief deck; the full
  plan only when asked — investors don't have time to read it (p. 142).
- **Blind emails ≈ blind résumés**: you are one of thousands; get a personal meeting or a
  recommendation from someone the VC respects (p. 142).
- **Accelerators**: graduates of *top* programmes raise VC faster; not true across the board — be
  selective (p. 142).
- **Forum pitch = 5–10 minutes per VC table**: bring a brief, compelling elevator speech and be
  totally prepared (pp. 142–143).
- **Choose team, advisers, board and attorney partly for their financier connections**; all else
  equal, pick board members with personal ties to financiers (p. 143).
- **Calm beats passion** with professional funders (p. 144).
- **Trustworthiness is worth +10% odds** of being funded (p. 144).
- **VC economics**: 1-in-15 bonanza thinking; LPs get principal + 75–85% of gains; GPs get 15–25% of
  gains + a 2–3% annual fee; gatekeepers 1% (p. 140).
- **Harvest horizon**: VCs typically exit after 4–5 years via IPO or sale (p. 141).
- **IPO ambition → a VC is almost essential** for the sophistication, connections and terms (p. 137).
- **Always get your own valuation** before accepting the VC's number (p. 146).
- **Read the legal agreement, not just the term sheet** — the details may be worse than the summary (p. 145).
- **Negotiate from strength**: a viable business that isn't desperate gets much better terms and
  keeps more of the company and control (p. 147).

## Process / steps
**A. Finding local angels — the referral chain** (pp. 134–135)
1. Join AngelList (subject to the one-third/one-half gate) and check the Angel Capital Association's
   directory of angel groups and platforms by region.
2. Work into the local angel network through your lawyer, accountant and entrepreneur
   acquaintances; talk with patent attorneys and share your business plan.
3. Ask successful local entrepreneurs: "Whom should I approach about private financing?"
4. If that person cannot help, ask them: "Do you know anyone who might want to invest in my company?"
5. Follow every lead until you connect with the right person.

**B. Persuading an angel** (pp. 135–136)
1. Target angels in professions related to your enterprise.
2. Have your act together: a working prototype, a well-managed lean operation, or at minimum a
   rock-solid business plan.
3. Prepare a well-rehearsed, right-to-the-point verbal presentation that explains clearly and
   specifically how the money will fuel profitable growth.
4. Offer a credible exit plan — angels want paper ownership converted to real money.
5. Focus on the team: founders' profiles, coachability, trustworthiness and character.

**C. The venture-capital process** (pp. 139–141)
1. Limited partners commit capital to the VC partnership.
2. The VC firm identifies, screens and closes deals; it forms a syndicate in which the lead does due
   diligence and takes the board seat.
3. Capital arrives as convertible preferred stock (or convertible debt with warrants).
4. Post-investment: monitor progress; use the network to strengthen teams; shape strategy via the board.
5. Harvest at IPO or sale, usually after 4–5 years; proceeds split between LPs and GPs per the
   partnership terms.

**D. Connecting with VCs** (pp. 141–143)
1. Start 6–8 months before the cash is needed.
2. Shortlist firms that specialise in your industry (Pratt's Guide, VCgate, Midas List) and read
   each firm's application instructions.
3. Email the executive summary, pitch-video link or brief deck — not the whole plan; an intrigued
   VC will ask for the thorough version.
4. Work for a warm route: a top accelerator; entrepreneurial forums (5–10-minute table pitches);
   team members, board members or an attorney the VC knows and respects.
5. On any contact, ask for an office meeting to pitch; deliver the highlights, hold details for questions.

**E. Making the presentation** (pp. 144–145)
1. Assume they have read your materials — the meeting is to answer questions, assuage concerns and
   let them get to know you.
2. Stay calm; project trustworthiness; listen actively and show openness to coaching.
3. Rehearse until you have it down cold; convey control of the facts and confidence in the future.
4. Anticipate pointed questions and prepare rock-solid answers for each.

**F. If you would rather not raise** (pp. 138–139): bootstrap → pace growth to financing → stay
tightfisted → outsource non-essentials.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Do I need outside equity at all?**
- Can internal cash plus loans grow the business to a size I would find satisfactory?
- Is my growth opportunity so broad that debt and internal cash cannot fund it?
- Am I willing to give an outsider ownership, a share of future profits and a voice in decisions?
- Have I exhausted bootstrapping, growth pacing, cost discipline and outsourcing?
- Could customers fund the growth (the GoViral model)?

**Angel-readiness checklist**
- [ ] Solid business plan and the know-how to launch and operate the company.
- [ ] Working prototype, lean operation, or a rock-solid plan.
- [ ] Rehearsed, right-to-the-point verbal pitch stating exactly how the money will be used.
- [ ] Credible exit plan for investors.
- [ ] Team story: schools, prior employers, evidence of coachability and character.
- [ ] Target list of angels whose wealth came from my industry or region.
- [ ] Referral chain started (lawyer, accountant, entrepreneurs, patent attorneys).
- [ ] AngelList profile ready, posted only once a third to a half is committed.

**VC-fit checklist — do I have *all* of these?**
- [ ] Potential to return ~10× within 5–10 years.
- [ ] Proven management (first-rate person plus a good idea).
- [ ] Substantial growth potential in a VC-favoured industry (software, hardware, biotech, medical
      devices, media/entertainment) or an equally scalable one.
- [ ] Willing to accept significant dilution, a board seat and strategic involvement.
- [ ] Comfortable with a 4–5-year harvest via IPO or sale.
- [ ] Aligned with a fast flip rather than a long private build.
- [ ] Started 6–8 months ahead of need.

**Pitch-meeting prep**
- [ ] Executive summary / video link / brief deck sent per the firm's instructions.
- [ ] Elevator speech (the opportunity and how the team will exploit it) for 5–10-minute forum slots.
- [ ] Presentation customisable for length and audience; highlights first, details on demand.
- [ ] Rehearsed cold; calm delivery; trust signals; active listening.
- [ ] List of anticipated pointed questions with rock-solid answers.
- [ ] Well-connected team member, board member or attorney to secure the intro.

**Questions to ask a VC before signing** (pp. 145–147)
- How exactly did you arrive at the valuation? (Then commission your own professional valuation.)
- What ownership percentage does the instrument imply after conversion, and at what ratio?
- What voting rights, cumulative dividends and liquidation preferences come with the preferred stock?
- Which term-sheet lines will expand into onerous legal detail?
- Who takes the board seat and what control rights follow — including the power to replace me?
- What is your exit horizon, and does it match my plan to stay private and build long-term?

**Networking script**
- "Whom should I approach about private financing?"
- "Do you know anyone who might want to invest in my company?"

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Jack, Minneapolis** (p. 133) — 62-year-old owner of a $43M-revenue trucking and maintenance
  company, minority stakes in two local firms, invests locally in what he understands, often with
  two wealthy friends ("Three minds are better than one") — the typical angel is a self-made local
  operator, not a Valley tech investor.
- **Google / Sun Microsystems founder** (p. 135) — a $100,000 cheque after a prelaunch demo because
  he grasped the technology — target angels from your industry.
- **Google, Amazon, Starbucks, PayPal** (p. 133) — all began with angel money.
- **Arthur Rock / Apple** (p. 138) — $1.5M invested, valued at $100M at the IPO three years later —
  the payoff VCs live for; without that potential, VC won't bite.
- **eBay / Benchmark** (pp. 145–146) — $6.7M for 22% in 1997; a bank would have earned ~$2M in
  interest over three years, Benchmark made more than $2B; the CEO recruiting it provided could be
  bought for under $200K and IPO advice for a consulting fee — VC is most expensive precisely when you succeed.
- **GoViral** (p. 139) — sold for $97M in 2011 with zero investment capital — customer cash can fund
  a category leader.
- **Corporate VC** (p. 137) — 1,068 → 1,501 US corporate VCs, $75B invested — a growing but
  increasingly return-driven source.

## Pitfalls
- Chasing VC without VC-grade potential or the full profile VCs demand.
- Sending the full business plan, or blind emails, to VCs.
- Waiting until cash is critical instead of starting 6–8 months ahead.
- Posting on AngelList before anchor investors have committed.
- Pitching with passion instead of calm; letting ego signal uncoachability.
- Treating the pitch as a first exposure rather than a Q&A on materials already read.
- Accepting the VC's valuation without an independent assessment.
- Signing a legal agreement whose details are worse than the term sheet.
- Underestimating control loss: preferred voting rights, board influence, the power to fire the
  founder, pressure to flip via IPO or sale ("venture vultures").
- Negotiating from desperation.
- Assuming any accelerator will open VC doors.
- Letting fund-raising swallow the day job.

## Key terms
`seed stage` — the startup phase of fund-raising.
`Series A` — the round raised while gearing up to release the first product; often angels.
`Series B` — the growth round after Series A; probably venture capital.
`angel investor` — a high-net-worth individual, usually a successful businessperson, providing early-stage capital as debt, equity or both.
`angel group / network` — a formal organisation of angels with professional screeners; more depth and capital, slower decisions.
`venture capital (VC)` — an individual or firm seeking large capital gains through early-stage equity or equity-linked financing of high-potential firms.
`corporate venture capital` — a large company investing in startups for strategic insight and acquisition options, increasingly for returns too.
`limited partner (LP)` — passive capital provider to a VC fund (pension fund, endowment, corporation, insurer, wealthy individual).
`general partner (GP)` — the VC firm that manages the fund and invests; earns a 2–3% annual fee plus 15–25% of gains.
`gatekeeper` — intermediary between LPs and funds; ~1% annual fee.
`portfolio company` — a firm a VC has invested in.
`syndicate` — a group of VC firms co-investing; the lead investor does due diligence and takes the board seat.
`due diligence` — the investor's investigation of the company before committing.
`convertible preferred stock` — preferred shares with voting rights, cumulative dividends, liquidation priority and the right to convert to common at a set ratio.
`conversion ratio` — the stipulated preferred-to-common exchange rate (e.g. 1:1).
`cumulative dividends` — dividends owed to preferred holders that accrue and must be paid before any common dividend.
`liquidation preference` — preferred holders are paid before common holders if the company is wound up.
`convertible debt with warrants` — a loan convertible into equity, packaged with rights to buy shares; the common alternative to convertible preferred.
`harvest` — when the VC cashes out via IPO or sale of the company, typically after 4–5 years.
`dilution` — the reduction in the founders' ownership share when equity is issued to investors.
`term sheet` — the concise summary of deal terms that later expands into the detailed legal agreement.
`valuation` — the estimated value of the firm that determines what share a given investment buys; part science, part art.
`bootstrap financing` — funding growth from internal cash and frugality rather than outside equity.

## Build ideas for the app
- **Funding-source router** — stage, growth ceiling, industry, 10×-plausibility, exit horizon,
  appetite for control loss → recommended path (self-fund/customers, angels, corporate VC, VC)
  with the chapter's fit criteria → stops founders wasting months on the wrong investor type.
- **Dilution & valuation calculator** — pre-money valuation, cheque size, conversion ratio, rounds
  (seed/A/B) → post-money, ownership %, founder stake over time, plus a cost-of-capital comparison
  against a bank loan (the eBay arithmetic) → makes "should $5M buy 20% or 51%" concrete before the meeting.
- **Term-sheet decoder** — tick or paste term-sheet items (preferred stock, voting, cumulative
  dividends, liquidation preference, board seat, conversion) → plain-English impact plus questions
  to ask → counters the VC's experience advantage.
- **Investor-readiness checklists** — angel and VC variants from this chapter → readiness score and
  gap list → gate before the outreach module.
- **Referral-chain tracker** — contacts, who introduced whom, the two scripted questions,
  follow-ups, AngelList one-third-raised gate → turns "network your way in" into a managed pipeline.
- **Raise timeline planner** — projected cash-need date → back-calculated start date (6–8 months)
  and milestones for summary, deck, forums, meetings → prevents raising from desperation.
- **Pitch rehearsal drill** — bank of pointed investor questions plus a self/peer rubric on calm,
  trust and coachability → founders who have it down cold.
- **Investor locator links** — AngelList, ACA directory, Pratt's Guide, VCgate, Midas List with
  non-US equivalents → one place to start the search.

## Summing up (book)
- The most likely source of outside venture funding is angel investors — high-net-worth individuals
  providing early-stage capital to startups.
- Networking is often the best way to connect with angels.
- Venture capital comes from an individual or firm seeking large capital gains through early-stage
  equity or equity-linked financing of high-potential enterprises.
- Don't waste time pursuing VC unless you have all the characteristics VCs look for.
- Most venture capital takes the form of convertible preferred stock or something similar, such as
  convertible debt with warrants.
- Venture capital is nice to have but costly — economically and in loss of control.
- When pitching an angel or VC, choose calm over passion and build trust in your character and coachability.
