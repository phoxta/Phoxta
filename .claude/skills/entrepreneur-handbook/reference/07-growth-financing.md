# Ch 7 — Growth-Stage Financing
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, chapter 7, book pages 115–130.

## Purpose
How to fund expansion once the business has proven itself: bank debt and what bankers test, how
much debt is safe (leverage ratios), the equity path to an IPO, what maturity-phase financing
looks like, and the rule for matching assets to financing. Load when a user asks about a bank
loan, "how much debt can I carry", lender ratios, or wants an overview of growth → IPO → maturity
funding options.

## Core ideas
- Growth needs capital for expansion, hiring and training, acquisitions and brand visibility.
  Operating cash flow helps but rarely covers strong growth.
- Proven credibility makes external capital easier. **Slow-to-moderate growth → bank debt;
  likely to grow large quickly → equity** (chapter 8).
- Banks lend **short and secured** to small firms — demand loans, seasonal lines of credit,
  single-purpose equipment loans — and avoid long-term or unsecured loans because failure rates
  are high. Small community banks approve more and satisfy more than big banks. 54% of small
  firms hold under $100,000 of debt.
- Bankers ask **three questions** (ability to repay, character, collateral) and rarely lend
  without satisfactory answers to all three.
- **Debt is the lowest-cost external capital** because interest is tax-deductible (US) — but
  that helps only once there is taxable income, and interest is a contractual obligation payable
  in bad years too.
- **Leverage** magnifies owner returns and bankruptcy risk; lenders police it with the debt,
  debt-to-equity and times-interest-earned ratios and write limits into loan covenants.
- Growth-phase companies are usually too small or unproven for an IPO; VC or angel money
  arrives only for hot industries or game-changing products. Most companies fail or are acquired
  before either.
- The **IPO** is the major milestone for the few: investment banks and their broker-dealer
  syndicates open access to millions of investors and let early holders turn paper into cash.
- **Maturity**: growth tapers, but financing continues — commercial paper, bank lines, term
  loans, sale-and-leaseback, bonds and stock — and timing the market matters.
- **Match assets to financing**: short-term assets with short-term money, long-term assets with
  long-term debt or shareholders' capital.

## Frameworks & tables
**The banker's three questions** (p. 116)
1. Will the borrower be able to pay me back? — judged from the entrepreneur's skills, the plan,
   projected cash flow and (if operating) the current and acid-test ratios.
2. Is the borrower's character such that he or she will pay me back? — judged from credit
   history: evidence you pay bills on schedule.
3. If the borrower fails, what marketable assets can I get my hands on? — collateral: current
   assets (cash, inventory, receivables) and fixed assets (vehicles, buildings, equipment); an SBA
   guarantee can stand in for the owner's assets.

**Lender ratios** (pp. 117–120)

| Ratio | Formula | What it tells a lender |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | Ability to pay bills as they come due. A business with steady, reliable cash inflows (utility, taxi firm) can live on ~1.10; a manufacturer with a long development and production cycle needs more |
| Acid-test (quick) ratio | Quick assets (cash, marketable securities, receivables) ÷ current liabilities | Absolute liquidity — inventory excluded because it can't be sold on short notice |
| Debt ratio | Total debt ÷ total assets | Degree of leverage |
| Debt-to-equity ratio | Total liabilities ÷ owners' equity | Leverage; as it rises, owner returns and risk both rise; creditors set limits beyond which loans are called |
| Times-interest-earned ratio | EBIT ÷ interest expense | How far income could fall before interest is uncovered — a test of staying power under adversity more than of solvency |

**Table 7-1 — Sources of capital for growth-stage financing** (p. 122)

| Source | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Internal cash flow from operations | Cost-free if shareholders aren't anxious for dividends | May not be enough to finance substantial growth in the productive base |
| Debt capital | Interest deductible from taxable income (if any); rate set by prevailing rates, loan term and creditworthiness | Costly; increases the riskiness of the enterprise |
| Venture capital | Comes with business advice that may be valuable — unlike any other capital | The most expensive capital: a significant share of ownership and of future prospects; the entrepreneur must share power |
| Initial public offering | Perhaps the only way to round up a large bundle of money | Dilutes the entrepreneur and earlier investors; public-company duties are onerous |

**Financing instruments across growth and maturity** (pp. 115–128)

| Instrument | Typical user | Term | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand loan / seasonal line / equipment loan | Growth-stage small firm, local bank | Short | What banks prefer to offer small firms |
| Asset-based loan or lease | Growing firm with a track record | Medium | Pledge assets as collateral; move upstream to a money-center bank once you outgrow the local one |
| SBA-guaranteed loan | US small business | Varies | See box below |
| Online lender / peer-to-peer | Small firms shut out of banks | Short | Kabbage, OnDeck, Funding Circle; Lending Club, Prosper |
| Term loan | Mature, creditworthy | Medium/long | From banks, insurance companies, pension funds; existing assets and cash flow as collateral lower the cost |
| Sale-and-leaseback | Mature | Long | Sell an owned asset, lease it back, release significant funds |
| Commercial paper | Large corporations with high credit ratings | 2–270 days | Short-term debt security sold at a discount, redeemed at face value; cheaper than short-term bank borrowing; bought by investors with idle cash |
| Bonds | Mature, public-market access | 1–30 years | IOU with fixed rate, usually paid semiannually, redeemed at face on maturity |
| Preferred stock | Mature | Perpetual | Equity paying a stated annual dividend; trades like a bond with rates and issuer credit |
| Common stock (IPO, follow-on) | Exceptional growth firms | Permanent | Via investment bank + syndicate; opens the door to individuals, mutual funds, pension funds |

**Box — SBA loans** (p. 119)
The SBA runs three loan programs for small businesses owned by US citizens. It does not lend; it
sets guidelines and its partners (lenders, community development organisations, microlenders)
make the loans. The SBA guarantees repayment up to certain levels, removing part of the lender's
risk; post-2008 legislation made the loans more attractive to both sides. Details at sba.gov.
Non-US note: look for the local government-guaranteed small-business loan scheme (e.g. the UK's
Start Up Loans / British Business Bank guarantees).

**Why growth tapers — four causes** (p. 122)
1. Success and profitability draw competitors into the market.
2. Demand is largely satisfied (market saturation).
3. A shift in the technology of the product — or the technology customers use.
4. The larger organisation loses ambition, agility or the ability to innovate.

**The matching principle** (p. 128): finance current assets with short-term financing and
long-term assets with long-term or permanent financing. Trade credit against inventory that sells
in 60 days is a match; office space, systems and equipment against long-term debt or
shareholders' capital is a match; municipalities fund bridges and stadiums with 20–30 year bonds
that roughly match the assets' productive life.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Growth speed picks the instrument**: slow/moderate → bank debt; likely to grow large quickly
  → equity.
- **Start with a small community bank or credit union** — higher approval and satisfaction than
  big banks or online lenders. Most small firms borrow small: 54% hold under $100,000.
- **Expect short-term, secured, single-purpose loans**; do not plan on a long-term unsecured
  bank loan as a young company.
- **Current ratio target depends on cash reliability**: ~1.10 can be fine for a utility- or
  taxi-like cash cycle; long production cycles need a larger cushion.
- **Creditors don't take payment in used delivery trucks.** A company rich in buildings, fleets
  and inventory can still go insolvent if quick assets can't cover bills as they fall due.
- **TIE stress test**: if EBIT were cut in half by a recession, could you still cover interest?
- **Deductibility is worthless without taxable income** — young loss-making firms get no debt
  tax shield.
- **Leverage cuts both ways**: higher debt/assets or debt/equity → higher owner returns when
  profitable, but interest is contractual and can force bankruptcy when cash flow falls.
- **Watch covenant limits**: lenders write maximum debt levels into loans and call them if
  breached.
- **Online lenders**: by 2015 ~20% of US small-employer firms had applied; most were approved
  for some credit but reported lower satisfaction than with a small bank or credit union.
- **Fast growers pay no dividends** — plow everything back (eBay returned "not even a cent").
- **When operating cash covers less than the investment outflow** (eBay 2000: slightly under
  half), the gap must come from external financing.
- **Outgrown your local bank?** Move upstream to a large money-center bank.
- **Mature-company timing**: sell bonds when interest rates are low, sell shares when prices
  are high.
- **Growth never fully stops**: a $1B company growing 3% still needs financing; few companies
  sustain high growth for more than a decade.
- **Never fund a long-term asset with a short balloon loan** (refinance risk) **and never fund a
  short-lived asset with long debt** (payments outlast the benefit).
- **Every borrowed dollar must buy assets whose incremental revenue or savings exceed the
  financing cost** over the period — the same test applies to owners' capital.

## Process / steps
Preparing a bank-loan application (built from the banker's three questions):
1. Demonstrate you understand the market and have a feasible plan for satisfying it.
2. Show the experience or knowledge (or both) needed to operate this type of business.
3. Present a business plan that is realistic, complete and based on reasonable assumptions.
4. Provide conservative revenue and cost projections — above all projected cash flow, because
   repayments come from cash flow.
5. If already operating, compute and present the current, acid-test, debt, debt-to-equity and
   times-interest-earned ratios.
6. Assemble evidence of credit history (bills paid on schedule).
7. List pledgeable collateral (current and fixed assets); consider an SBA-style guarantee.

The growth-financing ladder (eBay's sequence, pp. 123–127):
1. Self-finance from operating cash flow.
2. VC private placement.
3. IPO.
4. Follow-on public offering while the stock is high.
5. (Maturity) bank lines, term loans, commercial paper, bonds, preferred stock, sale-and-leaseback.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
Banker's question 1 — ability to repay (near-verbatim, p. 116):
- Does the applicant understand the market and have a feasible plan for satisfying it?
- Does the entrepreneur have the experience or knowledge — or both — required to operate this
  type of business?
- Is the business plan realistic, complete and based on reasonable assumptions?
- Are the plan's revenue and cost projections realistic and conservative? Is projected cash flow
  sufficient to service the loan?

Loan-readiness checklist:
- Credit history clean (personal and business bills paid on time)?
- Collateral list with resale values: cash, inventory, receivables, vehicles, buildings, equipment?
- Government guarantee (SBA or local equivalent) explored?
- Applying to a community bank / credit union before big banks or online lenders?
- Loan type matches the need: seasonal line for working capital, equipment loan for machinery,
  demand loan for short bridges?

Debt-capacity worksheet:
- Current assets ___ ÷ current liabilities ___ = current ratio ___
- Quick assets ___ ÷ current liabilities ___ = acid-test ratio ___
- Total debt ___ ÷ total assets ___ = debt ratio ___
- Total liabilities ___ ÷ owners' equity ___ = debt-to-equity ___
- EBIT ___ ÷ interest expense ___ = times-interest-earned ___; with EBIT halved: ___
- Covenant limits on any existing loan: ___
- Can the business survive a bad year at this interest load?

Asset–financing matching audit:
- For each major asset: useful life ___; funding source ___; term of that funding ___.
- Flag: long-lived asset on short money (balloon/refinance risk)? Consumable on long money?
- Will the asset's incremental revenue or savings exceed its financing cost over the period?

Maturity-phase financing menu (for a creditworthy company):
- Short-term need: commercial paper, bank line of credit, term loan (bank, insurer, pension fund).
- Lower the cost: pledge existing assets and cash flow; sale-and-leaseback.
- Public markets: bonds (when rates are low), stock (when prices are high).
- Strategic events with financing implications: mergers, acquisitions, restructuring.

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **eBay (1995–2000)** — Omidyar started on a free website with a filing cabinet, a school desk
  and a laptop; he and Jeff Skoll paid themselves $25,000 a year; early growth was self-financed
  from transaction fees; by December 2000: 22M registered users, 8,000+ categories, 6M auction and
  8M fixed-price listings a day; product development spend $4.6M (1998) → $24.8M (1999) → $55.9M
  (2000); FY2000 balance sheet: cash $202M, short-term investments $354M, long-term investments
  $218M, total assets $1,182M — growth financed first by operating cash flow, then mostly by
  selling shares.
- **Benchmark Capital** — $5M private placement for 22% of eBay; after the 1998 IPO its stake
  showed a 49,000% return — what early equity costs a founder, and why VCs chase it.
- **Pierre Omidyar** — held 44M+ shares pre-IPO; the offering and run-up made him a billionaire
  four times over; a 1999 follow-on netted eBay over $700M for expansion.
- **The $200,000 home on a 3-year loan** — 8% nonamortizing: $16,000 a year in interest, then the
  full $200,000 due in year three; refinancing depends on finding a lender and on rates not
  jumping to 10–12% — two "ifs" that end in foreclosure. Businesses die the same way.
- **Second mortgage for a dream vacation** — the vacation ends, the payments go on — borrowing
  long for a short-lived asset.
- **Asset-rich, cash-poor company** — office buildings, delivery fleets, warehouses full of stock,
  and still insolvent because quick assets can't meet bills.

## Pitfalls
- Over-leveraging so that contractual interest crushes the business in a downturn.
- Counting on the interest tax shield before the business has taxable income.
- Mistaking a big asset base for liquidity.
- Breaching loan covenants and having the loan called in.
- Financing long-term assets with short-term money, or short-lived spending with long-term debt.
- Expecting VC or an IPO when most companies fail or are acquired before either.
- Applying only to large banks (or only online) and skipping community banks.
- Paying dividends during hypergrowth instead of reinvesting.

## Key terms
`current ratio` — current assets ÷ current liabilities; short-term bill-paying ability.
`acid-test (quick) ratio` — quick assets (cash, marketable securities, receivables) ÷ current liabilities; liquidity without inventory.
`collateral` — an asset pledged to the lender until the loan is satisfied.
`leverage` — the degree to which a company is funded by liabilities and long-term debt rather than owners' capital.
`debt ratio` — total debt ÷ total assets.
`debt-to-equity ratio` — total liabilities ÷ owners' equity.
`times-interest-earned ratio` — EBIT ÷ interest expense; how many times pretax earnings cover interest.
`EBIT` — earnings before interest and taxes.
`private placement` — sale of shares to a private investor (e.g. a VC) rather than the public.
`IPO` — initial public offering; the transition from private to public company, managed by investment banks.
`investment bank / syndicate` — the firm(s) and broker-dealers that run a share issue and reach investors.
`money-center bank` — a large bank a company graduates to after outgrowing its local lender.
`commercial paper` — short-term (2–270 day) debt security sold at a discount by highly rated corporations.
`bond` — fixed-rate debt security with a stated maturity (1–30 years), interest usually semiannual.
`preferred stock` — equity paying a stated annual dividend; prices move like bonds.
`sale-and-leaseback` — selling an owned asset and leasing it back to release cash.
`peer-to-peer lending` — loans funded by individuals through networks such as Lending Club and Prosper.
`cash-flow statement` — totals cash entering and leaving through operations, investing and financing (see appendix A).

## Summing up (book)
- Growth-phase entrepreneurs draw on internally generated cash flow, asset-based loans and
  external equity.
- Bankers look at ability to repay, character and collateral before lending.
- The current, acid-test and times-interest-earned ratios give lenders insight into repayment ability.
- Debt is generally the lowest-cost capital because interest is tax-deductible, but it makes the
  enterprise riskier.
- An IPO is a major milestone for the few firms that reach it and a major cash infusion for growth.
- Maturity-phase financing for creditworthy companies includes bank loans, commercial paper,
  bonds and stock.
- Finance short-term assets with short-term money, long-term assets with long-term debt or
  shareholders' contributions.
