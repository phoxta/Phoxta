# Appendix A — Understanding Financial Statements
Source: HBR's Entrepreneur's Handbook, appendix A, book pages 225–240.

## Purpose
A primer on the three essential financial statements — balance sheet, income statement and
cash-flow statement — plus working capital, financial leverage and the debt/equity structure of
a firm. Load when a user asks "what do these statements mean", needs projected financials for a
plan or pitch, confuses profit with cash, or wants a ratio explained or computed. Skip for
users with a business degree or senior finance experience (the book says so itself).

## Core ideas
- Statements answer four questions: what the company owns and owes; where revenue comes from
  and how money was spent; how much profit it made; how financially healthy it is.
- Three statements, three lenses: **balance sheet** = position at one moment; **income
  statement** = results over a period; **cash-flow statement** = why cash changed over a period.
- Readers: managers (performance, where to intervene), shareholders (capital stewardship),
  investors (opportunities), lenders and suppliers (creditworthiness). Formats are standard
  enough to compare one business with another.
- Public companies must publish them (SEC full-disclosure rule); private ones need not, but
  owners and bankers expect them. *Non-US note:* the filing body differs (Companies House,
  CAC, etc.) but the same three statements are the norm everywhere.
- Every transaction keeps **Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity** in balance.
- Balance-sheet values are **historic cost**, not market value — the lesser of two evils versus
  annual appraisals nobody would trust. Only cash, receivables and payables sit near cash value.
- **Profit is not cash.** A November sale booked as revenue but paid next period raises profit
  and lowers cash flow. The cash-flow statement translates net income into a cash basis.
- Insight comes from **multi-period comparison** (trends, turnarounds), not one statement.
- **Leverage** magnifies returns on owners' capital in both directions; the CEO's job is a
  realistic debt/equity balance, not maximum debt.
- A startup's statements will be simple; learn the rest with your accountant as you grow
  (further reading: *HBR Guide to Finance Basics for Managers*).

## Frameworks & tables

### The three statements at a glance
| Statement | Question answered | Time frame | Governing equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance sheet | What do we own, what do we owe, what is left for owners? | Snapshot at a date (month/quarter/fiscal-year end) | Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity |
| Income statement (P&L) | Did we make a profit, and what did it cost to make it? | Cumulative over a defined period | Revenues − Expenses = Net Income (or Net Loss) |
| Cash-flow statement | Why did cash change, and can we pay bills as they fall due? | Over a period: opening cash → closing cash | Cash Flow from Profit + Other Sources of Cash − Uses of Cash = Change in Cash |

**Balance-sheet anatomy (order and meaning of the blocks)**
| Block | Contents | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | Cash & marketable securities, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses | Convertible to cash within one year; listed first, most liquid first |
| Fixed (plant) assets | Gross PP&E − accumulated depreciation = net book value | Hard to turn into cash; everything except land depreciates |
| Goodwill / intangibles | Price paid above fair value of an acquired company's assets; brands, reputation, patents | Goodwill is "strictly an accounting fiction"; brands and patents may have real value |
| Total assets | Current + fixed (+ intangibles) | |
| Current liabilities | Accounts payable, accrued salaries and expenses, accrued income taxes, short-term IOUs, **this year's instalment of long-term loans** | Claims due within a year |
| Long-term liabilities | Bonds, mortgages | Contractual obligation for interest and principal |
| Owners' equity | Contributed capital (cash received for shares) + retained earnings (accumulated net profit after dividends) | Also shareholders'/stockholders' equity; = total assets − total liabilities |

**Cash-flow anatomy — three activities and the sign rules** (uses = negative, sources = positive)
| Activity | Adds cash (+) | Uses cash (−) |
|---|---|---|
| Operations (starts from net income) | Depreciation add-back (no cash left the company); increases in payables, accrued expenses, tax payable (received or used, not yet paid) | Increases in receivables and finished-goods inventory (produced, not yet paid for); increases in prepaid expenses (paid, not yet consumed) |
| Investing | Sale of plant, property, equipment or financial instruments | Capital expenditures; purchases of financial instruments |
| Financing | Short- or long-term borrowing; issuing capital stock | Debt repayment; cash dividends |

| Link | Figure | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 347,500 | Bottom of income statement → top of cash-flow statement → added to retained earnings |
| Retained-earnings roll-forward | 935,000 + 347,500 − 0 dividends = 1,282,500 | Prior balance sheet + net income − dividends = current balance sheet |
| Depreciation | 42,500 | Expense on P&L = increase in accumulated depreciation on balance sheet = non-cash add-back on cash-flow statement |
| Working-capital changes | AR +43,000, inventory +80,000, prepaid +25,000, AP +20,000, accrued +21,000, tax payable +8,000 | "Increase (Decrease)" column of balance sheet = operating adjustments on cash-flow statement (sign flipped for assets) |
| PP&E | Gross +200,000 = 467,000 capex − 267,000 disposal | Balance-sheet change = investing section |
| Debt and equity | Short-term debt −65,000, long-term debt +90,000, contributed capital +50,000 | Balance-sheet changes = financing section |
| Cash | 486,500 + 166,000 = 652,500 | "Increase in cash" on cash-flow statement = change in the balance-sheet cash line |
Rule: a projected set of statements is only correct when every one of these ties.

### Financial leverage — the book's worked example
Buy a $400,000 asset with $100,000 of your own money + $300,000 borrowed (ignore loan payments,
taxes and interim cash flow). Four years pass.
| Scenario | Asset value | Repay loan | Owner's cash | Gain on owner's $100,000 | Gain on asset |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Leveraged, asset up 25% | $500,000 | 300,000 | $200,000 | **+100%** | +25% |
| Unleveraged ($400,000 own cash), asset up 25% | $500,000 | 0 | $500,000 | +25% | +25% |
| Leveraged, asset down 25% | $300,000 | 300,000 | $0 | **−100%** (whole stake lost) | −25% |
Generalised: return on equity = asset return × (asset value ÷ equity invested) — here 4×.
Interest on loans is tax-deductible in the US and most countries, making leverage more attractive.

### Financial structure of the firm
- Every dollar of debt raises risk twice: downside amplification, and fixed interest payments
  due in good times and bad. Many companies failed when a downturn cut their ability to pay.
- Creditors and investors price the **debt-to-equity ratio**: a highly leveraged firm may pay
  **14%** on borrowed funds vs **10–12%** for a less leveraged competitor, and equity investors
  demand a higher return too — no high risk without commensurately large returns.

## Decision rules & rules of thumb
- **Always compare with prior periods.** The "Increase (Decrease)" column and a 3–5 year P&L
  are where the story is; one statement says little.
- **Rising owners' equity** = moving in a positive direction. **Flat operating expenses while
  sales grow** = management holding the line on cost. **Falling interest expense** = a loan paid off.
- **Working capital: neither too little nor too much.** Too little → can't pay bills or seize
  opportunities. Too much → carrying cost (financed by interest-bearing loans) eats profit.
  The right level depends on industry and plans.
- **Inventory: buffer vs. burden.** Stock fills orders instantly and cushions stoppages, but
  every excess widget adds financing cost and obsolescence risk — PC finished goods lose
  about **2% of value per day**.
- **Read balance-sheet values as historic cost** except cash, receivables and payables; land
  bought in downtown San Francisco in 1992 is carried at what was paid. **Discount goodwill.**
- **Cash tight → budget conservatively; cash flush → look for new investments.**
- **Solvency = paying bills as they come due**; what matters is whether receivables are turning
  into cash. Small-company owners feel cash flow "almost every day".
- **Depreciation is an expense but not a cash outflow** — add it back when converting profit to cash.
- **Leverage at 4× asset/equity turns a 25% move into ±100% on your capital.** Use it when
  returns are reliable and interest is deductible; never maximise debt.
- **Expect to pay for risk:** high debt-to-equity → ~14% borrowing cost vs 10–12%, plus a higher
  return demanded by equity investors.

## Process / steps
**Read a balance sheet**
1. Confirm Total assets = Total liabilities + Owners' equity.
2. Walk current assets (most liquid first) → fixed assets net of depreciation → intangibles;
   then current liabilities (incl. this year's loan instalment) → long-term debt → contributed
   capital and retained earnings.
3. Compute net working capital and debt-to-equity; compare both with the prior year.

**Build an income statement (revenue waterfall)**
1. Revenue by stream (retail, corporate…) → total sales revenue.
2. − COGS → gross profit.
3. − operating expenses − depreciation → EBIT.
4. − interest → earnings before taxes.
5. − income taxes → net income.

**Derive the cash-flow statement from net income (indirect method)**
1. Start with net income; add back depreciation.
2. Operating assets (receivables, inventory, prepaid): subtract each increase, add each decrease.
3. Operating liabilities (payables, accrued, tax payable): add each increase, subtract each decrease.
4. Sum → cash flow from operations.
5. Investing: + asset sales − capital expenditures. Financing: + borrowings + stock issued − repayments − dividends.
6. Sum the three sections → change in cash; check it equals the balance-sheet cash delta.

**Compute a leveraged return** — equity E, borrowed B, asset A = E + B, exit value V:
owner's proceeds = V − B; return on equity = (V − B − E) ÷ E vs asset return (V − A) ÷ A.

## Worksheets, checklists & questions
**Minimum line items for a startup's statements**
- Balance sheet: cash; receivables; inventory; prepaid expenses; gross fixed assets; accumulated
  depreciation; payables; accrued expenses; tax payable; short-term debt; long-term debt;
  contributed capital; retained earnings.
- Income statement: revenue by stream; COGS; operating expenses; depreciation; interest; taxes.
- Cash-flow: opening cash; the change in each balance-sheet line; capex; asset sales;
  borrowings/repayments; stock issued; dividends; closing cash.

**Statement health check (each period)**
- Does the balance sheet balance, and does closing cash tie to the cash-flow statement?
- Did owners' equity rise or fall, and why (profit, dividends, new capital)?
- Is net working capital enough to pay bills and fund growth — without idle, financed surplus?
- Is inventory growing faster than sales? What does holding it cost, and is it ageing?
- Are receivables turning into cash, or is profit piling up as unpaid invoices?
- Which revenue streams grow, which stagnate? Are operating expenses held flat?
- What is the debt-to-equity ratio, what rate is it costing, and would equity survive a 25% fall?
- Which balance-sheet values are historic cost and materially off market value?

## Examples & cautionary tales
- **Ken Olsen, Digital Equipment Corporation (late 1950s)** — engineer with no finance; copied
  balance-sheet and P&L layouts from Samuelson's economics textbook for his VC plan and got
  funded — the layouts are learnable; don't let them block a pitch.
- **Smartphone-case maker** — $1M of plastic on 30-day terms: inventory +$1M, payables +$1M;
  borrows $100,000: cash and liabilities both +$100,000 — every transaction stays balanced.
- **Fire example** — $3M assets − $2M liabilities = $1M equity; $500,000 of uninsured assets
  burn → equity $500,000, liabilities unchanged — owners absorb losses first.
- **XYZ's San Francisco land** — carried at the $700,000 paid in 1992 — historic cost hides value.
- **Personal-computer industry** — finished goods lose ~2% of value per day — inventory melts.
- **Amalgamated Hat Rack** — retail growth, flat opex, falling interest, equity +$397,500, cash
  +$166,000 — a healthy small manufacturer to benchmark against.
- **Highly leveraged borrower** — pays 14% vs 10–12% — the market charges for balance-sheet risk.

## Pitfalls
- Treating profit as cash (and being surprised by insolvency while "profitable").
- Reading historic-cost balance-sheet values as market values.
- Counting goodwill as a real asset.
- Letting working capital or inventory drift too high (financing cost, obsolescence) or too low
  (missed bills, missed opportunities).
- Maximising debt because interest is deductible; forgetting interest is due in bad years too.
- Judging from one period instead of a multi-period view.
- Ignoring the cash-flow statement — "the least used and understood" of the three.

## Key terms
(Statement line items are defined in the anatomy tables above.)
`accounts receivable` / `accounts payable` — money owed to the company by customers / owed by it to suppliers.
`current assets / current liabilities` — convertible to cash / due within one year.
`net working capital` — current assets − current liabilities; money tied up in short-term operations.
`accumulated depreciation` — cumulative reduction in the stated value of fixed assets (all but land).
`goodwill` — purchase price paid above fair value of an acquired company's assets.
`contributed capital` / `retained earnings` — capital received for shares / net profits accumulated after dividends.
`historic cost` — recording items at what was paid, not market value.
`depreciation` — non-cash expense estimating an asset's consumption over its useful life.
`EBIT` — earnings before interest and taxes; operating earnings.
`profit margin` — net income ÷ revenue.
`capital expenditures` — cash invested in plant, property and equipment.
`solvency` — the ability to pay bills as they come due.
`financial leverage` — using borrowed money to acquire an asset; magnifies return on owners' capital.
`financial structure` — the balance of debt and equity on the balance sheet.
`debt-to-equity ratio` — the leverage measure creditors and investors price into rates and required returns.

## Summing up (book)
- The three statements are the essential documents of business; managers, owners, investors
  and lenders all read them. Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity; Revenues − Expenses = Net
  Income; cash from profit + other sources − uses = change in cash.
- Watch working capital, understand leverage, and seek a realistic debt/equity balance.
- There is far more to interpretation than this primer, but a startup's statements will be
  simple — learn the rest with your accountant or financial officer as the company grows.
