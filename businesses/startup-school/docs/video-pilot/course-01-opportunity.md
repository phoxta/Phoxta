# Course 01 video pilot — From Idea to Opportunity

This is the production package for the first eight-topic pilot. It establishes
the visual and editorial system that the other 98 topic videos will reuse.
It is intentionally a practical founder story, not eight disconnected lectures.

## Deliverables

- 8 episodes, 6–8 minutes each, plus the existing course assessment.
- 16:9 master, 1080p, with a 9:16-safe central composition for social clips.
- Human mentor narration or an approved, consistent narrator; never a different
  synthetic voice per episode.
- Captions, on-screen diagrams, and a 45–90 second in-app application pause.
- Recurring case: **Amara Okafor**, founder of **CornerCart**, a service that
  helps people order everyday household essentials from trusted nearby shops for
  pickup or delivery. It is fictional; no business or customer endorsement is implied.

## Visual bible

![Amara working through the CornerCart opportunity map](assets/cornercart-founder-keyframe.png)

`assets/cornercart-founder-keyframe.png` is the approved pilot keyframe. It was
generated with the built-in image-generation workflow as an illustration-story
reference: warm 2D editorial animation, violet / peach / mint / cream palette,
no branding or readable interface text. Keep Amara's purple shirt, patterned
headband, warm coworking setting, and the CornerCart shop / household / pickup
motif consistent. The generated keyframe is a reference, not a claim that any
person or business endorses Phoxta.

### Motion rules

1. Animate decisions, not decoration. A diagram assembles only as the narrator
   introduces each relationship.
2. Use 2D cut-out motion, camera pans, card reveals, and simple data marks.
   Do not use uncanny AI morphing, fake interviews, or photorealistic customer
   testimonials.
3. Keep live-action-style mentor footage optional: a 10–20 second opening or
   closing is enough. The main teaching should remain legible without it.
4. Show one visual idea at once. Do not narrate paragraphs over a dense canvas.
5. Reserve lower right for subtitles; never place critical labels there.

### Reusable scene kit

| Asset | Use |

| Amara desk keyframe | Episode cold opens and transitions |
| Shopper, shop owner, courier, and household silhouettes | User, buyer, influencer, and stakeholder maps |

## Episode template

| Time | Purpose | Visual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:25 | Founder tension | Amara encounters a decision, not a definition. |
| 0:25–0:45 | Learner promise | One sentence: “By the end, you will decide…” |
| 0:45–2:30 | Framework | One animated diagram, built in sequence. |
| 2:30–4:15 | CornerCart case | Show a concrete choice, counterexample, and evidence. |
| 4:15–5:30 | Worked application | Fill a real template in front of the learner. |
| 5:30–6:45 | Founder action | Pause card, then save to Venture or create a task. |

The narrator should speak at about 130 words/minute. Silence during the action
pause is purposeful; do not fill it with music or more explanation.

## Scripts and shot boards

### 1. What Makes a Business Opportunity?

**Founder decision:** Is CornerCart a real opportunity, or simply an app idea?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Amara circles “shopping app” on a sketch, then watches a shopper call two nearby stores for basic items. | “An idea is something you could build. An opportunity is a problem people already need solved.” |
| Framework | Four cards animate: customer, problem, timing, value exchange. | “An opportunity holds only when all four cards connect.” |
| Case | Show a shopper juggling calls, stock uncertainty, and a delayed pickup. | “Amara's first idea was an app. Her evidence pointed to people spending time checking availability and coordinating a simple household shop.” |
| Counterexample | A polished app icon falls away from an empty evidence board. | “A clever feature is not proof. A defined problem with a reachable customer is the beginning.” |
| Apply | Four-card board opens blank. | “Write one sentence: a specific customer struggles with a specific problem because…” |

**Editor notes:** use a visual line that physically joins the four cards. Do not
show market-size figures in this first episode.

### 2. Ideas vs Problems

**Founder decision:** Can Amara describe the struggle without pitching her product?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Split screen: “We need an app” versus a shopper and shop owner using calls, messages, and handwritten lists. | “The left side is a solution. The right side is a problem happening today.” |
| Framework | The product statement slides behind a curtain; a job, obstacle, and cost remain. | “Start with what a customer is trying to do, where the work breaks, and what that break costs.” |
| Case | A shopping-list token becomes repeated calls, a substitute item, and a missed pickup. | “The evidence is not that people like apps. It is that shoppers and shops spend time repairing a broken handoff.” |
| Apply | Three empty timeline prompts. | “List three moments your customer loses time, money, certainty, or status. Do not mention your product.” |

**Editor notes:** use actual-looking but unreadable calendar blocks; all labels
are rendered as motion-graphics text in post-production, never generated in an image.

### 3. Identifying Real Customer Problems

**Founder decision:** What counts as evidence that the problem is active?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | A sticky note saying “customers say it is useful” is weighed against a stack of workaround cards. | “Interest is easy to give. Past effort is harder to fake.” |
| Framework | Evidence ladder rises: opinions, complaints, workaround, spend, commitment. | “Look for traces: repeated complaints, delays, risk, budget, and what people already do to cope.” |
| Case | A shop owner manually answers the same stock question three times. | “The strongest early signal was not enthusiasm for CornerCart. It was people already paying with time and uncertainty.” |
| Apply | Evidence log with five rows. | “Collect five verbatim examples of the current workaround and what it costs.” |

**Editor notes:** make the evidence ladder a reusable 8-second motion asset for
Episodes 3, 6, and 8.

### 4. Understanding Pain Points

**Founder decision:** What is the consequence of the problem for each person involved?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | One unavailable item ripples to shopper, shop owner, courier, and household plans. | “A pain point is not a mild annoyance. It has a consequence, an owner, and a cost.” |
| Framework | Three rings: functional, financial, emotional. | “Name what the person cannot do, what it costs, and what it feels like when the risk lands on them.” |
| Case | CornerCart map labels shopper as user, shop owner as partner, and household as affected stakeholder. | “The shop owner sees a lost sale. The shopper sees wasted time. Both matter, but they need different proof.” |
| Apply | Sentence build: When ___ tries to ___, they struggle because ___. | “Finish the sentence, then name the consequence rather than your feature.” |

### 5. Opportunity Mapping

**Founder decision:** Who influences, pays for, uses, or blocks the first solution?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Amara talks to a shopper, then learns the shop owner controls the inventory and fulfilment. | “The person who feels the problem is not always the person who can make the service work.” |
| Framework | Ecosystem map expands from user to buyer, influencer, blocker, alternative, trigger. | “Map the roles before you plan the interview. Otherwise, one friendly conversation can mislead you.” |
| Case | CornerCart nodes reveal shopper, shop owner, courier, current WhatsApp workaround, and stock reliability. | “Amara now knows whom to interview and which relationship could stop adoption.” |
| Apply | Map canvas with seven prompts. | “Mark who experiences the problem, approves, pays, and could block adoption.” |

### 6. Market Need

**Founder decision:** Is the need frequent and urgent enough to justify the next test?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Four dials—frequency, cost, urgency, willingness to change—start at unknown. | “Need is not how enthusiastically someone agrees with you.” |
| Framework | Dials animate one at a time, with evidence tags attached. | “A useful score explains its evidence. A blank space or a guess is an invitation to test, not to pretend.” |
| Case | CornerCart scores high on repeated shopping friction but unknown on willingness to pay for convenience. | “The useful result is a weak score that tells Amara what to ask next.” |
| Apply | Four-dial scorecard. | “Score each dimension one to five. Write evidence beside it, or mark it a guess.” |

### 7. Problem-Solution Fit

**Founder decision:** What is the smallest test of CornerCart's promised outcome?

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | A full product roadmap shrinks into one concierge pilot. | “Problem-solution fit is a hypothesis, not a graduation ceremony.” |
| Framework | Hypothesis sentence assembles: customer + approach + outcome + reason. | “Say what you believe will happen, for whom, and why. Then choose the least expensive way to prove yourself wrong.” |
| Case | Amara offers ten households a manual ordering and pickup service with three nearby shops before building software. | “The pilot tests the outcome—reliable access to essentials—not their opinion of a future interface.” |
| Apply | Hypothesis and smallest-test template. | “Write one testable statement, then schedule the smallest test you can run this week.” |

### 8. Opportunity Assessment

**Founder decision:** Pursue, reshape, or pause based on the evidence gathered.

| Beat | Picture | Narration / on-screen direction |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Amara's board contains both green evidence cards and unresolved purple cards. | “A decision does not require certainty. It requires honesty about what is known.” |
| Framework | Six-part scorecard: pain, access, founder fit, feasibility, economics, defensibility. | “Weak scores are not failures. Hidden weak scores are.” |
| Case | Amara chooses “pursue with a validation test,” not “launch the product.” | “The next move is to test repeat ordering with a defined neighbourhood segment.” |
| Apply | Opportunity Brief gets a final review stamp: Guess, Evidence, Proven. | “Save the claims you can defend, name the riskiest one, and turn it into your next task.” |

**End card:** “Build your Opportunity Brief in Phoxta. Save your decision, then
take the course assessment.”

## Audio, captions, and accessibility

- Record narration clean and close-miked at 48 kHz. Leave 500 ms before and
  after each animated label so caption timing does not feel rushed.
- Deliver WebVTT captions after the final narration edit. Captions must describe
  meaningful non-verbal events, for example: `[calendar blocks reshuffle]`.
- Do not communicate a key distinction through colour alone: every evidence
  state also has a word label and shape.
- Keep background music below narration, remove it during application pauses,
  and do not use a timer sound unless the learner can mute it.

## Editor hand-off checklist

1. Confirm product, financial, and jurisdiction-specific claims with a subject
   expert before picture lock.
2. Build the reusable evidence meter, opportunity map, and pause card first.
3. Produce Episodes 1 and 3 as an animatic; test them with five founders before
   animating all eight episodes.
4. Add the final voice recording, then motion, captions, and a 10-second
   thumbnail-safe cold open.
5. Export a master, a caption file, a thumbnail, and 30-second social cutdowns.
6. Upload the finished video URL and caption URL to the corresponding lessons;
   the course UI already supports both.
